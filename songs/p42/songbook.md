# At a glance

**What it's about.** There's an old phone in a kitchen drawer with one saved message on it: twelve seconds of her husband's voice. You can hear his blinker, his ring tapping the wheel, his laugh before the hello. "Hey, it's me. Nothing important." In October the phone's battery swells and the screen lifts off the case. The kid at the repair counter can't save it. She sits up with it one last night, and then it won't turn back on. For three days she keeps the drawer shut. On the third morning, at the sink, she hears him: the same laugh, the same words. Only now the message ends differently. Not "Call me back," but "I'll call you back."

The song never says what happened to him or what it means. The chorus is his message, plain as a real voicemail, and each time it comes back the same words weigh more. The last line moves the promise to his side.

**How it goes.** A piano ballad, straight eighths at ♩ = 76, in B♭. The piano carries the whole song: a single pianist with the singer can perform it complete. A cello joins at verse 2. The band (bass, guitar, drums) comes in only for the last chorus and drops out for the final line, so "I'll call you back" falls quietly over the piano alone.

**The singer.** A warm, plain alto, F3 to A4, close to speech. The verses sit higher (around F4) and tell the story. The chorus, his message, sits lower and quieter (around D4), the way you'd repeat someone's words to yourself.

# Section by section

Pitches are where the voice sounds. Bar numbers count from the first bar of the intro.

## Intro (bars 1–4)

Piano alone. The right hand plays a short figure on the "and" of 1, beat 2, the "and" of 2 and the "and" of 3: B♭4, D5, G5, F5 over B♭. It moves with the chords (B♭ Dm E♭ F) over left-hand half notes.

## Verse 1 (bars 5–12)

| Chords | Line |
|---|---|
| B♭ Dm | There's an old phone in the kitchen drawer, |
| E♭ B♭ | one saved message from March ninth; |
| Gm E♭ | you can hear his blinker, his ring tapping the wheel, |
| Cm F | and he laughs like always before hello: |

The first line starts as a pickup on A4 (bar 4, beat 4) and steps down to "old" on F4, held two beats, then to "drawer" on B♭3 (bar 6). "One saved message from March ninth" climbs back up to G4. "You can hear his blinker" rocks between G4 and F4, and "his ring tapping the wheel" turns around E♭4–D4 to F4. "Laughs like always" reaches A4 and "hello" ends on A4 over F, open, leaning into his message.

## Chorus 1 (bars 13–20)

| Chords | Line |
|---|---|
| E♭ F | Hey, it's me, nothing important, |
| Gm E♭ | I'm at the light on Seventh Street and thought of you; |
| Cm F | hey, it's me, just wanted to hear you. |
| B♭ B♭ | Call me back. |

The chorus is his voicemail, and it's sung the way people talk on one: quick on the small words, slow on the ones that matter. "Hey, it's me, noth-ing" goes by fast, B♭3 C4 D4 up to G4, and "im-POR-tant" settles on E♭4, held two beats and more. "I'm at the light on Seventh Street" sits around B♭3–D4. "Hey, it's me" comes again on the same three notes. "Call me back" is three long notes, D4, G4, F4, the last held into the B♭ chord.

## Verse 2 (bars 21–28)

| Chords | Line |
|---|---|
| B♭ Dm | In October the battery swelled |
| E♭ B♭ | and the screen came up off the case; |
| Gm E♭ | the kid at the counter said, "Nothing to save," |
| Cm F | I said, "There's one thing on it," and he looked away. |

The same tune as verse 1, with the rhythm fitted to the new words. "Swelled" sinks to B♭3 and is held (bar 22). "The kid at the counter" rocks on G4–F4 as "you can hear his blinker" did. "There's one thing on it" lifts to A4 on "one," and "away" ends on A4 over F (bar 28).

## Chorus 2 (bars 29–36)

Exactly as chorus 1. The cello holds beneath it.

## Bridge (bars 37–44)

| Chords | Line |
|---|---|
| Gm Dm | So I sat up with it one last night, |
| E♭ B♭ | played it till the light went red, |
| Gm Dm | the blinker and the laugh on Seventh Street, |
| Cm F | and then it wouldn't turn back on. |

The bridge drops: it sits lowest of all, centred on C4, and touches F3 on "went" (bar 39). "One last night" falls to G3. "The blinker and the laugh on Seventh Street" moves in the low register, G3 to C4. "And then it wouldn't turn back on" starts on F4, comes down through D4, and ends on G3 over F (bar 44). Then nothing.

## Verse 3 (bars 45–52)

| Chords | Line |
|---|---|
| B♭ Dm | Three days I kept the drawer shut, |
| E♭ B♭ | didn't play the radio; |
| Gm E♭ | then the third morning at the sink I heard him, |
| Cm F | the laugh before he said hello: |

The verse tune comes back, but the piano holds one chord a bar for the first four bars and the cello drops out: the three days. "Drawer" dips to A3 (bar 46). At "then the third morning" the broken chords return and the cello comes back in. "Heard him" lands on F4 and E♭4 (bar 50). "Hello" ends on A4 over F (bar 52), exactly where verse 1 ended.

## Last chorus (bars 53–64)

| Chords | Line |
|---|---|
| E♭ F | Hey, it's me, nothing important, |
| Gm E♭ | I'm at the light on Seventh Street and thought of you; |
| Cm F | hey, it's me, just wanted to hear you. |
| Gm E♭ | Hey, it's me. |
| Cm F | Nothing important. |
| B♭ B♭ | I'll call you back. |

The band comes in. The first three lines are the chorus as before. Then "Hey, it's me" alone, the same three notes, held on D4. "Nothing important" is the chorus's own tune slowed to half speed: G4, F4, D4, and E♭4 held three beats and more over the Cm and into the F (bars 61–62). It's the longest the song holds anything. Then the band stops, and over the piano alone "I'll call you back" falls quietly, C4, D4, E♭4, and home to B♭3 on the "and" of beat 2 in bar 64, held to the end of the bar.

## Outro (bars 65–68)

Piano alone. The intro figure for three bars, then one B♭ chord on beat 1 of bar 68, let ring.

# For each player

**Voice.** Alto, F3 to A4. Tell the verses; they carry the story. Sing the chorus like his words remembered: light and quick on "hey, it's me, nothing," with the weight on "important." In the last chorus, hold "Nothing important" as long as it's written, then take "I'll call you back" down to almost nothing. Don't push it. It's a promise, said quietly.

**Piano.** The piano part is the whole song; with the singer, it needs nothing else.

- *Intro and outro:* the right-hand figure over left-hand half notes.
- *Verses 1–2:* broken chords in straight eighths, right hand from B♭3 under the voice; left hand root and fifth in half notes.
- *Choruses:* the broken chords a step higher; left hand in quarters (root, fifth, octave, fifth).
- *Bridge:* half-note chords over whole notes.
- *Verse 3:* bars 45–48 one chord a bar, held (the three days); from bar 49 the broken chords return.
- *Last chorus:* block chords on the beat over left-hand eighths. Bars 63–64, one chord a bar, alone and soft.
- *Outro:* the figure; bar 68, one B♭ chord, let ring.

**Cello.** It comes in at verse 2 with long held notes (B♭2 to G3) through verse 2 and chorus 2. In the bridge and the last chorus it plays a quiet countermelody, C3 to C4, always under the voice: it moves when the voice holds or rests, and holds a chord tone when the voice moves. It's out for the first four bars of verse 3, then holds again. It plays one note with the final chord.

**Bass.** The last chorus only, bars 53–62: whole notes for six bars, then root and fifth in half notes. Out from bar 63.

**Guitar (clean).** The last chorus only, bars 53–62: quiet arpeggios in eighths. Out from bar 63.

**Drums.** The last chorus only. Brushes for bars 53–58 (kick on 1 and 3, a tap on 2 and 4, the swirl on each beat); sticks for bars 59–62 (crash at bar 59; kick 1, 3 and the "and" of 3; snare 2 and 4; ride eighths); a small fill in bar 62. Out from bar 63.

# Arrangement

The song grows by subtraction as much as addition. It starts with the piano alone; the cello joins when the phone starts to fail; the bridge thins to whole notes; verse 3 nearly stops. The band arrives only when "Hey, it's me" comes back after the silence, and it leaves before the last line, so the promise is said over the same piano that started the song.

## Producer one-sheet

**The emotional target.** A widow's kitchen at night: plain, close, unsentimental. The listener should feel the loss in the ordinary details and the hope in one changed line, without anyone telling them either.

**The three references.**

- **Adele, "Someone Like You"** (2011): the piano-and-voice ballad, one pianist and one singer; take the broken-chord accompaniment that carries the whole song alone.
- **Joni Mitchell, "River"** (1971): the arrangement on the record is her piano and nothing else; take the close, confiding voice and the space.
- **Jason Isbell, "If We Were Vampires"** (2017): a love song about the plain fact that one of two will be left; take the restraint and the ordinary words.

**The dynamic arc.**

| Section | Bars | Level | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intro, Verse 1 | 1–12 | p | piano alone, then the voice |
| Chorus 1 | 13–20 | mp | piano a step higher, left hand in quarters |
| Verse 2, Chorus 2 | 21–36 | mp → mf | cello in, held |
| Bridge | 37–44 | p | half and whole notes; cello countermelody |
| Verse 3 | 45–52 | pp → p | bars 45–48 almost nothing (the anchor of silence); 49 on, the verse returns |
| Last chorus | 53–62 | f | the band, brushes to sticks at 59 |
| Last line | 63–64 | pp | the band out: piano and voice alone |
| Outro | 65–68 | p | the piano figure; one chord, let ring |

**The non-negotiables.** The chorus sung as speech, not a big tune. Bars 45–48 nearly empty. "Nothing important" held long in the last chorus. The band out for "I'll call you back." Bar 68 one chord, left to ring.

**The mix priorities.** The voice on top and close: every word of the message heard. The piano is the bed; the cello stays under the vocal. In the last chorus the band never covers the lyric. The last line is voice and piano only.

## Production brief

*The song.* A woman keeps her husband's last voicemail on a dying phone. It's a song about loss that is really about recognition and return, built so a listener feels the hope without hearing it named.

*References* (checked by search):

- **Adele, "Someone Like You."** It's a slow, plaintive ballad that pairs her voice with a looping piano line, and it was the first piano-and-vocal-only ballad to reach number one on the Billboard Hot 100. Dan Wilson, its co-writer, played the piano.
- **Joni Mitchell, "River"** (*Blue*, 1971). The arrangement on *Blue* is her piano and nothing else, and the accompaniment borrows from "Jingle Bells."
- **Jason Isbell and the 400 Unit, "If We Were Vampires"** (*The Nashville Sound*, 2017). A love song about the fact that one of a lifelong couple will have to die first; it won the Grammy for Best American Roots Song.

*Non-negotiables.* As on the one-sheet.

## Session notes

- **Tempo and click.** ♩ = 76, 4/4, straight eighths. A click is optional; the piano can lead.
- **Key.** B♭ major. No borrowed chords.
- **Chords per bar.** As in the form table and the chart: one chord a bar.
- **Range.** Voice F3–A4. Piano B♭1–C6. Cello B♭2–C4. Bass C2–D3. Guitar G3–C5.
- **Tracking.** Piano and voice together, live, if possible: the song is theirs. Then the cello. The band last, for bars 53–62 only.
- **Blend and panning.** Voice up the middle and close. Piano slightly left, cello right. In the last chorus, bass in the middle, guitar right, drums spread and low in the mix.
