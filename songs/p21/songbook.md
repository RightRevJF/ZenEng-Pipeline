# Make Do

*Words and music: Jason Bryant*

## The song

A dream-pop song in 4/4 at ♩ = 72, in G major, coloured by Gmaj7, Cmaj7, Em7 and Am7. It's built from:

- a slow drum machine;
- a bass that barely moves;
- clean guitars ringing in eighth-note arpeggios;
- a pad under everything.

It runs 68 bars, 3:47.

A baritone sings it low and close: B2–A3 in the verses, B3 on each chorus "DO," and D4 once, on the last.

Every line has two bars; the chords of both are shown beside it. The intro, the break and the outro are instrumental lines for clean guitar, written out as a cue on the lead sheet. Production notes are in italics.

It's the writer's poem "Cattywumpus," as it happens: in a car with the windows blacked out, in the minutes after.

## How the song works

The writer gave the poem "Cattywumpus" and asked what I thought of it. The critique he heard:

- **What works:** its restraint; the title word; "against the evidence"; the black eyelid; the wry last line.
- **Where it's softer:** "blade of light" is stock and competes with the eyelid; the warmth is said twice; "You beside me" is the flattest line.

He kept the poem's images and order (Q1 A). He didn't want "cattywumpus" as the chorus unless it could be made to work. The run said honestly it wasn't sure, and he chose the poem's last line, "the afternoon will have to make do," as the hook (Q2 B). The title follows the chorus.

The song stays in the car, in the present tense:

- **Verse 1:** the blacked-out windows, the leather still holding their shape, the air conditioner humming "against the evidence," a tremor, the smell of skin and cooling dust.
- **The chorus:** a blade of daylight finds its way in. The warmth is slow to leave, which is the writer's change: fulfilment, not depletion. The day can go on without them, and the afternoon will have to make do.
- **Verse 2:** the clothes in the footwell, the shade as a black eyelid that won't quite close, wrestled into place with all four hands, and one corner gone cattywumpus in the dark.
- **The break:** where the light comes in.
- **Verse 3:** new, at the writer's request: "an abstract way to capture the feeling… not quite turned to jelly, not quite what you were before." It goes "halfway back into my own shape" (answering verse 1's leather) and "I button myself back together, one hole wrong" (crooked, like the corner of the shade). It ends with neither of them reaching for the door.

## What the test showed about v64

**Worked:**

- **27.21, the instrumental line:** the first real run. Three lines were written and floored, notated as a cue voice, and played on their own track. It answers song 20's hand-composed drive.
- **The floor spans (fix 6):** verses arranged the same now read the same, 0.26–0.28.
- **By construction (fix 7):** the four certificates are printed beside the 77.9% rate.
- **10.20's middle-slot rule:** no line was in both top quarters, so it went to the most-repeated hook line.
- **The never-say lists, word by word:** nothing written. **The writer's reading of an unknown word:** "cattywumpus" as CAT-uh-WAM-pus. **Compounds (v63):** "footwell = foot + well."

**Needs fixing:**

1. **27.21's motif comes from the title line's first four notes, not the title's words.** The title line is "the afternoon will have to make do." Its first four notes are "the af-ter-noon," a neighbour figure on E3–F♯3, not the hook. The break was built on pickup words while the hook ("make DO," up a third and held) went unused. The motif could take the title's own words' cell (the kernel, or the last stressed notes with the peak), or the line's stressed notes.
2. **27.21's peak rule for a one-phrase line.** The intro and outro are four bars, so one phrase each. "Past the first third" isn't meaningful there, and the intro's peak fell at 0.31. The run exempted one-phrase lines. 27.21 should say whether a line of one phrase has a peak rule.
3. **A phrasal verb's stress.** The lexicon reads "do" as unstressed, so "make DO" put the hook's stress on "make" and the title cell couldn't be placed. The run added a rule: a phrasal verb's last word takes the phrase stress at the line's end. v58's stress rules could gain this one (make do, give up, let go…), printed when it fires.
4. **The voice question's options should come from 11.2's rows.** The run offered "male, low and close (about A2–D4)," but no row goes below B2. The contract proposes the row after the answer (v57); the options themselves should quote the rows (here, bass / baritone B2–C4, D4 in the final chorus) so the writer chooses a real range.
5. **The lead track's name.** 11.11 (v64) names it "Instrumental lead — <instrument>" with an em dash. MIDI track names are Latin-1, so the demo writer refused it, and the run used a hyphen. The name should be ASCII-safe.
6. **Does the instrumental line count in the band's energy?** The cue line's notes lift the instrumental sections (the intro reads 0.48 against the verses' 0.26). 9.12 should say whether the instrumental lead counts as band, or is read apart like the voice.
7. **The survival band, again.** 77.9% of those built, the third song running above the cell-first band. v64 lists what's satisfied by construction instead of re-measuring the band. With four certificates guaranteed every time, the band's upper edge is now routinely exceeded, and its warning carries no signal.
