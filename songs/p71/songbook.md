# This Side of the Door (second edition): songbook and production notes

Words and music: Jason Bryant

## At a glance

**What it is about.** A woman stands all night at her brother's door. He has shut himself in with his addiction, and she has come without anyone, only soup and a key that no longer fits. She presses her ear to the wood and listens for the sound of him living, and it has gone down to one note. The door never opens. She leaves the soup, and knocks the way they knocked as children.

**How to play it.** E major, ♩ = 80, 4/4, a ballad. 88 bars, about 4:24. One alto voice (F♯3 to B4), fingerpicked acoustic guitar throughout; a cello from verse 2; strings and bass in the bridge and the last chorus; a harmony voice in the last chorus. No drums.

## For each player

**Voice.** An alto, F♯3 to B4. She is talking through a door: plain, close, never performed. The choruses sit about 2 semitones higher; let them open a little without pushing. The bridge sits low (F♯3 to E4) while the band opens up around her: stay small there. The title line ends each chorus: F♯4 F♯4 G♯4 F♯4 D♯4 F♯4 F♯4. The song’s top note, B4, comes in bar 2 of each chorus.

**Harmony voice.** The last chorus only, with her in her rhythm and on her words, from its first line until “Three, and then two” (bar 81), where she is left alone. 60 in 100 of its notes sit a third to a fifth below her. Range D♯3 to A4; the notes under G3 want a low alto, or take them up an octave. Keep it soft and under her.

**Acoustic guitar.** Fingerpicked eighths from bar 1 to the end: the thumb on the chord's root (the fifth when a chord holds the whole bar), the fingers on its third and fifth. Where a chord tone would sit a step from the note she sings, pick another tone of the chord. The last bar: let the chord ring. Range E2 to G♯4.

**Cello.** From verse 2 (bar 35) through the last chorus: the chord's root, held a bar, or a half bar where the chord changes. Long, even bows; no swell. Range A2 to G♯3.

**Strings.** The bridge (from bar 47) and the last chorus until bar 80: the chord held high and soft. Notes a half step from the singer are left out. Range G♯4 to F♯5.

**Bass.** The bridge and the last chorus, until “Three, and then two”: quarter notes, roots and fifths, with passing notes into the next root. Range F♯1 to E2. Under the cello, never over it.

## For the singer

Breathe between lines: her phrases run 3.8 seconds at the median, the longest stretch without half a beat's rest 9.4 seconds. Lines that end on a long note (in verse 1: “Tuesday”, “door”, “frost”, “like”) want to be held to their length and released, not clipped. Do not act the grief: the words carry it. The verses are reported, the choruses are the plea. In the last chorus the band falls away at “Three, and then two”: sing those words like a knock, and leave the last line almost spoken.

## The arrangement

The song opens up while the door stays shut. The guitar is alone with her through the first verse and chorus. A cello comes in under the second verse. The bridge is the peak: strings and bass arrive while she sings at her lowest, so the size is in the room, not in her voice. The third verse drops back to guitar and cello, the vigil. The last chorus brings everything back with a harmony voice, then at “Three, and then two” it all falls away and she finishes with the guitar alone; the outro is the guitar by itself. No drums anywhere.

### Producer one-sheet

**Emotional target.** A sister talking through a locked door to a brother she cannot reach, with no anger left, only patience. It should break the listener's heart by staying calm: the door never opens, and she will be back.

**References.**

- Neil Young, “The Needle and the Damage Done” (Harvest, 1972): take the plainness, one voice and one guitar watching and telling, recorded live, short, without a sermon.
- Brandi Carlile, “Sugartooth” (By the Way, I Forgive You, 2018): take how an addiction is told from outside, through small observed details, and how the band grows under the voice.
- Lucinda Williams, “Sweet Old World” (Sweet Old World, 1992): take a woman's voice speaking straight to the one she is losing, an inventory of ordinary, tender things.

**Dynamic arc.**

| Section | Bars | Level | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intro | 1–2 | soft | guitar alone |
| Verse 1 | 3–14 | light | voice and guitar |
| Chorus 1 | 15–30 | full | guitar a little fuller |
| Turn | 31–34 | medium | the guitar alone; she rests |
| Verse 2 | 35–46 | light | cello in (bar 35) |
| Bridge | 47–58 | strong | strings and bass in; the peak |
| Verse 3 | 59–68 | light | strings and bass out |
| Last Chorus | 69–84 | full | all in, harmony voice; all out at bar 81 but the guitar |
| Outro | 85–88 | light | guitar alone |

**Non-negotiables.**

- ♩ = 80, steady; no push anywhere.
- No drums.
- The bridge is the band's peak, not the singer's: she stays low and plain.
- At “Three, and then two” (bar 81) everything but the guitar stops.
- The harmony voice only in the last chorus, under her.
- The last chorus ends on B, unresolved; only the guitar's outro comes home, to E.

**Mix priorities.** The voice in front, dry and very close, as if through wood. The guitar just left of centre, under her. The cello left, the strings right and back. Bass felt more than heard. Nothing may cover the words; nothing may swell over the last two lines.

### Production brief

**The song.** “This Side of the Door”, words and music by Jason Bryant: a ballad in E major at ♩ = 80, 4:24, for an alto with fingerpicked acoustic guitar, cello, strings, bass and a harmony voice.

**References** (checked): Neil Young, “The Needle and the Damage Done” (1972); Brandi Carlile, “Sugartooth” (2018); Lucinda Williams, “Sweet Old World” (1992). What to take from each is on the one-sheet.

**Non-negotiables.** As on the one-sheet.

### Session notes

- Tempo and click: ♩ = 80 throughout, 4/4. The voice enters in bar 2.
- Key: E major. Chords bar by bar are on the number chart and above the lead sheet.
- Range: voice F♯3 to B4; harmony D♯3 to A4.
- Tracking: guitar and voice together if the singer can, for the live feel of the reference; then cello, strings, bass, and the harmony voice last.
- Blend and panning: voice centre; guitar slightly left; cello left; strings right; harmony slightly right; bass centre.
- Every part can be played by one player at once; the strings are one section, or one player overdubbed.
