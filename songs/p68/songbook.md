# Same Time Tomorrow (sixteenth edition): songbook and production notes

Words and music: Jason Bryant

## At a glance

**What it is about.** An old preacher in his last days tells about the boy who comes to change his sheets, and the promise they make each other every evening: same time tomorrow. It is a small promise, kept daily, and by the last chorus it has come to hold up more than the pulpit ever did.

**How to play it.** A major, ♩ = 84, 4/4, a folk ballad. 118 bars, about 5:37. One baritone voice (A2 to A3), a harmony voice in the choruses, acoustic guitar, bass and drums.

## For each player

**Voice.** A baritone, A2 to A3. Tell it plainly in the verses; the choruses sit where the verses do, so let the band and the harmony voice lift them, not the pitch. Sing the title as written, on the chorus's downbeat: E3 E3 E3 E3 E3. The song’s top note, A3, comes 7 times: on “doesn't” in the bridge (bar 71); on “know” in the bridge (bar 72); on “I” in the bridge (bar 72).

**Harmony voice.** Under the lead in all three choruses, in his rhythm and on his words; 62 in 100 of its notes sit a third to a fifth below him. Range E2 to F♯3: the lowest notes want a true bass, or take them up an octave. Keep it soft; he leads.

**Acoustic guitar.** Steady eighth-note strums from bar 1 to the end, under his voice; where a chord tone would sit a step from the note he sings, leave it out. Range F♯3 to G♯4.

**Bass.** Quarter notes on every beat: roots and fifths, with chromatic passing notes a half step from the next root. Range E1 to F♯2. Lock with the kick on 1 and 3.

**Drums.** Kick on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4, hat eighths, from bar 2 to the end. A tom fill in the bar before each chorus that follows a verse (bars 18, 52, 98).

## For the singer

Breathe between lines: his phrases run 3.9 seconds at the median. Some lines run straight into the next with less than a beat between them (into bars 15, 19, 20, 49, 53, 54, 83, 95 and others); take a quick breath in the gap or sing through, as feels natural; the longest stretch without half a beat's rest is 7.9 seconds. Lines that end on a long note (in verse 1: “young”, “story”, “morning”) want to be held to their length and released, not clipped. Verse 3 is the hardest to sing plainly; do not lean on it. The last chorus is longer than the others and ends on the title: leave the last word open.

## The arrangement

The band stays in one groove the whole way: the guitar strums eighths under him from the first bar to the last, with the bass on every beat and the drums on 1 and 3, 2 and 4. What changes is the harmony voice in the choruses, a tom fill leading into each chorus that follows a verse, and the last chorus, two bars longer than the others. After chorus 1 the band plays four bars alone. Keep the verses intimate and let the choruses fill out only as much as the harmony voice brings.

### Producer one-sheet

**Emotional target.** A dying man telling, without self-pity, about a boy whose daily visit has come to mean more than his whole ministry. It should feel like a kept promise, not a farewell.

**References.**

- Jason Isbell, “Elephant” (Southeastern, 2013): take the plain, unsentimental telling of a friend’s dying, and the quiet, patient acoustic setting.
- John Prine, “Hello in There” (John Prine, 1971): take the conversational phrasing and the way small details of ordinary lives carry the weight.
- Johnny Cash, “Hurt” (American IV, 2002): take the low voice right in front, with a sparse acoustic guitar that leaves room around every word.

**Non-negotiables.**

- ♩ = 84, steady; no push into the choruses.
- The title as written every time: on the chorus's downbeat.
- The harmony voice below the lead, never above.
- The guitar under him, never a step from the note he sings.
- The last chorus’s last bar: E A.

**Mix priorities.** The voice in front and centre, dry and close. The harmony voice tucked under it, slightly right. The guitar left of centre, under the voice. Bass and kick locked together under everything. Nothing may cover the title line.
