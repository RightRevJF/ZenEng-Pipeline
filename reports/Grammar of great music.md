# Lasting songs share a skeleton, not a language

The evidence supports half of the conjecture. Songs that last do share a grammar, but it is a small core of habits that every culture uses, spoken in many local dialects. It is not one complete language waiting to be decoded. The core is consistent and measurable: small steps between notes, a home note, a steady pulse, few note lengths, short phrases that repeat, and a stable "skeleton" of strong-beat notes that survives when a tune is passed from singer to singer. Songs that lasted in oral traditions hold to this core more tightly than songs that changed or vanished. Surprise also follows a placement rule: it pays off when the song has made the listener confident, and arrivals (phrase ends, cadences, the chorus) tend to be predictable. But every effect is small. Notes-only features explain roughly 5 to 10 percent of the difference in survival or recognisability, and social luck caps what any notes-only judge can achieve. The best "Rosetta Stone" is not the hit list. It is the tune family: one tune preserved in many versions, where the notes that were kept and the notes that were changed show what memory and love select for. That is also where Luthier can test its seed ideas now, without waiting for outcome data that is still the project's bottleneck.

## The short version, in plain words

|---|---|

| A steady beat with simple ratios (1:1, 2:1) | Very sure |
| Few different note lengths | Sure |
| Short phrases that repeat | Sure |
| Phrases that arch or fall | Fairly sure |
| Octaves and "sweet" harmony | Not shared; learned |

**Your seed ideas**

| Seed idea | Status |
|---|---|

**Why outcome data is the bottleneck.** To learn what makes a song last, you need songs that lasted *and* songs that did not, with trustworthy counts. Chart lists and cover lists contain only winners. The project's own folk study matched only 473 of 12,761 printed tunes to recordings, had about a one-in-five chance of detecting a real effect, and about half of its Irish and Scottish title matches were wrong. The way around this is to study change *within* tune families first, where the outcome is built into the data.

## One core, many dialects: the shape of the world's song

No feature of the notes is found in every culture, but a small set of statistical universals appears in every region studied. Savage and colleagues coded 304 recordings from nine world regions and found **no absolute universals but 18 statistical universals**, consistent across all nine regions, plus a network of 10 features that tend to occur together ([Exeter ORE](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/31311)). In that sample an isochronous beat appeared at a long-term frequency of **0.85**, motivic patterns 0.90, phrases under 9 seconds 0.81, and fewer than five note lengths 0.72 ([Savage et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517223/)). Coding reliability for several rhythm features was weak (κ as low as −0.02 for "few durations"), so the exact percentages are soft even if the pattern is not.

The Natural History of Song gives the sharpest numbers. Music turned up in all 315 societies in the ethnographic record. In 118 field recordings from 86 societies, **unison, major second and minor third made up 73% of melodic note pairs**, and the tritone 0.2%. Simple rhythm ratios of 1:1, 2:1 and 3:1 made up **86% of rhythmic note pairs**. Expert listeners heard a tonal centre in **97.8%** of ratings ([Mehr et al. 2019](https://dcknox.github.io/files/MehrSinghKnoxEtAl_UniversalityDiversityHumanSong.pdf)). Two caveats matter for "every culture weighed equally": the distributions were pooled across cultures rather than computed per culture, and the tonality judgments came from Western theorists and a key-finder trained on Western listeners ([Harrison, ch. 13](https://pmcharrison.github.io/intro-to-music-and-science/music-across-the-world.html)).

Phrase shape adds another shared habit. In 35 solo songs from the Garland collection, phrases arched and fell in both Western and non-Western subsamples, and the average contour correlated **r = .95 with bird song** and r = .88 with the Essen folk collection ([Savage, Tierney & Patel](https://core.ac.uk/download/74204746.pdf)). Scales show the same mix of constraint and freedom: across 896 scales from 73 societies, most have seven or fewer notes, steps cluster near 200 cents, and humans use only about **1% of possible octave scales** ([DaMuSc](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.00842)). Rhythm perception is shared at its base. All 39 groups tested in 15 countries showed discrete rhythm categories at small-integer ratios, with **isochrony (1:1:1) and 1:1:2 present in every group** ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990)).

### The dialects are real and sit on top of the core

The same rhythm study shows where cultures part ways. The 3:3:2 pattern is the main axis of difference (strong in African and Afro-diasporic groups, d = 2.60), 2:2:3 is strong among traditional musicians in Turkey, Botswana and Bulgaria, and 7:2:3 appears among Malian drummers. Two dimensions captured **85.9% of between-group divergence**, and university and online samples looked more like US students than like local musicians in their own countries ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990)). Malian jembe drummers play uneven short-medium-long subdivisions as precisely as even ones, which led the authors to call isochrony "a historically popular option" rather than a biological constraint ([Polak et al. 2016](https://frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00285/full)).

Two features often treated as natural laws look learned. Tsimane' listeners in Bolivia with little exposure to global music showed **no preference for consonant intervals** (BF10 = 0.24), while more integrated Tsimane' did, and aversion to roughness was the only universal result ([McPherson-McNato et al. 2025](https://mcdermottlab.mit.edu/papers/McPherson_etal_2025_global_integration.pdf)). Octave equivalence was absent in Tsimane' singing, weak in US non-musicians and strong only in US musicians, while logarithmic pitch and relative-pitch accuracy held everywhere ([Jacoby et al. 2019](https://piv.medicina.uc.cl/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Jacoby_et_al_2019.pdf)). For Luthier this means octave-based and consonance-based rules belong in the dialect layer, not the core.

### Most variation lives inside each culture

The language analogy holds up when variance is measured. Across 5,242 songs from 719 societies, **54 to 72% of musical variation lies within societies** and 29 to 43% between them, compared with 93 to 95% within for genes. Music correlates only weakly with genes (Mantel r = 0.15) and language (r = 0.18) and diffuses across language boundaries up to about 4,000 km ([Passmore et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-48113-7.pdf)). That rules out both extremes: there is no single world grammar, because consonance, octave and rhythm data differ by culture, and no culture is a sealed world, because the universals recur across regions and methods. The honest model has three levels: a core shared by all, dialect parameters per culture, and the individual song. A lasting song probably needs to be well-formed at both of the first two levels. One gap stands out: no study has partitioned notes-only features across many cultures with a multilevel model, since Passmore's data are performance-style codes rather than transcribed notes. Study 6 below fills it.

## Surprise pays only when the song has earned it

The best single predictors of note-by-note expectation are statistical models trained on a corpus, chiefly IDyOM, which scores each note's surprise against what the training music made likely. Information content (surprise) explains **up to 83% of variance** in listeners' pitch expectations on short Western contexts ([Pearce 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6849749)). Surprise is always relative to a culture. A model trained on German folk songs finds Chinese songs more surprising (1.72 bits per note) than German ones (1.34), and a Chinese-trained model finds the reverse ([Pearce 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6849749)). Simple rules still add something learned statistics miss: listeners pull to the tonic at cadences **more strongly than either IDyOM or a rule model predicts** ([Morgan et al. 2019](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0zd498dt/qt0zd498dt.pdf?t=pufkb7)). Those rule-like pulls, rather than raw surprise, are the better cross-cultural grammar candidates.

### Pleasure depends on surprise and confidence together

Across about 80,000 chords from 745 Billboard songs, stripped so the songs could not be recognised, pleasure was highest for **a surprise after a confident context, or a predictable event after an uncertain one** ([MPG press release](https://www.mpg.de/14119325/the-science-of-a-billboard-hit-song)). This interaction has been reproduced with melodies, but within one research network and with Western music only. Gold and colleagues found an inverted U for liking against average surprise (R² = .263) with only a marginal interaction ([Gold et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867811)). Their 2023 fMRI follow-up kept the interaction but found surprise only **lowered** liking in a straight line, with no inverted U ([Gold et al. 2023](https://frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2023.1209398/pdf)). A review of 57 studies over about 115 years found 87.7% compatible with an inverted U for familiarity and complexity ([Chmiel & Schubert 2017](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/back-to-the-inverted-u-for-music-preference-a-review-of-the-liter/)), but one large online study found the group-level inverted U was really **two clusters**, complexity-lovers and simplicity-lovers, averaged together ([Güçlütürk & van Lier 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6457315)). The robust rule is conditional: surprise has to be set up. Average surprise alone is a weak target, and for unfamiliar listeners less surprise on average tends to beat more.

The only test that removes enculturation used an invented scale (Bohlen-Pierce). US and Chinese participants both preferred melodies with "grammatical" endings over prediction-error endings, and liking rose then fell over 16 hearings ([Kathios et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10516817)). That is the closest thing yet to a culture-free expectation rule, though it tested a simpler manipulation than the full surprise-and-confidence surface.

### Placement: surprise in the journey, calm at the arrival

Several separate literatures point to the same placement rule. In 50 Haydn quartet expositions, strong cadence arrivals were **less surprising than other events**, and surprise rose in order from perfect authentic to deceptive to evaded cadences ([Sears et al. 2018](https://www.music.mcgill.ca/~caplin/sears-simulating-melodic.pdf)). In 545 Billboard songs, top-quartile hits carried more harmonic surprise than bottom-quartile hits (d = 0.29), but the gap lived in the **verses** (d = 0.33). Songs whose surprise *dropped* into the chorus showed the largest difference (d = 0.96 in that subset) ([Miles et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00263/pdf)). Every song in that study charted, so the result compares winners with lesser winners. In oral variants, strong final downbeats were rarely changed and short ornamental notes were the ones typically deleted ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/researchers-analyzed-folk-music-like-it-was-mutating-dna-they-found-amazing-parallels-between-life-and-art/)). In Palestrina, leaps fall disproportionately on longer notes, which fits the idea that surprising events are given time ([Temperley 2019](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.19.25.2/mto.19.25.2.temperley.pdf)).

Peaks follow the mirror rule. In 1,019 pieces with 1,806 timestamped chills, **melodic uncertainty was the single most important predictor of chill onsets**, with surprise rising at the onset, but the classifier reached only AUC 0.597 ([de Fleurian et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.10.02.616280.full.pdf)). Dopamine in the caudate rises in the roughly 15 seconds **before** a chill and the nucleus accumbens peaks during it ([Salimpoor et al. 2011](https://www.zlab.mcgill.ca/publications/docs/salimpoor_2011_nn.pdf)), and drugs that raise or block dopamine shift musical pleasure up or down ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/01/190124110958.htm)). The grammar this suggests: build uncertainty legibly during the approach, place a local surprise at the peak, ideally on a long, strong note, and make arrivals predictable. No study has tested surprise on the title or hook word, so the words-as-amplifier question is still open.

### The tune that teaches itself has never been measured

Theory already separates the two kinds of surprise in this seed idea. IDyOM's long-term model stands for the culture, and its short-term model "learns the current piece and is reset before each new piece" ([Pearce 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6849749)). The brain weights the culture more: cortical surprise responses fit the long-term model far better than the short-term one (d = 2.19 vs 0.97), and the brain's effective context peaks at only **about 7 to 8 notes** ([Kern et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9836393)). So any "teaching" happens at motif scale, and the second statement of a motif is where surprise against the song so far should collapse. Memory supports the idea indirectly: over three sessions, predictable sequences were remembered better and better, and by session three surprise explained 85% of variance in recognition memory ([Pearce 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6849749)). In pop, recognisability rose with both typicality against other songs and recurrence within the song itself, though marginal R² was only .07 to .10 ([Van Balen et al. 2015](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2015/paper/000148.pdf)).

Repetition matters, but its timing matters too. Listeners preferred a Berio piece after sections were cut and looped to make it repetitive ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2014/04/07/300178813/play-it-again-and-again-sam)). Liking rose steadily over 28 spaced hearings across four weeks in daily life ([Madison & Schiölde 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5374342)), while seven hearings in a row lowered it ([Gold et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867811)). **No study has plotted surprise-to-the-culture against surprise-to-the-song across time and linked that path to survival, covers or longevity.** The idea is open, testable, and in Luthier's hands. The predicted shape: surprise against the song drops steeply after each motif's first statement, while surprise against the culture stays moderate rather than minimal.

## Oral memory keeps a skeleton and sands off the ornaments

The cleanest evidence about what survives comes from songs passed mouth to mouth. Savage and colleagues aligned 10,062 English and Japanese folk melodies like DNA and found that **notes with stronger rhythmic roles change less, substitutions go mostly to neighbouring notes, and insertions and deletions outnumber substitutions**. The same patterns held in both traditions despite very different scales ([Savage et al. 2022](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/sequence-alignment-of-folk-song-melodies-reveals-cross-cultural-r/)). The paper was published in *Current Biology*, not PNAS. Because indels dominate, any variant comparison Luthier runs should use gapped alignment rather than note-by-note matching.

In Dutch oral tradition, Janssen and colleagues measured how often each of 9,639 phrases from 147 tune families recurs across variants. Shorter phrases were most stable (β = −0.30), followed by **less surprising phrases (−0.24)**, earlier phrases (−0.10), smaller intervals (+0.10), and phrase and motif repetition (+0.09, +0.08). The hypothesis that surprising material is more memorable was rejected. Marginal R² was only .05, rising to .22 once tune-family differences were included ([Janssen et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5403935/)). The direction is clear but the effect is small.

Lab transmission chains show the same filter at work. In 12 experiments with 1,797 participants in the US and India, random tone sequences passed down chains of singers became structured. **Leaps above 7 semitones fell from 36.4% to 11.9%**, contours turned into arches, melodies settled at 6 to 7 tones, and copying errors fell ([Anglada-Tort et al. 2023](https://research.gold.ac.uk/36541/1/Anglada-Tort_2023.pdf)). The attractors were cultural: mean interval was 3.25 semitones in India against 5.71 in the US, and social transmission **widened** the gap between the two cultures (divergence 0.17 vs 0.05 under individual transmission). Melodies passed through a slider drifted toward large intervals, so the small-step habit comes from the voice and memory, not from abstract taste ([Anglada-Tort et al. 2023](https://research.gold.ac.uk/36541/1/Anglada-Tort_2023.pdf)). Drumming chains produced all six rhythmic universals from Savage's 2015 list and became easier to learn ([Ravignani et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-016-0007)).

This settles one part of the Rosetta Stone question. Survival selects for a transmissible skeleton (strong-beat notes, home and fifth degrees, small steps, short repeated phrases) and lets the surface mutate. Because social transmission sharpens dialects, songs that lasted within a tradition should be **more typical of that dialect**, not less. Memorable songs add one twist: earworms had common overall contours but slightly unusual interval patterns, and three features reached 62.5% accuracy against popularity-matched controls ([Jakubowski et al. 2017](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1371487)). "Typical in shape, distinctive in detail" is the closest thing to a recipe the evidence offers, and it rests on UK chart pop alone.

A flagged result agrees. McBride and Fitch's 2026 preprint reports that substitution rate tracks metrical hierarchy (r = 0.71) and that tonic and dominant are the most stable pitches ([arXiv 2607.12673](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12673)). **Its main corpus is TheSession.org, so it may be mentioned only as a flagged finding, and no Luthier study may use or re-derive its data.** A 2024 cross-corpus paper by McBride and colleagues uses an unnamed "database of Irish folk songs" in one comparison ([arXiv 2408.12635](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.12635v1)). Its provenance should be checked before anything from that comparison is reused.

## Charts and covers hide the forgotten songs

Predicting hits from features has a low ceiling. Pachet and Roy found that the "high popularity" class "cannot be learned" from acoustic features, and a control built from the letters of song titles scored almost as well as the music ([Pachet & Roy 2008](https://csl.sony.fr/wp-content/uploads/2008/01/pachet-08c.pdf)). Dance-chart studies reached **AUC 0.53 to 0.67** ([Herremans et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.08076)). From melody alone, a random forest separated hits from non-hits at 52.6%, near chance ([Frieler et al. 2015](https://www.psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.12034/2443)). Among 26,846 Billboard songs, optimal differentiation from recent hits produced an inverted U, but a song one standard deviation less typical stayed on the chart only about **11.5 weeks instead of 11** ([Askin & Mauskapf 2017](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/25509/Mauskapf_productfeatures.pdf)). The project's within-artist chord-repetition effect of about −0.04 sits in this same small-effect range. It matches the literature rather than being a failure.

Social luck sets a hard ceiling. In the MusicLab experiment with 14,341 participants, social influence made outcomes more unequal and less predictable. **The best songs rarely did poorly and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible** ([Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~bor/303s19/docs/salganik_dodds_watts.pdf)). A notes-only judge should be evaluated on whether it sorts the tails, not on ranking the middle.

Covers are concentrated and social too. In a network of 855,650 cover versions, **only 22% of artists had ever been covered and the top 0.1% accounted for 41% of versions**. Genre is the main driver, language second, and 63% of artists are English-speaking ([Ortega 2021](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/f132/4479f4f0d56a4014d98322613582fdd56f11.pdf)). Simonton's study of 15,618 classical themes found fame rising with the originality of the opening notes and then falling at high originality ([Simonton 1980](https://stafforini.com/works/simonton-1980-thematic-fame-melodica/)). A small 2022 replication on 428 Romantic pieces, using Google hits as the popularity measure, found nothing (r = −0.034) ([arXiv 2210.12201](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.12201)). Repetition shows the same pattern: chorus repetition in lyrics predicted reaching number one ([Nunes et al. 2015](https://iris.unibocconi.it/handle/11565/3955321)), but audio repetition did not predict chart position within the German Top 40 (r = .03 to .10) ([Czedik-Eysenberg et al. 2023](https://homepage.univie.ac.at/christoph.reuter/unterwegs/ICMPC2023_Czedik-Eysenberg_Roos_Weber_Klooss_Cui_Reuter_Oehler.pdf)). Repetition looks like a threshold every lasting song clears rather than a dial that keeps paying.

Almost every corpus is survivor-selected, including PDMX, Lakh, chart samples and cover databases, and in PDMX only 5.6% of 254,077 scores have ratings at all ([PDMX](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.10831v2)). Real denominators exist in a few places. The New York Philharmonic archive records every performance since 1842 under CC0, including works played once and never again ([NY Phil](https://github.com/nyphilarchive/PerformanceHistory)). Hymnary.org counts each "instance" of a tune in a hymnal ([Hymnary definitions](https://hymnary.org/wiki/definition_of_terms)), which gives dated reprint counts with one-time tunes as natural non-survivors. The Roud index holds about 250,000 references to nearly 25,000 songs with singer, place and date ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roud_Folk_Song_Index)). Catalogs of copyright registrations list every registered song, survivors or not ([NYPL CCE](https://cce-search.nypl.org/about)). No published study has yet compared the melodies of survivors and non-survivors using reprint or reference counts. Outside Europe and East Asia, no notes-level popularity study and no licence-checked survival counts were found at all, so equal weighting of cultures is weakest exactly where the outcomes are.

## Economy beats raw complexity, and 1/f is a style fingerprint

Tension models predict moment-to-moment tension ratings well but have never been tested against long-run outcomes. Farbood's model, built from 2,661 listeners in 108 countries, correlated with continuous tension ratings from r = .67 to .99, and found that **parallel changes across features strengthened perceived tension while conflicting changes made it ambiguous** ([Farbood 2012](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/f/11865/files/2020/08/Farbood_2012_Musical_Tension.pdf)). That is the closest experimental support for "layers peaking together," though it concerns how clear tension is, not liking or survival. Which layer carries the tension depends on the piece: a harmony-only reduction tracked the original at .85 for a Mozart movement but −.41 for a Mendelssohn piece ([Lehne et al. 2013](https://www.stefan-koelsch.de/papers/Lehne_2013_THE%20INFLUENCE%20OF%20DIFFERENT%20STRUCTURAL%20FEATURES%20ON%20FELT%20MUSICAL%20TENSION%20IN%20TWO%20PIANO%20PIECES%20BY%20MOZART%20AND%20MENDELSSOHN.pdf)).

At the bottom of the hierarchy, layers clearly coincide. English songs mismatch stress and beat only 1 to 3% of the time and listeners chose stress-matched settings 82.5% of the time, while French songs mismatch about 35 to 40% ([Lee et al. 2017](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/20574/1/LeeBrownMullensiefen_MLE_MusicPerception_2017.pdf)). Any alignment score must therefore be calibrated to the lyric language. In 909 Chinese pop songs, cadences are a coincidence of long note, tonic and boundary: V–I appears at 94% of section ends against 47% elsewhere, and whole notes or longer end 72% of section-final phrases against 6.4% of mid-section ones ([Dai et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.07518)). Claims that climaxes fall at the golden section rest on anecdote without significance testing ([Science Frontiers](https://science-frontiers.com/sf107/sf107p14.htm)).

### Compression works only when it understands music

Economy is measurable, but the compressor must understand music. On 360 Dutch folk melodies, a music-aware pattern compressor (COSIATEC) sorted tunes into families with **83.9% accuracy against 12.5% for bzip2**, even though bzip2 compressed the files more. Compression ratio alone was a poor guide ([Meredith 2014](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/198267449/PID3175191.pdf)). Across algorithms, shorter descriptions went with better musical analyses ([Louboutin & Meredith 2016](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/using-general-purpose-compression-algorithms-for-music-analysis)). In 909 Chinese pop songs, repeated phrases cover about 50 to 90% of each song, most songs use only two or three distinct phrases, and many returns are partial rather than exact ([Dai et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.07518)). A small, non-peer-reviewed test found top-10 Billboard melodies no more compressible than ranks 11 to 20 ([Sanchez & Albrecht 2020](https://oaks.kent.edu/ugresearch/2020/art/melodies-pop-measuring-melodic-repetitiveness-throughout-billboard-era)). Predictive information rate, which is zero for both pure repetition and pure noise, is the most principled formula for "compressible but not trivially so," but its authors offered it as a conjecture and never tested it on listeners ([Abdallah & Plumbley 2009](https://core.ac.uk/download/30696123.pdf)). No study has linked any compression measure to covers or oral survival.

### 1/f depends on what is measured

Scaling is real but depends on what is measured. Across 1,788 movements by 40 or more Western composers, rhythm spectra had a median exponent of **β = 0.78**, with shuffled controls near zero. Beethoven was the most predictable and Mozart among the least, and no listener or popularity data were collected ([Levitin et al. 2012](https://www.logosfoundation.org/kursus/PNAS-2012-Levitin-3716-201.pdf)). Pitch sequences run steeper (β about 1.2 to 1.9), and listeners judged artificial tone sequences most "melodic" around **β ≈ 1.5**, not 1. The optimum differed by tradition (Western near 1.58, Chinese near 1.35), and β tracked interval size almost perfectly ([Beauvois 2007](https://researchgate.net/profile/Michael-Beauvois/publication/240759806_Quantifying_Aesthetic_Preference_And_Perceived_Complexity_For_Fractal_Melodies/links/5a5a48d6aca2727d60860cfa/Quantifying-Aesthetic-Preference-And-Perceived-Complexity-For-Fractal-Melodies.pdf)). Since revered composers sit at opposite ends of the range, 1/f is best used as a well-formedness band (not white noise, not drifting) and a style fingerprint. It is not a greatness ranker. No study links β to popularity or survival, and the often-quoted Zipf-law accuracy figures for music could not be verified.

## Rhythm: a universal pulse with local accents

Groove is the best-replicated pleasure law in rhythm. Three labs found that medium syncopation maximises wanting to move and pleasure, with quadratic fits explaining **35% and 43% of variance** in one study ([Witek et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0094446)) and a strong inverted U again in 201 respondents ([Matthews et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6328141)). Placement beats amount here too. A **single salient anticipation of a phrase-final strong beat** worked as well as many syncopations, and maximal syncopation was rated lower ([Sioros et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01036/full)). High syncopation also has a memory cost: tapping error correlated .82 with syncopation and next-day recognition fell as syncopation rose (r = .58) ([Fitch & Rosenfeld 2007](https://web.uvic.ca/~aschloss/course_mat/MUS%20511/ARTICLES%20AND%20REFS%20FOR%20320/FitchRosenfeld20071.pdf)). That cost explains the falling side of the curve and links groove to oral survival.

Every one of these groove studies used 4/4 with mostly European and North American listeners. The same rhythms are rated **more syncopated in 6/8 than in 4/4** ([Song et al. 2013](https://core.ac.uk/download/77038427.pdf)), so any syncopation target must be set per metre. Triple and compound metres are a core folk option, not a waltz niche: about 43% of 5,592 Essen melodies are in triple or compound metre, against about 18% of 7,351 Finnish tunes. A notes-only classifier recovered metre about 83% of the time, with durational accent as the strongest cue ([Toiviainen & Eerola 2005](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000049.pdf)). That classifier doubles as a self-test for Luthier drafts: if the notes alone cannot project the intended metre, the draft is not projecting it. Transmission leans duple. Drumming chains produced binary but not ternary grouping ([Ravignani et al. 2016](https://core.ac.uk/download/195266656.pdf)), and two-beat subdivision appears in 84% of world recordings ([Savage et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517223/)). Triple-metre songs probably need firmer downbeat anchors to travel, though no study has tested this.

Many famous timelines across cultures are "maximally even" patterns, such as the tresillo E(3,8), the West African bell E(7,12), and the Bulgarian and Turkish aksak patterns ([Toussaint 2005](https://cgm.cs.mcgill.ca/~godfried/publications/banff.pdf)). Their claim to being "good" rests on survival in traditions, and a critic notes the work offers no perceptual validation ([Gotham 2013](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.2/mto.13.19.2.gotham.php)). Speech rhythm leaves a mark too: English themes show more durational contrast than French ones ([Patel & Daniele 2003](https://ccc.inaoep.mx/~villasen/bib/ritmo%20lenguage%20y%20musical%20-Patel_Daniele_Cognition.pdf)), though English and French folk songs did not differ significantly ([Hannon et al. 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00939/pdf)). Rhythm, then, has a universal floor (pulse, small ratios, few lengths, a long note at the cycle's end) under culture-specific categories that a scorer must reward rather than punish.

## Grammars and geometry describe well, predict little

Formal grammars of harmony now exist in probabilistic, partly learnable form, but they are scored on whether they reproduce an expert's parse, not on musical value. A trained abstract grammar matched expert jazz trees 45.95% of the time against 39.43% for a plain grammar, evaluated on only 13 annotated pieces ([Harasim et al. 2018](https://ismir2018.ismir.net/doc/pdfs/258_Paper.pdf)). For melody, a hand-built Schenker-style grammar won on Bach chorales but **lost to a simple first-order interval model** on Essen folk songs, and its authors warn against assuming grammars are superior ([Abdallah & Gold](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1436801/1/main.pdf)). The most useful result for this project is Bod's memory-based parser. It learned folk phrasing from stored fragments of earlier songs and reached 81% boundary accuracy against 70.6% for a Markov model, but needed **about 4,000 training songs** to approach that level ([Bod](https://www.phil.uu.nl/ozsl/articles/Bod03.pdf)). A tradition's grammar appears to be partly a stored repertoire of successful fragments, which suits a Rosetta Stone approach.

Neural generators show the gap between statistical fluency and song structure. Jazz Transformer output scored 0.12 on long-term structure against 0.35 for real solos ([Wu & Yang 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.01307)), and models trained on pop missed phrase-level repetition and the tonic-at-phrase-end pattern ([Dai et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.00182)). For Luthier this argues for measuring structure directly rather than trusting a large model's likelihood.

Geometry explains why Western practice looks as it does but has not been tested against value. Voice-leading geometry shows composers moving by short paths between near-symmetric chords ([Tymoczko 2006](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/the-geometry-of-musical-chords/)). In the one direct test, Tonnetz distance predicted perceived triad distance at r = .92 while voice-leading distance added nothing ([Milne & Holland 2016](https://core.ac.uk/download/82982228.pdf)). The DFT of pitch-class distributions separates historical styles ([Yust](https://people.bu.edu/jyust/tonalDist_rev.pdf)). The quantum model of tonality is a two-dimensional fit to single probe tones and does not embed melodies ([beim Graben & Blutner](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.07417)). Topological methods have no accuracy figures or baselines yet ([Bergomi & Baratè 2020](https://math-musique.pages.math.unistra.fr/bibliothèque/2020%20-%20BERGOMI%20-%20Homological%20persistence%20in%20time%20series,%20an%20application%20to%20music%20classification.pdf)). On the Hilbert-space intuition, the DFT of a pitch-class vector already is a Hilbert-space projection, so the space itself is easy. The hard part is choosing coordinates that track what people value, and the evidence above says those coordinates are surprise placement, repetition, interval economy and skeleton stability, not chord geometry. Critics add that music has no lexicon of sound-meaning pairs. Jackendoff concluded that language and music share only one detailed formal structure, metre ([Katz & Pesetsky](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/000959/current.pdf)). A musical grammar can encode expectation, tension, resolution and stored fragments, not meaning, which fits "words as amplifier."

## Seven studies Luthier can run next

Outcome data is the bottleneck, and the folk-to-Discogs study shows why: title matching across languages fails, small matched sets leave power near 0.2, and cover counts carry genre, language and prestige noise. The strategy below starts with studies whose outcome is built into licence-checked data (change within Dutch tune families). It then maps core versus dialect across cultures and turns to whole-song survival only once permissions are cleared. Every study follows the same rules so it can be handed straight to the judging research. Measures, predictions and pass marks are written and frozen before the test half is opened. Halves are split by tune family or song, never within one. Power is stated in advance. The pass level is .05 divided by the number of tests. Each measure is benchmarked against a simple interval-Markov baseline and a shuffled-label null, which follows the project's standing caution to keep predeterminism in check. Power figures are my own normal-approximation calculations, assuming independent units. Clustering within tune families lowers them, so the judging research should confirm them by simulation on the training half.

### Study 1: the tune that teaches itself

| Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Data | MTC tune families, Janssen's frame (about 9,639 phrases, 147 families) |
| Rule | Culture model trained only on families outside the test half; song model reset per melody. Per phrase: teaching gap = culture surprise minus song-so-far surprise. Per melody: the path of both curves over time, summarised by when the song curve first falls below the culture curve, and the area between them |
| Prediction | A larger teaching gap means a more stable phrase, after controlling for culture surprise, phrase length, position and repetition. An earlier crossover means a more stable family |
| Pass mark | Standardised effect ≥ 0.05, same sign in both halves |
| Tests | 3 (gap; crossover; gap under a pooled multi-culture model), pass level .0167 |
| Power | About 4,800 phrases per half. If clustering cuts the effective number to about 2,000, power is about .88 for r = .08 but only .44 for r = .05 |
| A fail means | Self-teaching reduces to plain repetition |

The repetition controls are essential, because the song model's surprise falls whenever material repeats. The test asks whether the *path* adds anything beyond the amount of repetition. This is the first test of the idea anywhere, and it is the "shape" version Jason proposed.

### Study 2: where surprise sits

| Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Data | MTC variants, aligned with gaps allowed |
| Rule | Arrival dip = surprise of phrase-final notes minus phrase-interior surprise. Rewarding-quadrant share = share of high-surprise notes after confident contexts plus low-surprise notes after uncertain ones. Surprise-duration coupling = correlation of surprise with length times metric weight |
| Prediction | Deeper arrival dips mean more stable phrases. High-surprise notes that are long and on strong beats are kept more often than high-surprise short weak notes |
| Pass mark | Effect ≥ 0.05 in both halves |
| Tests | 3, pass level .0167 |
| Power | As in Study 1 at phrase level; note-level tests have tens of thousands of notes, so power exceeds .9 for r = .05 before clustering |

### Study 3: layers peaking together

| Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Data | MTC aligned variant pairs, note by note |

| Item | Specification |
|---|---|

### Study 7: which whole songs lasted

| Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Data, route A | Hymn tunes first printed within a span; outcome = later hymnals carrying the tune. **Blocked until a person reads Hymnary's terms or gets written permission** |
| Data, route B | NY Philharmonic repeat programming (CC0) with public-domain scores; opening-notes originality, a Simonton-style test with a real denominator |
| Rule | A composite frozen from Studies 1 to 6 before any outcome is opened |
| Prediction | The composite separates long-lived tunes from one-offs at AUC ≥ 0.60, with clearest separation at the top and bottom tenths |
| Tests | 4, pass level .0125 |
| Power | AUC 0.60 is about d = 0.36. Power .8 needs about 173 per group in the test half; d = 0.3 needs about 248. Plan on roughly 1,000 tunes in total. The folk study's 473 matched tunes gave power near 0.2 |

Two routes are excluded on purpose. Folk-to-recording matching by title is ruled out until matching uses writer credits and language-aware rules. Any use of TheSession.org data is ruled out entirely. Studies 1 to 4 test only Dutch tradition, so a second within-family culture is the next data request: the Savage English and Japanese set, once its licence is confirmed.

## Conclusion

The search has moved from "what do hits have in common?" to "what does memory keep when a song is passed on?" The second question has a cleaner answer and better data, and it is where Jason's seed ideas live. The skeleton that survives transmission (strong beats, stable degrees, small steps, short repeated phrases) is the nearest thing to a universal grammar. Surprise placed in the journey and calm at the arrival is the nearest thing to a universal rhetoric. Both are necessary conditions that lasting songs clear more firmly. Neither is a sufficient formula, and social luck will always blur the middle of the range. The realistic goal for a notes-only judge is a calibrated weak prior that reliably flags the outliers, and that is still worth having.

The literature leaves the most interesting idea, the tune that teaches itself, completely untested, and Luthier can test it now with licence-checked data. If the path from "surprising to the culture" to "predictable to itself" predicts which phrases survive beyond plain repetition, that would be a genuine discovery about how songs become learnable. If it does not, the project learns that repetition alone carries the effect, and the engine can be simplified. Either result moves the work forward, provided predictions stay written before the data is opened.
