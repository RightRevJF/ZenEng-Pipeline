## Summary

- **v7 writes the instrument around the voice (figures, riffs, fills, answers) but has no solo,** and only metal places one. Songs 52 to 55 had 2- to 4-bar links quoting the hook; none had a solo.

- **Solos rose, then fell.** In 739 Billboard songs the share with a solo grew from 22% (1958–64) to 46% (1985–91); three quarters of 1980s hits had a mid-song instrumental section. Later sources describe the decline: instrumental content in number ones fell with hip-hop's rise in the 1990s, and about half of Japan's 2021 year-end hits had no solo at all.

- **A solo is about 8 bars and 20 seconds,** starts just past the middle (0.45 to 0.65 of the song), most often follows the second chorus and leads into the last verse or chorus, and plays its own chords half the time (the verse's 30%, the chorus's 20%). Guitar leads about half; saxophone led early-1960s solos; synthesizer appeared in the 1980s.

- **Short links are nearly universal:** 4 bars in nine of ten POP909 songs, 8-bar interludes in Billboard.

- **What v8 should do:** two kinds of instrumental section (link and solo) with bands for length, place and material; a solo default by era and style; the solo's line from the style's lead instrument, restating or elaborating the tune or building on its scale, and handing back to the voice for a fresh re-entry.

## 2. The academic record

### Where a solo goes in the form

- **In verse-chorus form,** Open Music Theory (Hughes and Lavengood, after Covach 2005) describes the solo as able to stand in for a section: in their model analysis the last cycle begins with a guitar solo that functions as a bridge and replaces the verse.

Covach's compound AABA reading of rock puts the contrasting middle (bridge or solo) after the second verse-chorus cycle.

- **In blues-based forms,** Carter (Music Theory Online 31.1, 2025), studying blues-based songs, names a "solo-bridge model", AABA interrupted by an instrumental solo before the bridge, and an AABA-solo-BA "two-bridge" model.

- **In metal,** a 2022 Oregon dissertation on verse-chorus-bridge form shows bridges given to instrumental passages and dance sections, diverting the usual chorus goal; v7's metal module already puts the solo or breakdown there.

- **At the end,** Osborn's terminally climactic forms and Spicer's cumulative form let the last section, often instrumental, be the climax (Part 25).

### How common solos are

- **Guitar solos in number ones** (Michael Rays, Number One with an Axe, rating every Billboard number one from 1955): the high-water mark was 1957, 40% of number ones; the early 1960s were a "drought" with under 10%; in 1965 only 20% had a guitar solo though 68% had a riff.

- **Instrumental content in number ones** (Chris Dalla Riva, 1940–2021): the average share of a hit without voice declined in two steps, the sharpest beginning in the 1950s and in the 1990s with hip-hop's rise.

- **Now:** a 2021 count of Japan's year-end Kohaku hits found nearly 70% had no guitar solo and almost half had no solo of any kind. Hit Songs Deconstructed reports electric guitar on 61% of US top-10 hits in the first half of 2025, up from 38% in 2024, so instrument use swings with fashion.

### What a solo plays

Analysts describe three common materials: the verse's chords (the "solo over the verse"), the chorus's chords, and new chords of the solo's own; and three common contents: a melodic restatement of the tune, an improvised line on the section's scale, and a riffbased break. None of the sources gives counts; section 3 counts the chords.

## 3. Measured: 739 Billboard songs and 909 POP909 songs

**Data.** The McGill Billboard annotations label each section (solo, interlude, instrumental, theme), give its time, chords and section letter (shared letters mean shared music), and mark the lead instrument and the lead voice. **Where it sits:** a solo follows a chorus 40% of the time, a verse 18%, the bridge 11%, and leads into a verse 33%, a chorus 24%, a second solo 10%, the fade 7%.

### What the numbers say

1. **Solos rose through the corpus:** from about a fifth of early-1960s hits to nearly half by 1985–91, and three quarters of 1980s hits had some mid-song instrumental section. The literature's later decline (Dalla Riva; the Kohaku count) falls after 1991, so v8 needs an era setting here too.

2. **A solo is 8 bars, sometimes 16, about 20 seconds,** and sits just past the middle of the song (median 0.54, middle half 0.45 to 0.65), the same place as v7's breath-and-reentry window (749.19, 0.55 to 0.60).

3. **Half of solos have chords of their own;** the rest play over the verse (30%) or the chorus (20%). Almost none use the bridge's chords: the solo is the bridge, or sits next to it.

4. **The usual path is chorus, solo, verse or chorus:** the solo is the contrast after the second chorus, before the last cycle.

5. **The lead instrument follows the era:** saxophone and horns led early-1960s solos as often as guitar; guitar led about half from 1965 on; synthesizer took 14% in the early 1980s.

6. **Short instrumental links are everywhere:** a 4-bar link in nine of ten POP909 songs, and 8-bar interludes in Billboard. Songs 52, 54 and 55's 4-bar breaks fit this, not the solo.

## 4. What v8 should do

### S1. Two kinds of instrumental section

|Kind|What it is|Length|Where|Material|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|Link (interlude, turnaround, break)|A short instrumental passage between sections, usually playing the hook or the riff|2 to 8 bars (POP909 median 4; Billboard interludes 8)|Between a chorus and the next verse; may recur|The intro's figure, the hook, or a call and answer (songs 52, 54, 55)|
|Solo|An instrumental section that takes the song's middle|8 bars, sometimes 16; about 20 seconds|After the second chorus, starting at 0.45 to 0.65 of the song, leading into the last verse or chorus; may replace the bridge or follow it|Its own chords (half), the verse's (30%) or the chorus's (20%)|

The intake asks whether the song has a solo, defaulting by era and style: rare in an early1960s sound (about one in five); common in a 1970s or 1980s rock or pop-rock sound (a third to a half); uncommon now outside rock, country and worship. Songs without a solo still get links.

### S2. The solo's line

- **Instrument:** the style's lead (guitar in rock and country; saxophone in early-1960s pop, soul and R&B; synthesizer in 1980s pop; fiddle or steel in country; organ or piano in gospel).

- **Content, in three kinds:** a restatement of the hook or verse tune on the instrument (the safest, and what songs 52 to 55's links did), an elaboration (the tune with fills and runs), or a new line built on the section's scale, using 27.21's figure and riff tools.

- **Shape:** it rises to its peak in its second half and hands back to the voice with a fill (the band window's fill rules), so the voice's re-entry is the fresh moment of 749.19.

- **Intensity:** at or above the chorus before it (749.17's rising returns); the arrangement thins only if the song's plan puts a breakdown there instead.

### S3. The documents

The lead sheet prints the solo's chords and a written line (or slashes with "solo, 8 bars" when it's to be improvised); the parts give the soloist the line or the changes; the songbook says what the solo should do in a sentence.

### S4. Tests

- The form report prints each instrumental section's kind, length, place and material against these bands and the era's solo share.

- The solo's line passes the instrumental-line floors (27.21) and the perception checks (no masking of the voice, which is silent there).

- First targets: song 54 or 55 rerun with an 8-bar fiddle solo over the verse's chords after chorus 2, and song 56 with a saxophone or guitar solo over its own chords before the bridge.
