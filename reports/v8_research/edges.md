## Summary

- **v7 checks the edges but doesn't write them.** It has an intro-bar median (4), a chorusarrival limit, the peak-end design spec and four ending kinds (resolved, open, abrupt, return). Every intro and ending in songs 52 to 56 was chosen by hand, and all five intros are 4 bars.

- **Intro length is an era setting.** In 739 Billboard songs the intro doubled from about 9 seconds (1958–64) to 17 (1985–91), 4 bars to 8; Léveillé Gauvin found the time to the voice then fell to about 5 seconds by 2015. Neither length nor time to the voice separates hits from misses, so v7's single median of 4 bars is right only for a 1960s sound.

- **The first five seconds matter:** a quarter of Spotify plays end there. Whatever the era, something identifiable (hook tune, riff or voice) should sound in them.

- **Endings:** 61% of Billboard songs to 1991 fade (71% in the late 1970s); fades then nearly vanished. Cold endings land on the tonic 81% of the time, by V–I (a third), IV–I (nearly a third; Temperley's grand plagal cadence, which songs 54 and 55 used) or ♭VII–I (growing after 1980). A fade keeps listeners' pulse running past the end; a final slowing adds no pleasure.

- **What v8 should build (section 5):** six intro kinds (own figure, hook preview, verse vamp, buildup, voice first, none) with era bands; ending kinds that name the cadence and add the fade, the bookend and the coda; a fade only where the song shouldn't end; the demo rendering fades and held last chords; and bands for the commercial profile.

## 2. The academic record

Pop theory has studied song middles far more than edges. Nick Braae's 2026 Music Theory Online article on closure in 1970s rock opens by noting that intros have had some attention and endings much less. What exists:

### Intros

- **Summach's module theory** (dissertation, Yale 2012, top-20 rock 1955 to 1989) treats the intro as its own module. An intro often shares material with the outro, giving a "bookend"; several intro modules in a row are possible, and having none is less common than having more than one.

- **The buildup introduction** (Robin Attas, dissertation 2011 and later work; Nicole Biamonte in the Routledge Companion): layers of the groove enter one at a time, so the intro teaches the listener the beat and the band before the voice. Attas calls intros "a site for first impressions". Spicer's accumulative form (2004) is the largerscale version.

- **The attention economy.** Hubert Léveillé Gauvin (Musicae Scientiae, 2018) measured 303 US top-10 singles from 1986 to 2015: the time before the voice fell from about 20 seconds to about 5, tempo rose, and the title arrived sooner. A second test, 60 hits paired with less popular songs by the same artists, found none of these features separated the hit from the miss. Both halves agree with v7's Window 29: intro length tracks the era, not success.

### Endings

- **Kinds.** Teaching texts and Braae's model list the usual closing devices: a final tonic chord (often held), completion of the melody on the tonic, a ritardando, the fade-out, a coda of new material, and a modulation in the last section. Braae sorts them into structural closing markers (that make the music complete) and rhetorical ones (that signal "this is the end"), following David Heetderks's term "closing markers". Codas present new material; outros repeat old material with closing rhetoric (Open Music Theory, after Summach).

- **The grand plagal cadence.** Temperley (Music Theory Online, 2011) names a rock ending: a strongly emphasised IV to I, often with a held last chord, that comes once, near the end. Songs 54 and 55 ended this way by instinct; v7 has no name for it.

- **Terminally climactic form.** Osborn (2013) describes songs, mostly post-1990s alternative rock, whose last section is new material and the climax. Spicer's cumulative form ends at its fullest texture.

- **The fade-out's rise and fall.** William Weir's survey of Billboard's year-end top 10 since 1946 (reported by NPR, 2014) found fade-outs peaked in 1985, when all ten year-end songs faded, then declined through the 1990s until they almost vanished.

v7 already has resolved, open, abrupt and return endings. Missing from it: the fade, the held final chord, the slowing, the grand plagal cadence, the bookend, the buildup intro, and the coda of new material.

## 3. Listeners: the first seconds and the last

|Finding|Numbers|Source|
|---|---|---|
|Listeners decide fast|24% of Spotify plays skipped within 5 seconds, 29% within 10, 35% within 30; 48.6% before the song ends; skipping highest in teenagers|Paul Lamere, "The Skip" (Spotify data, billions of plays, 2014)|
|Hits got to the voice sooner|Time before the voice in US top-10 singles fell from about 20 s (mid-1980s) to about 5 s (2015); tempo rose; the title came sooner|Léveillé Gauvin, Musicae Scientiae 2018 (303 songs, 1986–2015)|
|But it doesn't pick the hit|Hits and less popular songs by the same artists did not differ on any of these features|Léveillé Gauvin 2018, study 2 (60 pairs, 2015)|
|A fade keeps the song going in the head|With a fade-out, listeners went on tapping the pulse after the sound stopped; with an arranged ("cold") end they stopped tapping before the end|Kopiez and colleagues (Hannover), "When the pulse of the song goes on", Psychology of Music (80 listeners, three versions of one song)|
|A final slowing doesn't add pleasure|Ordinary final ritardandi did not make endings more pleasant; exaggerated ones made them less pleasant; the closing harmony mattered more|Kleinsmith, Friedman and Neill 2016 and follow-up|
|Closure is expectation met|A cadence's last event is highly predictable, and predictability drops right after it, which is felt as an ending|Huron 2006; Sears, Caplin and McAdams|
|Phrase ends are lengthened|Performers, and even non-musicians pacing chords themselves, lengthen phrase ends|Palmer 1989; dwell-time studies|

### What this means for Luthier

- **The first five seconds matter on streaming.** A quarter of plays end there, before most intros finish. That argues for an intro that carries something identifiable (the hook's tune, a riff, the voice) rather than for a short intro as such, since length alone didn't separate hits from misses.

- **Era sets the length.** A song written to sound like 1975 can take 15 seconds before the voice; a song meant for streaming now wants the voice or the hook within about 5.

- **A fade is a meaning, not a default.** It leaves the pulse running: right for a song that shouldn't end (a vamp, an endless love, a nocturne), wrong for a song that resolves. v7 offers it only to the nocturne.

- **The ending is in the harmony.** The cadence carries closure more than a slowing does. A held last chord after a strong cadence is the reliable close; a ritardando is optional and should be slight.

1. **Fades were the majority ending from 1965 to 1991,** rising to 71% in the late 1970s. That agrees with Weir's year-end count peaking in 1985. The later collapse (section 2) is after this corpus.

## 5. What v8 should do

Keep v7's ending kinds, chorus-arrival check and commercial profile. Add a writer for each edge, with bands by era and style.

### E1. Six kinds of intro

|Kind|What it is|Evidence|
|---|---|---|
|Own figure|A riff or figure used nowhere else, or only in instrumental sections|55% own material, 27% shared with an instrumental section (Billboard)|
|Hook preview|The chorus's or hook's tune on an instrument|Songs 54 and 55's fiddle; 7% play the chorus's music|
|Verse vamp|The verse's chords and groove, the band settling in|11% play the verse's music|
|Buildup|Layers enter one at a time until the band is full|Attas; Spicer's accumulative form|
|Voice first|The voice alone or nearly alone: a pickup line, an a cappella phrase, a spoken word|8% of Billboard intros carry the voice|
|None|The voice on bar 1|7% of Billboard songs; common after 2000 (Léveillé Gauvin)|

The intake (or the style default) chooses one; the arrangement writer's layer counts (Part 19) fill it.

### E2. Intro length by era, and the first five seconds

- **Bands from the tables:** a 1958–69 sound, 4 bars and about 9 to 10 seconds before the voice; a 1975–91 sound, 8 bars and about 15 to 19 seconds; a streaming-era sound, the voice or the hook's tune within about 5 seconds.

- **The first five seconds carry something identifiable** : the hook's tune, the riff, or the voice. This is a check for every era, since a quarter of streaming plays end there and length alone doesn't make hits.

- Window 29's chorus-arrival bar should be read with the era's intro, not the cross-era median.

### E3. The ending's kinds

v7's resolved, open, abrupt and return stay. Make "resolved" say how, and add three:

|Kind|Built as|Use|
|---|---|---|
|Resolved: authentic|V–I, last chord held|The default before 1965 (half of cold endings)|
|Resolved: plagal|IV–I, once, emphasised, last chord held (Temperley's grand plagal cadence)|Rock, country, gospel, hymns; songs 54 and 55|
|Resolved: ♭VII–I|The Mixolydian close|Rock after 1980 (about one in ten)|
|Fade|The chorus or a vamp repeated, about 8 bars and 10 to 20 seconds, the level falling to nothing|Only where the song shouldn't end: a vamp, an ongoing state, the nocturne. The last words heard are the hook's|
|Bookend|The intro's material returns to close|13% of songs|
|Coda|New material at the end, possibly the climax (Osborn's terminally climactic form)|Alternative rock, worship builds|
|Open, abrupt, return|As in v7|As in v7|

- **Slowing:** optional and slight. Listeners find ordinary final ritardandi no more pleasant and exaggerated ones less pleasant; the cadence does the work.

- **Outro length:** cold outros of 4 to 15 bars (median 7, Billboard); POP909 median 4. A song ending straight on the last chorus is a pre-1965 sound.

### E4. The demo and the documents

- The demo renders a fade (a volume ramp over the fading bars) and a held last chord (a fermata, written in the score and rendered in the MIDI).

- The songbook says which ending it is and how to play it live: a fade needs a live ending, usually the hook once more and a held chord.

### E5. Tests

- The commercial profile (fix 71) gets bands: intro bars and seconds against the chosen era, the voice's entry, what sounds in the first five seconds, the ending's kind and last two chords, the outro's length.

- Songs 52 to 56 as the first test: all five have 4-bar intros, right for a 1960s sound and short for a 1975–91 sound; songs 54 and 55's IV–I endings match the grand plagal kind; none fades.
