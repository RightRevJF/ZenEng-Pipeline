# Plainness travels; the record keeps love

The science of musical preference says that lasting love has two different carriers, and it agrees with your studies about which is which. A tune survives being passed from person to person when it is plain: short phrases, small steps, few distinct elements, much repetition, low surprise. A recording stays loved when a listener's memory binds to that exact sound, above all to a sung voice standing clear in it, and when the record suits years of calm, half-attended, repeated listening. Neither carrier is strong. In every literature read here, what can be computed from the song explains about 5 to 10% of the outcome, while the listener's own history, the performer's standing, and luck explain most of the rest. The literature explains your null on written tunes among hits (every hit already sits inside the narrow band listeners accept), corroborates your plain-tunes finding with an independent Dutch folk-song study of the same sign and size, and gives indirect support to your "fewer events, less cheerful, less danceable" profile. Your concentrated-spectrum result and your same-song test appear to be new: nobody was found to have published either. The sharpest suggestions are that "sparse sound" may really be "voice left unmasked", that "pentatonic" may be partly an effect of being passed on and not only a cause, and that breadth and devotion are different kinds of lasting, fed by different listening.

**How sources are tagged.** The seven researchers hit access limits. Where a finding below comes from anything less than a full reading, it says so in brackets: *abstract only*, *second-hand* (known through another paper, a press release or slides), or *from memory* (unverified). Untagged findings come from full texts. Full texts were read through a tool that returns extracts, so wording is paraphrase.

## Song content explains 5 to 10%; history explains most of the rest

Start with the ceiling, because it sets how to read every number after it.

In the one large randomised experiment on musical success (MusicLab: 14,341 people, 48 unknown songs, eight separate "worlds"), a song's appeal constrained only the extremes. "The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible" ([Salganik et al. 2006](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/research/papers/years/2006/salganik2006a.pdf)). In a follow-up, the rank correlation between appeal and final popularity was **0.82 in an undisturbed world and 0.42 when early download counts were falsified** ([Salganik & Watts 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3785310)). "Appeal" there is a crowd's liking for a recording, not anything computed from notes.

On 852 million tweets, content features explained "well below 0.05" of the variance in how far a post spread, while the author's past success lifted it to **0.42 to 0.48**. Simulation put the realistic ceiling near 0.6 even with good knowledge of quality ([Martin et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1602.01013)). The split matches yours in kind: content near zero, the performer a third or more.

Music-specific tests agree. On 32,978 titles, neither audio nor 629 human-entered labels could predict high popularity better than chance (3 to 4% against 3%), though the same method learned labels like "nostalgic mood" well ([Pachet & Roy 2008](https://csl.sony.fr/wp-content/uploads/2008/01/pachet-08c.pdf)). On about 25,000 Hot 100 songs, eleven sound features plus controls explained **4.7% of peak chart position** ([Askin & Mauskapf 2017](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/25509/Mauskapf_productfeatures.pdf)). Hit-prediction papers report 60 to 75% accuracy, but on artificially balanced sets, and release year alone gives 72% in one of them ([Zangerle et al. 2019](https://dbis-web1.uibk.ac.at/sites/default/files/2019-12/zangerle_ismir_19.pdf)). Adding a flag for "artist charted in the last five years" lifts accuracy from 0.69–0.75 to 0.82–0.86 ([Interiano et al. 2018](https://escholarship.org/content/qt96b901t6/qt96b901t6_noSplash_929973e502e1e8745c9f9a35fae5ce8e.pdf)).

The listener's share is as large as the song's is small. In the one study that splits the variance of chills (goosebumps to music), **listeners alone account for 45% and tracks alone for 10%** ([de Fleurian 2022](https://remidefleurian.com/files/2022-dissertation.pdf)). In a recognition game with 973 players, all sound and tune features together explained 6 to 10% of how fast a song fragment is recognised, while the song's identity (its fame and airplay) explained about 40% ([Van Balen et al. 2015](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/27087957/148_Paper.pdf)). Across a century of number-one songs, the listener's age when the song was popular explained 62% of decade-level liking, and liking correlated **0.92 with simply recognising the song** ([Krumhansl 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00431/full)). A replication of the classic "music of your youth" result puts the preference peak near age 17, yet only 13% of individuals show a significant personal peak ([Marketing Letters replication, 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11002-022-09626-7)).

So your correlations of 0.04 to 0.25 are not small by this field's standards. They are what the field finds when it measures honestly.

## What listeners reward, stated as things a program can compute

### Predictable beats surprising; contrast beats level

The best-known claim, that pleasure peaks at a middling amount of surprise, holds up less well than its fame suggests.

Researchers measure surprise with *information content* (IC): how improbable a note or chord is under a statistical model of the style, in bits. *Entropy* is how uncertain the model was before the event. In the two model-based studies, the main effect of surprise on pleasure is **negative**. For chords from Billboard songs, the re-fitted model is pleasantness = −0.319·IC − 0.139·entropy − 0.118·IC×entropy, all in standard units ([Cheung et al. 2023](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3558121_1/component/file_3558256/Cheung_2023.pdf); the original 2019 paper was *not reached*). The researcher's arithmetic on those coefficients shows that surprise only stops hurting when the context is extremely certain. For melodies, liking = −0.21·IC − 0.09·IC² ([Gold et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867811/)), and the curved term failed to repeat in the same lab's later study ([Gold et al. 2023](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2023.1209398/full)). A 2025 study of 380 listeners found liking fell steadily as surprise rose, with no hump: "listeners consistently gravitate towards highly predictable pitches" ([Korkor et al. 2025](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/32249/4/FullText.pdf)).

When pitch and rhythm predictability were crossed, melodies predictable in both were rated most pleasant (109.9 against 70.0 on a 230-point scale), and rhythm surprise outweighed pitch surprise by 1.1 to 4.3 times ([Sauvé et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.03687)). The hump is real for one thing, syncopation (off-beat accent) in bare drum loops, where it explains 43% of pleasure ratings across 50 loops ([Witek et al. 2014](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/d9d000f1-421f-4984-8e28-d5dc02f2762e/PubMedCentral/d9d000f1-421f-4984-8e28-d5dc02f2762e.pdf)). On real drum patterns from records it nearly vanishes: syncopation explains 1.0% and listener taste plus familiarity 15% (*abstract only*: [Senn et al. 2018](https://folia.unifr.ch/global/documents/112592)).

Two details matter for an engine. First, a model that looks only one chord back predicted listeners' felt surprise better than a long-memory model, so cheap local statistics are enough ([Cheung et al. 2023](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3558121_1/component/file_3558256/Cheung_2023.pdf)). Second, the one corpus test on real hits found that what separated the top chart quartile from the bottom was a **contrast**, not a level. Context-aware chord surprise showed no difference at all. But top-quartile songs dropped more in chord surprise from verse into chorus: 2.15 against 0.95 bits, a large effect (d = 0.96; d is a difference in standard-deviation units) ([Miles et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5435755/)). That is chart peak, not lasting love, and it has not been replicated.

### Repetition wears in before it wears out, and how you listen decides which

In the laboratory, "rises then falls with over-exposure" appears only under close attention to realistic music in one sitting: liking peaks at about 2 to 8 hearings and is back to baseline, or below, by 32. The same music heard in the background while doing something else just keeps rising through 32 hearings ([Szpunar et al. 2004](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/Szpunar.pdf); [Schellenberg et al. 2008](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SP&V2008.pdf)). Sad-sounding pieces gained most from background listening. Fewer than half of individuals follow the group curve (37% rise-then-fall, 28% fall, 19% rise, 16% dip-then-rise), and listeners high in openness tire almost at once ([Hunter & Schellenberg 2011](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/HunterSchellenberg2011.pdf)).

Over a month of daily home listening (15 people, 40 pieces, 28 hearings), liking rose steadily at every level of complexity. Complexity had no significant effect (p = .494); familiarity with similar music mattered most ([Madison & Schiölde 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5374342)). One caution against "plain is always better": in that study low melodic complexity was what made pieces sound *dull*, and the most complex were liked most at every stage, though not significantly.

Streaming logs show the same two regimes. Most plays are replays: 69% on Last.fm, 72 to 89% in later Deezer and Last.fm sessions ([Benson et al. 2016](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~arb/papers/sequences-www2016.pdf); [Tran et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.16578v1)). For new releases, interest peaks at about 12 plays, but the whole curve moves only between about 0.76 and 0.80 probability of listening, and tracks on one album peaked at 18, 9, 8 and 14 plays ([Sguerra et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.16226)). The median listener-track pair is played once; the 99th percentile is 57 ([Sguerra et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02492)). Models that predict replays run on recency and frequency; content tags added nothing ([Reiter-Haas et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.02138v2)). Nobody was found to have tested audio features against long-run replaying.

The one study of songs people play hundreds of times is the most relevant in this whole section. Of 204 people asked about their most-replayed song, those who described it as bittersweet reported a mean of **790 lifetime plays, calm 515, happy and energetic 175**. Bittersweet-song listeners named beat or rhythm as the draw 19% of the time against 59% for happy songs, and 88% of them reported a deep connection against 57% ([Conrad et al. 2019](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jason-Corey/publication/322800023_Extreme_re-listening_Songs_people_love_and_continue_to_love/links/5aa5d25eaca272d448ba9cd4/Extreme-re-listening-Songs-people-love-and-continue-to-love.pdf)). It rests on 26 and 23 people in the two small groups, on self-report, and on no analysis of the audio. Independently, across a century of number ones, songs rated "energized" were ones people were *less* willing to hear more of ([Krumhansl 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00431/full)).

Repetition inside a piece helps too. Inserting literal repeats into difficult modern music raised enjoyment, interest and judged artistry (*abstract only*: [Margulis 2013](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/aesthetic-responses-to-repetition-in-unfamiliar-music/)). Margulis's account is that repetition lets a listener sing along inwardly, and that music, unlike speech, is remembered word-for-word at the surface ([Margulis 2013, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00167/pdf)).

### Peak moments are arrivals against what came just before

Chills cluster where something arrives: a voice or instrument enters, the level swells, the register widens, a soloist gives way to the full group. A review of 167 studies counts only loudness and change of texture as confirmed by experiment ([de Fleurian & Pearce 2021](https://www.marcus-pearce.com/assets/papers/deFleurianPearce2021b.pdf)).

The numbers an engine can use: a gradual 6-decibel rise over 8 seconds took validated chills from 11 to 29 in one piece. Making the same passage **brighter nearly abolished them (1), and darker doubled them (22)** ([Bannister 2020](https://etheses.dur.ac.uk/13582/1/Bannister_Thesis_2020.pdf)). Cutting the chill passage out of its context reduced chills, and passages with matching features elsewhere in the same piece did nothing ([Bannister & Eerola 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/pdf)). Scrambling music in quarter-second pieces cut chills sharply; what scrambling destroys is slow change in loudness below half a cycle per second ([Beier et al. 2020](https://memlab.bard.edu/files/2020/12/Beier20_Chills_AestheticsCreativityArts_preprint.pdf)). Pleasure starts climbing 6 seconds before a chill ([Salimpoor et al. 2009](https://wicri-demo.istex.fr/Wicri/Musique/explor/MozartV1/Site/fr/Pmc/Curation/bibRecord.php?hk=000798)).

Effects are small. On 1,019 tracks with 1,806 reported chill moments, the best classifier scored 0.597 where 0.5 is chance. Its top feature was melodic uncertainty measured against a folk-song model, ahead of every sound feature ([de Fleurian 2022](https://remidefleurian.com/files/2022-dissertation.pdf)). So the written line is not silent; it matters locally, at the moment of an arrival.

Chills are a marker of pleasure more than a cause of attachment. They track momentary pleasure at r = .71 to .74, come more in liked styles and familiar tracks, and did not fade over eight hearings ([Salimpoor et al. 2011](https://www.zlab.mcgill.ca/publications/docs/salimpoor_2011_nn.pdf); [de Fleurian 2022](https://remidefleurian.com/files/2022-dissertation.pdf)). No study has followed pieces for years to see whether chill-giving ones stay loved longer.

Two results bear on your "less cheerful" finding. Tracks cited for chills were less cheerful than matched tracks by the same artists, by 0.033 on Spotify's 0–1 valence scale, and sat lower on a combined energy, loudness, danceability and tempo factor ([de Fleurian & Pearce 2021, i-Perception](https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/0695c63ed1e5b482bf323b2bf1d15ab4/PMC8323431.pdf)). And in 308 listeners, liking for sad music correlated .69 with *being moved*; once being moved was in the model, sadness itself had no direct effect ([Vuoskoski & Eerola 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00439/pdf)). The active ingredient is "moving", not "sad".

### The sung voice is the memory hook, and the sound is stored with the tune

The largest effect anywhere in these notes is not a feature of melody. The same unfamiliar folk tune is remembered far better sung on "la" than played on piano, banjo or marimba: **d = 0.78 to 1.07 across three singers**, in trained and untrained listeners, and in children from age 7. The sung versions were liked *less* ([Weiss et al. 2012](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/WeissTrehubSchellenberg2012.pdf); [Weiss et al. 2017](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/WeissEtAl2017MP.pdf)). The voice works on attention and memory, not on prettiness.

Memory keeps the sound with the tune. A week after hearing a melody twice, listeners no longer noticed a change of key or tempo, but a change of instrument still hurt recognition, as much as on day one ([Schellenberg & Habashi 2015](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SchellenbergHabachi2015.pdf)). What is stored is "this tune in this sound".

In the recognition game, the strongest predictor of how fast a fragment is recognised was a component the authors call vocal prominence (+0.14 per standard deviation), then how *conventional* the timbre, melody and harmony are for the corpus (+0.05 to +0.09 each), then how repetitive the melody is (+0.12 on the 99 songs with transcriptions). "Recognizable sections are more typical than they stand out" ([Van Balen et al. 2015](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/27087957/148_Paper.pdf)). The "vocal" label was assigned by ear and never tested.

In a field study of who sings along in clubs, setting explained about 40% and musical variables about 25 points more, and the musical variables that counted were all in the vocal performance: high chest voice, effort, clear consonants, little ornament. The slide summary reads "It's the singer not the song!" (*second-hand*, authors' slides and abstract only: [Pawley & Müllensiefen](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/10171/1/Pawley_Mullensiefen_Singalong_2011.pdf)).

Earworms (tunes stuck in the head) add little. With chart success matched, melody features told 100 earworm tunes from 100 controls at 62.5% ([Jakubowski et al. 2017](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1371487)), and diary studies find stuck tunes "virtually unique to each individual" ([Beaman & Williams 2010](https://core.ac.uk/download/9534.pdf)).

A warning for any acoustic judge of a singer: 300 standard sound measurements explained under 5% of how much listeners liked a singing voice, while the listeners' own impressions (in tune, full, softly and precisely attacked, some vibrato) explained 43% ([Bruder et al. 2024](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3588032_1/component/file_3588033/Bruder_2024_PerceptualButNot.pdf)).

### Sound: activity in the low bands, level that breathes, parts that enter one at a time

Hits already share a spectrum. Across 772 number ones, energy falls about 5 decibels per octave from 100 Hz to 4 kHz, and later decades are flatter, with more deep bass and more top ([Pestana et al. 2013](https://joshreiss.github.io/documents/2013/Pestana%20Ma%20Reiss%20-%20AES135.pdf)). On 63 commercial tracks, *liking* went down as high-frequency extension went up, while judged *quality* went with dynamics; the two ratings were nearly independent across songs ([Wilson & Fazenda 2016](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1494893)).

For the urge to move, the best single sound measure was how much the loudness varies over time (r = .57 on 80 clips). Band by band, only activity below 200 Hz and between 400 and 1,600 Hz predicted groove; nothing above 1.6 kHz did. Playback level made no difference ([Stupacher et al. 2016](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2sw3r2dw/qt2sw3r2dw.pdf?t=nzi8vc)). Two or three parts grooved as well as four, and bringing instruments in one at a time beat starting together on enjoyment and on wanting to keep listening, with some of the largest effects in the groove literature ([Hurley et al. 2014](https://escholarship.org/content/qt06k2j4h8/qt06k2j4h8.pdf?t=plo4qb)). Loose timing does not help; exaggerated timing hurts ([Senn et al. 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01487/pdf)).

"Would dance to it" and "like to listen to it" are different targets. On 148 real songs, a modern learned audio model predicted the dance rating at R² = 0.59 but the listen rating at 0.35, and a hand-built feature set predicted listening at zero ([Marmoret et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.27237v1)).

On the charts, sounding less like the previous year's hits helps, modestly. A song one standard deviation less typical stays about half a week longer. But to get *onto* the chart at all, more typical is better ([Askin & Mauskapf 2017](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/25509/Mauskapf_productfeatures.pdf)). In the same models "energy" lowers both peak and weeks on chart, and valence does nothing.

### Passing a tune through many voices makes it plain

When random pitch sequences are sung from person to person for ten rounds, they converge on what folk tunes look like. Leaps wider than a fifth fall from **36.4% to 11.9%** of intervals. The set of distinct interval sizes shrinks. Arch shapes appear. Long sequences shrink to 6 or 7 notes. Copying error falls ([Anglada-Tort et al. 2023](https://research.gold.ac.uk/36541/1/Anglada-Tort_2023.pdf)).

The paper separates three causes. The *voice*: the same task done with a slider drifts toward octave leaps, not steps. *Memory*: a 10-second distraction between hearing and singing shrinks intervals by about 2 semitones more. *Liking*: when 415 listeners rated bare intervals, pleasantness peaked at the fourth, fifth and octave, not at steps, and a liking-only model cannot reproduce what singers do. So small steps are something throats and memories impose, not something ears ask for. What counts as plain is also local: American chains settled on fourths and fifths, Indian chains on seconds and minor thirds.

The same marks show up across the world's song. A survey of 304 recordings found, among 18 statistical universals: seven or fewer scale notes, small intervals, descending or arched shapes, short phrases, repeated motifs, few note lengths (*summary only*: [Savage et al. 2015, as quoted](https://mindblog.dericbownds.net/2015/07/universal-features-of-human-music.html)). A researcher tabulated the public Cantometrics file directly: of 5,776 songs, 69% span an octave or less, 46% end on their lowest note, and in 63% half or more of the text is repetition ([Cantometrics repository](https://github.com/theglobaljukebox/cantometrics)). Human song and birdsong share arches, small steps and long final notes, which points to breath-driven voices as the cause ([Tierney et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3174665)).

One corpus study measures survival directly, and it is the closest published neighbour to your session and hymn studies. In 147 Dutch tune families (9,639 phrases), a phrase is more likely to recur across variants if it is **shorter (−0.30), less surprising under a style model (−0.24), earlier in the tune (−0.10), more stepwise (+0.10) and more repeated (+0.09, +0.08)**. Together these explain 5% of the variance, 22% with the tune family known. The idea that surprising phrases survive better was rejected ([Janssen et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00621/pdf)).

Two cautions. Oral transmission itself removes the seventh scale note at cadences, pushing tunes toward pentatonic (*abstract only*: [Shanahan & Albrecht 2019](https://new-savanna.blogspot.com/2019/02/oral-transmission-of-folksongs.html)). And the "a leap is followed by a step back" rule is mostly an artefact of limited range, not a virtue to score ([von Hippel 2002](https://lbj.utexas.edu/sites/default/files/file/faculty/Expectations%20as%20learned%20heuristics.pdf)).

## What this means for the commissioning studies

### 1. Nothing in the written tune or chords separates lasting hits: explained

The literature predicts this null, for three reasons that stack.

*Range restriction.* Laboratory effects of predictability need stimuli spanning an Irish reel to atonal flute music (2.3 to 16.5 bits of mean surprise), and even then individual fits run from R² 0.001 to 0.54 ([Gold et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867811/)). Hits occupy a sliver of that span. The gate is on the way in: charting songs are slightly *more* typical than non-charting songs by the same artists ([Askin & Mauskapf 2017, Appendix A](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/25509/Mauskapf_productfeatures.pdf)). Your session-tune reading ("every hit already has them") is the same argument.

*No variance to work with.* Across 464,411 recordings over 55 years, the vocabulary of pitch patterns barely changes (year-to-year rank correlation 0.97) while timbre varies far more (0.57) ([Serrà et al. 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1205.5651)). A domain that hardly varies cannot grade much.

*Familiarity swamps structure.* With repeated hearing, complexity stops mattering ([Madison & Schiölde 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5374342)), and across 143,353 released pieces the relation between a machine "energy" rating and chart success had r² ≤ .002 ([North et al. 2017](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/64290/7/JCU_64290_North_et_al_2017_accepted.pdf)).

Two things sharpen it. Where score features carry any signal they are *relative to a corpus* (how typical the motifs are) or *contrasts between sections*, not whole-song levels. The verse-to-chorus surprise drop in Miles and colleagues is the one published chord result that separated chart tiers, and it deserves a run against your yardstick if it was not among the roughly 700 measures already tried. And the closest published counterpart to your melody null, a melody-only hit-prediction study titled "Is it the song and not the singer?", is recalled by one researcher as scoring only a few points above chance; it was *not retrieved* and is at the top of the chase list.

Nothing read contradicts this finding. The mild counter-signals (rare chords in verses, d = 0.29; rate of harmonic change helping a 59%-accurate chart model, [Lee & Lee 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1812.00551)) concern chart peak, not what lasts.

### 2. The recording's sound separates them, weakly: partly explained, partly new

**Concentrated spectrum.** No published study was found that measures spectral concentration or arrangement sparseness against liking, memory or survival. On the evidence reached, this result is yours. The neighbours point the same way: less high-frequency extension goes with more liking; darker climaxes give more chills; the bands that matter for groove are the same low and low-middle bands; "energy" shortens chart life. One researcher could not run searches on "sparseness" or "space in the mix" at all, so "new" is provisional.

The literature suggests what the measure may really be picking up, and each is testable:

- *An unmasked voice.* The voice is the memory hook (d ≈ 1) and vocal prominence is the top predictor of recognition. Lead vocals sat about 5 dB above the band in 1946 hits, fell to about 1 dB by 1975, and stayed there ([Gerdes & Siedenburg 2023](https://www.geidai.ac.jp/~marui/course_enshu_2024/gerdes_siedenburg_202304.pdf)). A spectrum with its upper bands left open is one where little competes with the singer. Your own result that the effect weakens after 1990 fits a period when spectra flattened and filled at both ends, though that is an inference, and your 1990–2005 reading rests on previews only.
- *Restrained drums and cymbals.* Prominent percussion is what puts energy at both extremes of the spectrum (*abstract only*: [Elowsson & Friberg 2017](https://aes2.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=18638)).
- *Easy processing.* A clear figure on a quiet ground is processed fluently and judged more pleasing ([Reber et al. 2004](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkielman/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf)). This is an analogy from vision, untested for sound.

You have already held era, tempo, genre, acousticness, energy and valence. The literature adds two controls you have not: vocal level and percussive share.

Your two McGill leads that failed on 60-second previews, "movement of energy over time" (+0.184) and "variation in beat loudness" (+0.171), are the measures this literature would have picked first. Loudness variability is the best groove correlate, slow swells are what make chills possible, and a preview cannot show a song's rise. They deserve a second test on whole recordings only.

**Fewer sound events per second.** Supported indirectly: lower activity in the spectrum goes with higher judged quality (R² up to .28 in one band, [Wilson & Fazenda 2016](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1494893)), and bittersweet most-replayed songs are rarely loved for their beat. The groove literature runs the other way, but only for the urge to move, and only by about 1% of variance on real patterns. Read together: density buys a little immediate motion and nothing shown for love.

**Unlike other hits of its years.** Askin and Mauskapf is the direct precedent, on weeks in the chart. You extend it to decades. Three sharpenings. Their relation is a hump whose peak lies three or more standard deviations below average typicality, so across the real range "less typical is better" holds, but a cap is the published form. Their effect vanishes in 1986–90 and 1992–96, so expect era dependence. And the recognition game finds *conventional* timbre is recognised faster. The compatible reading is conventional notes, distinctive sound, voice forward.

Your within-song test adds something the literature cannot: between two hit versions of one song, the less typical one was the more loved only 48.5% of the time. So distinctiveness marks which *songs-as-records* last, not which *version* wins. That fits a reading where being unlike your year is a trait of certain performers and projects, not a dial on a mix.

**Lower valence.** Not contradicted, and narrowed. Valence does nothing for chart peak or weeks, and charting songs are *happier* than the mass of releases ([Interiano et al. 2018](https://escholarship.org/content/qt96b901t6/qt96b901t6_noSplash_929973e502e1e8745c9f9a35fae5ce8e.pdf)). So cheerfulness helps a song in and does not help it stay. Chill-cited tracks, most-replayed favourites and "want to hear again" ratings all lean away from cheerful and energetic. Your mood study found the part of mood that predicts lasting is in the recording, not the page; the being-moved literature says the same in its own terms.

Two rival readings must be ruled out. Hit music as a whole has moved toward minor and slow (major-mode share of the Top 40 fell from 85% to 42.5% between the late 1960s and late 2000s, [Schellenberg & von Scheve 2012](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SchellenbergVonScheve2012.pdf)). So old low-valence songs may simply fit *today's* ear, in which case the finding would drift as taste drifts. It would also make "lower valence" and "unlike its years" one finding, since a sad song in a cheerful year is both. Enter them in one model. Second, Spotify's valence is an unvalidated machine guess; partial out tempo, energy and brightness before calling it sadness.

### 3. Lasting love does not transfer across recordings; the performer explains about 36%: explained in mechanism, untested in print

No published study runs your same-song test. Three lines explain why it comes out as it does.

*Memory stores the surface.* Timbre stays bound to a tune after key and tempo fade; music is remembered verbatim; heavy listeners can replay a whole track in their heads, more of it the more they have played it ([Conrad et al. 2019](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jason-Corey/publication/322800023_Extreme_re-listening_Songs_people_love_and_continue_to_love/links/5aa5d25eaca272d448ba9cd4/Extreme-re-listening-Songs-people-love-and-continue-to-love.pdf)). A cover does not match the stored trace.

*Exposure attaches to tracks.* Wear-in curves differ track by track on one album, and what a cohort recognises from its youth is presumably the recording that circulated, though no study has tested the bump version by version.

*Attention pools on one version.* In networks of cover versions built from audio similarity, the original tends to sit at the centre of its group ([Serrà et al. 2012, cover networks](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.6003)). Your slightly *negative* correlation (−0.09), and the earlier recording winning 67% of pairs, look like the rich-get-richer dynamics MusicLab produced on purpose: one version takes the love.

The performer share is in line with the field: past success supplies nearly all the predictable variance in Martin's data, the superstar flag adds 11 to 15 points of accuracy, and 0.1% of artists receive 41% of all cover versions ([Ortega 2021](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/f132/4479f4f0d56a4014d98322613582fdd56f11.pdf)). But note a split: artists with ten or more prior hits peak higher and leave the chart *sooner* ([Askin & Mauskapf 2017](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/25509/Mauskapf_productfeatures.pdf)). Launch power and staying power are different things, and your 36% is the second.

One tension. Before about 1960 songs plainly did survive across hundreds of recordings with no dominant one; covers per artist peaked in the 1950s and halved by the 2010s ([Ortega 2021](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/f132/4479f4f0d56a4014d98322613582fdd56f11.pdf)). Your yardstick has two halves, Spotify love and cover counts, and they may be measuring two different objects: the record and the composition. The same-song result may hold for the first and reverse for the second. Cover counts also change meaning at about 1960 and cluster by genre (73% of jazz covers are of jazz artists), so they need an era and genre adjustment.

### 4. Where there is no recording, plainer tunes last: corroborated, and sharpened in four ways

This is the best-supported of your findings. Janssen's Dutch study gives the same sign and the same size from a different tradition and a different definition of "kept". The singing chains give the mechanism. The universals show your plainness parts are the centre of world song.

*Add the two strongest published predictors.* Phrase length and mean surprise under a corpus-trained model lead Janssen's list, ahead of stepwise motion and repetition. Neither appears among the parts your write-up lists. Early phrases are also more stable, which argues for weighting the first strain.

*Plainness is transmissibility, not taste.* Steps are imposed by voice and memory; liking for bare intervals favours fourths and fifths. Listeners then come to like what is easy and frequent, through fluency and exposure. This answers your open question ("greatness or ease?") on present evidence: ease first, with liking following from it.

*Your Irish-over-hymn ordering (+0.12 against +0.04) fits a memory account.* Session tunes live in players' memories; hymn tunes live in printed books with harmony underneath, which lifts most of the memory load. This is the researcher's inference from your numbers, not a published result. A related prediction from the literature does *not* fit your data on its face: if small steps come from the voice, the penalty on leaps should be stronger in sung hymns than in fiddle and flute tunes. You found the reverse (leaps −0.043 in session tunes, +0.002 in hymns). Hymn incipits are only 15 to 20 notes without rhythm, so the test is weak, but as it stands it favours memory over throat as the reason leaps are lost.

*Pentatonic may be effect as well as cause.* If passing a tune along strips the seventh at cadences, then a much-played tune becomes more pentatonic because it is much played. Your strongest single part (pentatonic share, +0.116) is the one most exposed to this. The Session holds many settings per tune, which allows a direct check.

What contradicts it is about a different outcome. After one hearing of a new phrase, *rare* motifs raise "I've heard that" ratings, though partly as a guessing bias ([Müllensiefen & Halpern 2014](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/10838/1/PSY-Mu%CC%88llensiefen2014.pdf)). Over a month of chosen listening the most complex pieces were liked most. And when singers may *choose* which neighbour's melody to pass on, the melodies that evolve are reported as more complex and more pleasant than under plain copying (*abstract only*: [Marjieh et al. 2025](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/49332/galley/37293/download)). That last is the nearest experimental picture of the difference between an oral tradition and a market of records: copying simplifies; copying plus choice keeps some interest.

Your "ending off the tonic" result for session tunes (−0.055) is not a contradiction of world song ending low. Dance tunes are built to loop; ending on the way back round is function.

### 5. A scaling exponent near 1 characterises tunes but does not grade them: mirrored

The literature has the same shape of result. Melodic and rhythmic patterns follow power laws in every society sampled (*abstract only*: [Mehr et al. 2019, author summary](https://stevenpinker.com/publications/universality-and-diversity-human-song)), and tapped rhythms converge on small whole-number ratios in all 39 groups tested across 15 countries ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990)). These laws mark out the region where music lives. They do not rank points inside it. No published study was found testing a scaling exponent against liking or survival, so your null is also the only test.

### 6. On last.fm, sound predicts devotion better than breadth, but not a sparser spectrum: supported, with a useful split

The published nearest neighbour is Conrad's most-replayed songs, and it matches your signs closely: calm and bittersweet over energetic, beat rarely the draw, deep connection. Your "fewer events, less danceable, less happy, darker" is that profile in machine terms.

Your method is also ahead of the field. At artist level, depth rises with breadth on average and reach explains only a minority of loyalty (*abstract only*: [Šulik 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41270-026-00541-8)), so plays per listener must be read with listeners held, as you did. Replay models in print gain nothing from content tags, and no study was found regressing plays per listener on audio. A reader that finds +0.22 on unseen tracks is new evidence that sound does carry it.

The disagreement with the Hot 100 (devotion goes with a *fuller, more complex* spectrum) is what the listening-mode research would predict. There are two routes to lasting:

- **Lasting through breadth** runs on background, spaced, shared listening, where liking never turns down and plain, calm material gains most. That favours an open, concentrated sound that can be lived with.
- **Lasting through devotion** runs on chosen, attentive replaying by particular listeners. There, low complexity reads as dull, complex pieces hold up, and one theory separates *pleasure* (from ease) from *interest* (from mastering something that first resisted) (*summary only*: [Graf 2018 on the Pleasure-Interest Model](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00472/pdf)).

Both share slow, dark and unhurried; they part on how much there is to hear. Last.fm scrobblers of the 2000s are an attentive, enthusiast crowd, as you note. The split may be a fact about them, and that is testable on a broader log.

## Conclusion

The literature and your studies, read together, describe three judges, not one. The first asks whether a tune will survive being carried by ordinary voices and memories; it reads the page, and its answer is plainness. The second asks whether a recording will be recognised, bound to, and lived with; it reads the sound, and its answer is a clear voice in a calm, uncrowded, slowly breathing record. The third asks whether particular listeners will return hundreds of times; it also reads the sound, but wants more to hear. Merging them into one score would hide what each knows. And each is a gate more than a grade. Plainness makes survival possible and does not make it happen. After every computable measure, 80 to 95% of the outcome remains with history, function and association: who sang it, where, to whom, and what happened to them while they heard it.

That puts a songwriter's task in a useful light. The accounts of lasting attachment that survive in this literature are all about accumulated association: memory, being moved, music met at fourteen. On that view a tune's job is to be a durable carrier, easy to take up and hard to wear out, so that lives can attach to it. That can be designed for. The attaching cannot be computed, and the evidence here suggests no engine should claim to.

## The most promising next tests

Each is a measure a program can compute, with the data to run it on.

1. **Is "concentrated spectrum" really an unmasked voice?** After separating the lead vocal, compute its level minus the accompaniment's (A-weighted, one-second frames) and the share of time a voice sounds. Run on your 271 whole recordings and 3,472 arena recordings. Test whether concentrated spectrum survives with vocal level held.
2. **Or restrained drums?** Compute the ratio of percussive to harmonic energy on the same recordings, and hold it too.
3. **Swell and breath.** Compute the standard deviation of the loudness curve, the share of loudness change slower than 0.5 Hz, the largest rise over any 8-second window, and the count of part entries after a gap. First check that they mark reported chill moments in oChiM. Then test against your yardstick on whole recordings only, where your "movement of energy" lead reached +0.184.
4. **Add surprise and phrase length to the plainness reader.** Compute mean information content under an n-gram model trained on the same tradition (the melody-features package has one), mean phrase length in notes, and a version weighting the first strain. Run on the 23,205 session tunes and 10,360 hymn tunes. Replicate outside your data on the roughly 18,000 Dutch melodies with tune-family labels (Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.3551003), using family size as "kept".
5. **Throat or memory?** Split the reader into a production index (mean interval, share of leaps above a fifth, range) and a memory index (repetition, compressibility, count of distinct intervals, length, surprise). Compare each across session tunes and hymns.
6. **Pentatonic: cause or effect?** For Session tunes with many settings, compare the pentatonic share of the earliest setting with later ones, and set plainness against the alignment-based mutability in TheSessionEvo (Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.21356647).
7. **Verse-to-chorus surprise drop.** Compute −log2 of each chord's frequency, average by section, and take verse minus the following chorus. Run on your 553 McGill Billboard songs and 944 Hooktheory songs against the lasting yardstick.
8. **One finding or two?** Put valence and "unlike its years" in a single model on the 18,024 Hot 100 songs, with tempo, energy and brightness held, and with typicality in Askin's form (genre-weighted similarity to the previous 52 weeks, plus its square). Add "similarity to the hits of 10 to 20 years later" to test whether lasting songs sounded like the future.
9. **Record or composition?** Recompute the same-song correlation on your 745 works separately for the Spotify half and the covers half of the yardstick, and separately for works first recorded before and after 1960.
10. **Devotion in a broader crowd, and a fitted memory.** Compute plays per listener with reach held on MLHD+ or ListenBrainz joined to audio descriptors, or on Deezer REACTA or Yambda using their audio embeddings, and check whether "fuller spectrum" keeps its sign outside last.fm's 2000s scrobblers. Then fit Candia's two-rate decay to each song's yearly plays and use the fitted share passed into long-term memory as a yardstick that is a parameter, not a snapshot.
