# Every tradition reuses its rhythms; Luthier forgets them

The world's music sets low floors for form, not targets. In the one large coded sample, 304 recordings from the *Garland Encyclopedia*, **90% build at least a fifth of their notes from a recurring rhythmic pattern, 77% use eight or fewer distinct phrases before one repeats, and 81% keep phrases under 9 seconds**. Coders agreed poorly on exactly these form features (κ 0.13 to 0.26) ([Savage et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517223/)). A 16-bar section with 10 different bar rhythms clears every one of those floors, so the cross-cultural universals cannot say whether Luthier's 10 or pop 3/4's 6 is right. That is a style weight, to be read from the style tables as 749.233 already sets out. What the evidence does show, consistently, is four things. Rhythm repeats more than pitch in about 36,000 melodies (71% against 51% of a melody removable as repeats; that paper's corpus list still has to be checked for TheSession content). Rhythmic vocabularies are small everywhere (three duration ratios make 86% of rhythmic bigrams in 86 societies). The song-making and memory effects of repetition depend on exact, recurring frames. And sung phrases last about five seconds (4.5 s in Western song), so bars per line have to change with metre and tempo. Call and response is the most widely claimed form trait and the least measured: Savage 2015 never coded it, Mehr et al. 2019 coded it and then left it out of the analysis, and Lomax's scale separates simple from overlapping alternation, but no counts were read. **No published source counts distinct bar rhythms per 16-bar section for any tradition**, so the project's clip count of 6 stands alone. For the open problem itself, the two numbers in 749.259 are one statistic, and a plain multiplicative weight on a few windows among hundreds barely moves the share of repeated bars. Every expectation in this report is a hypothesis for the maintainer to test rule first. None is a result for the engine.

## How to read this report

Every claim carries one of three labels. **MEASURED** means a study or corpus count reports it. **THEORY ONLY** means a published model or description states it with no data behind that point. **OUR INFERENCE** means the researchers worked it out and no source states it. The researchers read almost every source through a web fetch tool that returns an extraction of the page, not the page itself, so it counts as a summarising tool. Every figure read that way should be checked against the original table before it goes into a rule file. Where a source was read only through an abstract, a catalogue record, a review or another paper's description, the text says so. The companion file `3_form_and_repetition.sources.json` records the reading route for every source. No data from TheSession.org was opened or used. No figure from the project's withdrawn studies (judging run 10, studies/session_lab, studies/gates_strengthened, studies/transfer, studies/next_tests t4) appears here. ## The universals set floors that a ten-rhythm section already clears

**MEASURED.** Savage, Brown, Sakai and Currie coded 32 CantoCore features on 304 *Garland* recordings: there were 305, and one made only of frog sounds was dropped. They then dropped 103 more for horizontal transmission, which left 201 indigenous recordings for the phylogenetic tests. Regions hold 14 to 54 recordings each. The paper reports 21 globally significant features, 18 of them called universal ([Savage et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517223/); read through the fetch tool, including SI Tables S3 and S5; check every figure below against the PDF). The form-relevant rows, with their coding thresholds from the SI classification scheme, are below.

| Feature | Coded present when | Frequency | κ | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Motivic patterns | at least 20% of notes built from a recurring rhythmic pattern | 0.902 | 0.172 | universal |
| Short phrases | phrases under 9 s (old classes <5 s and 5 to 9 s merged) | 0.811 | 0.260 | universal |
| Phrase repetition | eight or fewer different phrases before a repeat | 0.765 | 0.127 | significant, "narrowly failing" universal |
| Group performance | not solo (mixed, **alternating** and group singing merged) | 0.741 | 0.426 | universal |
| Few durational values | four or fewer duration values | 0.716 | −0.017 | universal |
| Isochronous beat | definition not extracted | 0.850 | 0.259 | universal |
| Two-beat subdivision | definition not extracted | 0.838 | 0.452 | universal |
| Metrical hierarchy | definition not extracted | 0.775 | 0.603 | universal |
| Syllabic singing | at most two consecutive notes without a new syllable | 0.509 | 0.578 | not universal |
| Dance accompaniment | definition not extracted | 0.325 | NA | not universal |

**MEASURED.** Two coders took part: Savage coded all 304 recordings and a second coder coded a random 30. Mean agreement over the 32 features was 80.8%, with mean κ 0.445, and the form features sit at the bottom of that range ([Savage et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517223/)). Phrase repetition missed universal status because its trend held "across all but one region". In the paper's co-occurrence network of 10 features, phrase repetition, motivic patterns, few durational values and isochronous beat cluster with group performance, percussion and dance, and the network is "centered on group performance and dance". Short phrases are not in it. **THEORY ONLY:** the authors propose that "simple, repetitive rhythms play a fundamental role in coordinating group performance" (same source). Trehub's commentary notes that repetition received "limited attention" ([Trehub 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517276); carried from prior notes, not re-checked).

**OUR INFERENCE.** These floors are cheap. A 3/4 section with 10 distinct bar rhythms in 16 bars still has 6 bars on a rhythm already sung, so it easily clears "20% of notes in a recurring rhythmic pattern". Savage's phrase repetition counts *distinct phrases before a repeat*. A 16-bar section of eight two-bar cells has at most eight distinct cells, so it passes that threshold by construction. The universals therefore say only that some motif must recur, and that repetition belongs to the sing-together, dance-together cluster. The informative quantity for Luthier is the same count at a stricter cut, read per style table. Given the κ values, a frequency like 0.765 indicates a direction and carries wide uncertainty.

**THEORY ONLY.** Two other cross-cultural schemes code form, though no frequencies were read for either. Brown and Jordania list 70 putative universals across pitch, rhythm, melodic structure, form, vocal style and context in a four-way typology. Only the abstract was read, not the list ([McMaster record of Brown & Jordania 2013](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/display/publication1425172)). **MEASURED (dataset description).** Lomax's Cantometrics, now in the Global Jukebox (5,776 songs from 1,026 societies, median four songs per society, overall mean κ 0.54), codes three form lines: Line 16, melodic form ("litany, strophe, or through-composed"); Line 17, length of a typical phrase; and Line 18, "Number of melodic phrases that occur in a song before a full repeat of the entire phrase sequence". The article gives no numeric scale points for these lines, and the reliability table for them (S12) was not read ([Wood et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9629617); read through the fetch tool). **THEORY ONLY.** Britannica opens its account of formal types with four general types and gives Western examples of strophic form, but no frequencies ([Britannica, Formal types](https://www.britannica.com/art/musical-form/Formal-types)). The four names (iterative, reverting, strophic, progressive) and their source in Nettl's *Theory and Method in Ethnomusicology* (1964) come only from search-result titles ([Nettl listing](https://es.scribd.com/doc/296752387/Nettl-Theory-and-Method-Ethnomusicology)). Nettl himself was not read.

**OUR INFERENCE.** Three variables recur across CantoCore, Cantometrics and the Natural History of Song, so they are the best-attested form variables: distinct phrases before a full repeat, form type (litany, strophic, through-composed), and phrase length. All three can be computed from notes. A pop verse and chorus is a strophic section with a returning chorus, which is Nettl's "reverting" form at the level of the section.

## The Natural History of Song codes repetition but finds function in accent, tempo and metric consistency

**MEASURED.** The NHS Discography holds 118 songs: four types (dance, lullaby, healing, love) from 30 regions, drawn from 86 societies. Penalised logistic regression on expert annotations and transcription features predicts song type from what the authors call "relational properties such as accent, meter, and interval structure". Dance against lullaby turns on accent (−0.64), tempo (−0.65) and macrometer consistency (−0.44). Dance against healing turns on tempo (−0.76), macrometer consistency (−0.46) and rhythmic variation (+0.42). Naive listeners (N = 29,357) identified function at 42.4% against 25% chance, with dance easiest (54.4%) and love hardest (26.2%). Rhythmic complexity separated dance (more complex, P = 0.01) from lullaby (less complex, P = 0.03), while melodic complexity did not predict type. Across the transcriptions, the ratios 1:1, 2:1 and 3:1 make up **86% of rhythmic bigrams**, and unison, major second and minor third make up 73% of melodic bigrams ([Mehr et al. 2019, author PDF](https://dcknox.github.io/files/MehrSinghKnoxEtAl_UniversalityDiversityHumanSong.pdf); read through the fetch tool, including the SI; check the coefficients).

**MEASURED (method and codebook).** Three expert musicians transcribed each recording blind to society, "with efforts to limit Western influence": **no time or key signatures, no beaming, no bar lines**. The expert codebook (Table S10) has small-scale and large-scale repetition (`repeat_small`, `repeat_large`), type of variation in repeats (`repeat_vary`), `call_response` ("if more than one singer"), number of singers, micrometer (duple, triple, both, neither), macrometer consistency (1 to 6, including "No macrometer but has clear phrases"), and indicator columns for macrometer cycles of 2 to 15 beats. Number of singers and call and response were **excluded from analysis as "contextual"**, and repetition appears only in the limitations discussion. No per-type counts of repetition, call and response or cycle length were found in the text read ([Mehr et al. 2019 SI](https://dcknox.github.io/files/MehrSinghKnoxEtAl_UniversalityDiversityHumanSong.pdf)).

**OUR INFERENCE.** The function signal sits in accent contrast, tempo and how consistently the bar-level cycle holds, not in any particular rhythm. Those three keep their meaning in any metre, so they are good candidates for style-level weights. The NHS transcriptions cannot be read for "bars per phrase" because they have no bar lines. The expert `macrometer_k` columns, however, give the nearest open measure of whether cycles of 2, 4, 8 and 16 beats dominate cycles of 3, 6 and 12 across cultures. The repetition and call-and-response columns are countable per song type and region, although the paper never reported them.

## Phrases last about five seconds, so bars per line must move with metre

**MEASURED.** In 35 solo a cappella *Garland* songs, with phrases defined as "periods of continuous vocalizing separated by breaths" and marked by ear by a blind assistant, 387 phrases averaged **5.3 s (SD 3.4)**: 6.2 s (3.8) in 19 non-Western songs and **4.5 s (2.8)** in 16 Western songs. No medians or ranges were reported ([Savage, Tierney & Patel 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/74204746.pdf); read through the fetch tool). Savage 2015's under-9-second feature is present in 0.811 of recordings ([Savage et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517223/)). Fabb timed 1,155 performed lines from 54 metrical poems: 59% ran over 3 s, 40% over 3.5 s and 26% over 4 s. He concludes that verse lines are not held to a time-limited capacity ([Fabb 2013, DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/ed0d479257764fe39a7e9033a1a5ed9e); read as abstract and summary page, so check the figures). **THEORY ONLY, with mixed evidence.** Pöppel's 3-second "now" is contested. A 2017 review found no sharp boundary at 3 s and argued for an integration window with no fixed limit ([BPS Research Digest on White 2017](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/new-review-punctures-myth-now-three-seconds-long); press summary only). **MEASURED (notes, not seconds).** In Essen subsets, German phrases run 2 to 21 notes and Chinese phrases 2 to 28, with no breakdown by metre ([Rodríguez-López et al., CMMR 2013](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/289643/MRodriguez-CMMR13-corrected.pdf?sequence=1); co-authors not confirmed). Phrase-final notes in Essen are about 1.58 times the mean note duration ([Tierney, Russo & Patel 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3174665); carried from prior notes, not re-checked). In Dutch oral transmission, shorter phrases are the most stable (β −0.30, the strongest predictor) ([Janssen et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5403935/)). Sung transmission chains converge on melodies of six to seven tones ([Anglada-Tort et al. 2023](https://research.gold.ac.uk/36541/1/Anglada-Tort_2023.pdf); prior notes, not re-checked). **No study found compares phrase duration between triple and duple songs, and none tests the four-bar phrase across cultures.**

**OUR INFERENCE (arithmetic).** Seconds per line = bars × counted beats per bar × 60 / BPM, where the counted beat is the quarter in 3/4 and the dotted quarter in 6/8.

| Metre and tempo | 2 bars | 3 bars | 4 bars |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4/4, quarter = 100 | 4.8 s | 7.2 s | 9.6 s |
| 3/4, quarter = 90 (slow waltz, hymn) | 4.0 s | 6.0 s | 8.0 s |
| 3/4, quarter = 120 (pop, moderate waltz) | 3.0 s | 4.5 s | 6.0 s |
| 3/4, quarter = 180 (fast waltz, one in a bar) | 2.0 s | 3.0 s | 4.0 s |
| 6/8, dotted quarter = 60 | 4.0 s | 6.0 s | 8.0 s |
| 6/8, dotted quarter = 80 | 3.0 s | 4.5 s | 6.0 s |

A two-bar 4/4 pop line (4.8 s) sits on the Western breath-group mean. A two-bar 3/4 line at pop or waltz tempo lasts only 2 to 3 s, about half a breath group. Matching seconds rather than bars puts a 3/4 line at three to four bars, which is where 749.226's density repair arrived by another route (every line three sung bars in a four-bar phrase, 2.09 notes a bar; [fixlist_v54.md, 749.226](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)). A moderate 6/8 line already reaches 4 s in two bars, so a padded fourth bar (8 s) approaches the 9-second edge. That is consistent with 749.226's 6/8 reading, where the padded bar overshot Essen's density and "bars from the table's density" was adopted. If the *Garland* durations were roughly normal (they cannot be exactly, since durations are positive and skewed), about 86% would fall under 9 s, which fits the 81% of recordings coded as short-phrase. A working band is a median line of 3.5 to 6.5 s with a cap near 9 s, near 4.5 s for pop and hymn and longer for folk and non-Western styles. The hypothesis to test: phrase seconds, not bars, is the metre-invariant variable, and bars per line = target seconds × BPM / (60 × counted beats per bar). "Four bars" is a notational unit whose length in seconds changes by a factor of two or more with metre and tempo. The NHS transcribers drew no bar lines at all.

## Rhythm repeats more than pitch wherever it has been counted, and nobody has counted bar rhythms per section

**MEASURED (via Huron's own summary).** Ollen and Huron sampled 50 recorded works, mostly international and world-music CDs plus a few Western genres, and excluded lyric-driven, improvised and through-composed works. They found that "94 percent of all musical passages longer than a few seconds in duration are repeated" somewhere in the work. The average repeated unit lasted 5 s under the strict criterion and 23 s under the broad one, and repetition was heavier in the first half of a work ([Huron 2013](https://core.ac.uk/download/161451256.pdf); read through the fetch tool). Huron dates the corpus study 2003, while a review of Margulis attributes the figure to Huron and Ollen 2004 ([Albrecht, MTO 20.4](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.14.20.4/mto.14.20.4.albrecht.php)), so the year should be checked against the original. **MEASURED (secondary, encyclopedic).** Kirby writes that "in the music of nonliterate cultures, simple iterative and strophic structures predominate". Indian music rests on reiterating a long rhythmic cycle (tāla), gamelan is marked off by regularly recurring colotomic entrances, and the Arabic bashraf is "a reverting type not unlike the Western rondo" ([Britannica, Non-Western forms](https://www.britannica.com/art/musical-form/Non-Western-forms)). No measured rates of timeline or tāla repetition were retrieved.

**MEASURED, flagged.** McBride, Kim, Nishikawa, Saadakeev, Pearce and Tlusty measured repetition in about 36,000 melodies by recursively removing repeated sub-sequences of two or more symbols. "Rhythm sequences have substantially more repetition (71 %, averaged over all melodies) than pitch sequences (51 %)", and pitch entropy exceeds rhythm entropy in 78% of corpora ([McBride et al. 2024, arXiv 2408.12635](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.12635v1); HTML and PDF read through the fetch tool, so check against the figures). **Flag: its corpus list was not checked for TheSession content.** Prior notes record that the paper uses an unnamed "database of Irish folk songs" whose provenance must be checked before any reuse ([prior note, expectation_reward.md](/home/claude/research_notes/Grammar%20of%20great%20music/expectation_reward.md)). The notes also disagree on the corpus count (113 here, 117 in the prior note). Until that is settled, the 71-against-51 figure is a flagged direction, not a number for a rule file. Whether the measure is corrected for chance was not confirmed. **OUR INFERENCE:** part of the gap may come from the smaller rhythm alphabet, so any Luthier comparison should be reported against a shuffled-bar baseline in the same metre.

**MEASURED.** Vocabularies are small. Mehr's 86% for three ratios holds across 86 societies (above). In 4/4 only, with onsets quantised to sixteenths in half-note units, the Public Domain Song Anthology uses **54 of 128 possible half-note patterns** while POP909 uses all 128. Real phrases have "a limited vocabulary compared to phrases assembled from random half-note units", and a Transformer's output had "a much higher rhythm vocabulary compared to real songs" ([Dai, Yu & Dannenberg 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.00182); read through the fetch tool). In POP909, more than 90% of songs have two or three distinct melodic phrases, and 20% of sections exactly repeat the previous section's phrases ([Dai, Zhang & Dannenberg 2020, as described in Dai et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.00182); carried from prior notes). Exact whole-phrase repetition, with the same onsets and the same pitches, is low in Dutch folk song: a mean of 1.17 occurrences per phrase in its own melody (SD 0.39, range 1 to 4). Repeating phrases are a little more stable in transmission (β 0.09), but fixed effects explain little (marginal R² 0.05) ([Janssen et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5403935/)). Among Dutch tune-family variants, "repeated patterns between melodies turned out to play the most important role for similarity" ([Ren et al. 2018](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~veltk101/publications/art/fma2018.pdf)). In lyrics alone, child-directed songs compress more than adult songs (ratio 0.322 against 0.362), with shorter and more regular redundancy spans, in three WEIRD cultures ([Labendzki, Goupil & Wass 2025](https://repository.uel.ac.uk/download/642ddc4f8d91ab005161a24d332491bf941b8b5cc64c24956fbe127022dad916/2152809/s44271-025-00219-4.pdf)).

**MEASURED (project counts, quoted as the open problem).** In a section's first 16 sung bars, the engine uses a median of 10 distinct bar rhythms (middle half 8 to 12), against 6 (4 to 8) in the Hooktheory pop 3/4 clips. Bars on a rhythm already sung are 0.375 against 0.625 ([749.259](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)). By whole song, the 538 lead-sheet waltzes have bars on a rhythm already sung at 0.706, 0.846 and 0.918 (10th, median, 90th) ([749.227](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)). Among 54 Discogs-joined songs, "rhythm again" is 0.55 for pop in three against 0.85 for the lead-sheet waltz ([749.233](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)).

**The gap, stated plainly.** No published study reports distinct bar rhythms per 16-bar section, or an exact two-bar rhythm repeat rate, for Essen, Meertens, Chinese, Indian, Arabic or African repertories. All the pop structure studies read kept only 4/4. No source breaks repetition down by metre, and none counts "same rhythm, different pitch" repeats directly.

**OUR INFERENCE.** Read together, McBride's rhythm-over-pitch gap and Janssen's low exact phrase repetition say that in folk song much of the repetition lives in rhythm while the pitches move. A section can have few repeated phrases and still reuse a handful of bar rhythms. Mehr's three ratios imply that a small bar-rhythm vocabulary is a cross-cultural invariant, not a pop habit. In 3/4 at eighth resolution there are 2⁵ = 32 onset patterns with an onset on the downbeat. If most ratios are 1:1 and 2:1, only a few of these are common, so six distinct bars in 16 is plausible across styles. POP909 using all 128 patterns across 909 songs, while PDSA uses 54, shows that a large corpus-wide vocabulary is compatible with a small per-section vocabulary. The engine's draw from "hundreds" of windows per line matches the corpus, not the section. The whole-song waltz figures cannot be turned into a 16-bar distinct count, because a whole song spans more bars and repeats more than a section does, as 749.258 found for the old charge.

## Call and response is claimed widely and measured nowhere in these corpora

**MEASURED (absence).** Savage 2015 has no call-and-response feature, because "alternating" was folded into group performance (0.741, κ 0.426) ([Savage et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517223/)). The NHS coded `call_response` and then excluded it from analysis ([Mehr et al. 2019 SI](https://dcknox.github.io/files/MehrSinghKnoxEtAl_UniversalityDiversityHumanSong.pdf)). **THEORY ONLY (coding scheme with regional claims, no counts on the page).** Cantometrics Line 1 is the only scale found that separates simple from overlapping alternation. It codes simple leader-group (6), simple group-group (7), overlapping leader-group (8), overlapping group-group (9) and interlock (10). It calls overlap "a leading trait of speaking and singing in sub-Saharan Africa", illustrates interlock with the Mbuti, and illustrates simple leader-group alternation with a Hebridean waulking song and a French Canadian work song. Overlap examples come from the Andros Islands and a Wolof praise song ([Global Jukebox, Line 1](https://theglobaljukebox.org/data/songsofearth/canto_line01.html)). **THEORY ONLY (secondary historical description).** In lining out, a clerk or precentor gives each hymn line before the congregation sings it "slow, drawn-out" and "often profusely ornamented". The practice was outlined by the Westminster Assembly in 1644 and survives in Gaelic psalmody, among Primitive and Old Regular Baptists in the southern Appalachians, and in many African American congregations ([Wikipedia, Lining out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lining_out)).

**The gap, stated plainly.** No source read gives a measured prevalence of call and response by region, a ratio of call length to response length, or overlap timing for any tradition. Sea-shanty and work-song structure was not read, because the fetches needed approval. Nketia and Agawu were not reached. Prevalence is countable from NHS `call_response` and Global Jukebox Line 1, but both data licences are unconfirmed.

**OUR INFERENCE.** For a notes-only engine, call and response is a form fact before it is a performance fact: a fixed short unit (the response) recurs after each changing unit (the call). In Luthier's terms the response is a copy with a fixed rhythm, so it falls under 749.228 (the join before a copy) and 749.234 (lines that share a tune share their bars), and it is the strongest case for exact rhythmic repetition. In lining out, the response carries the call's own words, sung slower and longer, so the ratio of response to call exceeds 1. In refrain-type work songs it is presumably below 1. Neither ratio is measured. Overlap cannot be represented in one melodic line. In a lead sheet it would show only as a call ending on a weak position with the response entering on the beat.

## Repetition helps memory and singing along only when the frame recurs exactly

**MEASURED.** In the speech-to-song illusion, a spoken phrase heard ten times "moved solidly from speech to song" when repeated exactly. Transposed repeats "moved slightly towards song" but stayed in the speech region, and repeats with the syllables reordered produced "no transformation". After ten exact repetitions, 11 choir-experienced women reproduced the phrase as song, while 11 who heard it once reproduced it as speech ([Deutsch lab page on Deutsch, Henthorn & Lapidis 2011](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/psychology/pages.php?i=212); group sizes for the rating experiment are not on the page). Phrases that transform have steadier pitch within syllables (27.6 against 40 semitones per second, P < 0.0001), while the regularity of stressed-syllable timing was only a trend (P = 0.17) ([Tierney et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3539450)). Native speakers of tonal languages experienced "significantly weaker speech-to-song effects" ([Jaisin et al. 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00662/text); abstract only, check the figures). Random tone sequences looped six times were later rated more musical, though "the effect size was small" ([Margulis & Simchy-Gross 2016](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/repetition-enhances-the-musicality-of-randomly-generated-tone-seq/); abstract only). In Margulis's repeated-listening tasks, listeners found short repeats early and long ones only after many hearings ([Albrecht review of *On Repeat*](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.14.20.4/mto.14.20.4.albrecht.php); review only, no sample sizes). **THEORY ONLY.** Huron's habituation-fluency theory has repetition raising fluency while habituation dulls response, with form balancing the two ([Huron 2013](https://core.ac.uk/download/161451256.pdf)).

**MEASURED (via another paper's description; Wallace 1994 not read; check the figures).** Wallace played three verses of unfamiliar ballads. One melody repeated across all verses gave the best verbatim recall, and a different melody per verse was "less effective than hearing a spoken version" ([Ludke, Ferreira & Overy 2014, describing Wallace 1994](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Ludke-et-al.-2014_SingingForeignLanguage_Mem-Cog.pdf)). **MEASURED.** With songs chosen for few repetitions, singing did not help lyric recall (immediate recall: sung-sung 60.0%, sung-spoken 70.9%). The authors conclude that "musical constraints appear to be of limited help for lyric recall in general" ([Racette & Peretz 2007](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03193445.pdf)). Singing did help 60 adults learn Hungarian phrases over speaking and rhythmic speaking (production 4.0 against 1.9 and 2.7 of 20; d = 0.75 against speaking) ([Ludke et al. 2014](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Ludke-et-al.-2014_SingingForeignLanguage_Mem-Cog.pdf)). Rubin found that variants of counting-out rhymes "always maintain the rhythmic, poetic, and other forms of organization". When five traditional singers sang a ballad twice about six months apart, "the melody seemed more stable than the words", and beats per line and lines per verse stayed about constant ([Rubin 2009](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/d382fa40-6737-46bc-a30b-de6d9a57aa3e/content); book record [Rubin 1995](https://academic.oup.com/book/53811)). **THEORY ONLY (abstract).** A 1991 *Discourse Processes* paper on North Carolina ballads argues that refrains, rhyme, metre, the "agreement of metrical stresses with musical beats" and repeated lines together limit word choices ([DukeSpace record](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/3f395fed-4458-4ad3-a246-8eefafa49a33/full); attribution to check, likely Wallace and Rubin). In English and Japanese folk variants, metrically strong notes mutate least ([Savage et al. 2022](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/sequence-alignment-of-folk-song-melodies-reveals-cross-cultural-r/); prior notes, not re-checked).

**MEASURED (review).** Earworms are most often "the chorus or refrain of popular music pieces", fragments of 10 to 30 s, and their commonest trigger is "recent and repeated exposure" ([Liptak, Omigie & Floridou 2022](https://research.gold.ac.uk/30547/16/mp.2022.39.3.320.pdf)). **THEORY ONLY.** Savage et al. 2021 argue that "repetition enhances memorization and predictability", letting many performers coordinate, and that metre lets many individuals contribute "out of phase, to the same integrated rhythm". **MEASURED (cited there; primary studies not read).** Meta-analyses find that synchronising with others raises prosocial behaviour, though they are inconclusive on the specific roles of music and isochrony ([Savage et al. 2021](https://core.ac.uk/download/373915152.pdf)). **No study found measures how many exact bar-rhythm repeats a section needs before an untrained group can sing along**, and the bonding evidence does not separate repetition from isochrony.

**OUR INFERENCE.** The mechanism these studies share is a reusable frame, and a frame exists only if it recurs exactly. A section that changes its bar rhythm on most lines is closer to Wallace's "different melody per verse" condition, the one that did worse than speech. It is also closer to Deutsch's near repeats, which carried only a fraction of the effect. Margulis's short-before-long result suggests that listeners lock onto bar-level and two-bar repeats first, so a section that repeats only whole lines gives the ear nothing to grab early. Rubin's singers kept beats per line and melody while words drifted, which is the split 749.240, 749.251 and 749.252 need: hold the strong-beat skeleton and adjust the syllables. Jaisin's result fits the "one language, many dialects" shape. The mechanism is shared, and its weight differs by language or style.

## What the evidence says about ten rhythms against six

**OUR INFERENCE (arithmetic).** The two figures in 749.259 are one statistic. Over a fixed window of 16 bars with D distinct bar rhythms, each rhythm's first bar is new and every other bar is on a rhythm already sung, so the share already sung = (16 − D)/16. D = 10 gives 6/16 = 0.375, and D = 6 gives 10/16 = 0.625, exactly the two reported shares. The map is monotone, so medians and quartiles carry across: the engine's middle half of 8 to 12 is 0.25 to 0.50, and the clips' 4 to 8 is 0.50 to 0.75. Any fix that moves one figure moves the other by construction, so the pair cannot serve as two independent checks.

**OUR INFERENCE.** The cross-cultural universals cannot choose between 10 and 6, because both clear every floor. The project's own tables already disagree by style: pop in three repeats rhythm far less than the lead-sheet waltz (0.55 against 0.85 by song). The target is therefore a style weight read from the table the lyric's genre names (749.233). What the world's music does supply is the direction and the shape. Rhythm repeats at least as much as pitch, vocabularies per section are small, and the effects of repetition depend on exact recurrence. Luthier currently has the reverse: per 749.259, pitch has a memory of figures already sung (923) and rhythm has none. A cross-cultural target would have rhythm repetition at or above pitch repetition in every metre, with only the size of the gap varying by style. A different variable per culture is not needed.

**OUR INFERENCE.** 749.257's withdrawn reading narrows where the shortfall lives. Lines sung again already keep their source's rhythm (0.935 with the same syllable count; 0.968 and 0.988 with one more or fewer), but only 0.294 of lines sung again have their source's syllable count. In 749.259 the lyric made no difference (songs 49 and 55 both give 10), and singing again across sections cannot raise a figure read within one ([fixlist_v54.md](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)). That leaves the fresh lines, each drawing its own window. In folk song, by the reading above, rhythm recurs across lines whose words and pitches differ, which is exactly the repetition a fresh-line draw with no section memory cannot produce.

**OUR INFERENCE (mixture-weight reasoning).** Suppose a line chooses among N candidate windows, k of which are made of bars already sung in the section, and those k get a multiplicative weight w. Then P(reuse) = k·w / (k·w + N − k). With N in the hundreds (749.259's "hundreds") and k small, a weight of 2 to 5 barely moves anything: for N = 300 and k = 3, w = 2 gives 6/303 ≈ 0.02 and w = 5 gives 15/312 ≈ 0.05. Reaching a reuse probability near 0.625 needs w = 0.625·(N − k) / (0.375·k), which is about **165** for N = 300 and k = 3, so roughly of the order N/k. A mixture form sets the share directly: P(window) = λ·P_section(window) + (1 − λ)·P_corpus(window), with λ the probability of drawing from material already sung. The prior report's 0.7 weight on a song's own material for pitch is the same order of strength, applied as a mixture probability, not a multiplier ([Dai, Yu & Dannenberg 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.00182)). Two caveats apply. This calculation is about a window's draw, not the bar share itself, and 0.375 of bars already repeat with no weight. The researchers also did not read the engine's actual N, k or weight form. The point is the order of magnitude: a small weight will look inert, and that is a property of the arithmetic, not evidence that section memory does not matter.

**OUR INFERENCE.** Any target must be held per metre and against chance. A 3/4 bar and a 6/8 bar both have six eighth slots, so differences between them reflect style and grouping, not grid size. A 4/4 bar has eight, so chance alone makes repeats commoner in threes than in 4/4. This corrects one candidate idea in the notes, which expected 6/8 to use more bar rhythms than 3/4 because of grid size. The honest comparison is each corpus's D against its own shuffled-bar baseline.

## Conclusion

The cross-cultural record changes the open question. It is no longer "is 10 too many?" but "what kind of memory does a section need?" Every tradition that has been counted keeps a small rhythmic vocabulary and recycles it more readily than its pitches, while the universal thresholds stay loose enough that a pop 3/4 table, a waltz table and a folk table can sit far apart above them. The engine's gap is therefore a missing variable (section-level rhythm memory), not a mis-set weight on an existing one. Because the two 749.259 figures are a single statistic, a fix needs one independent check beside them, such as the lag profile of 749.257, rhythm-minus-pitch repetition, or the share of bars covered by the top two rhythms.

The second change concerns units. The only form universal that crosses metres without conversion is in seconds, not bars. Bars per line, the padded fourth bar and the "four-bar phrase" all look like consequences of tempo and counted beats rather than primitives. If the seconds hypothesis survives a count on Essen phrase marks and the clips, 749.226's bar choice becomes a formula rather than a per-metre table. The largest unmeasured territory is call and response: three major corpora coded it, and none reported a count. For a songwriting engine it is the clearest case of a unit that must repeat exactly.
