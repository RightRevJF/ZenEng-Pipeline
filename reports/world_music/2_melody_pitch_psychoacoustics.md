# Luthier's waltzes recite where folk songs travel

Wherever melody has been counted, a sung tune moves mostly by small steps over a set of five to seven pitches. On average it arches and falls. It carries its tonal centre more by time and position than by raw note count, and a whole song spans about an octave even though one phrase spans only about nine semitones. Against that background the engine's 3/4 tunes look like recitation, not dance song. They have a range of 9 semitones, 6 pitches, and 0.39 to 0.42 of their notes on the commonest pitch. In the literature, the traditions that sing text on one pitch over a narrow range are chant, parlando and litany, and they are where that profile belongs. Two findings are firm enough to act on. First, the engine's whole-tune range equals one folk phrase. That puts the deficit in how phrases move between registers, not in how far any one phrase spans, and it fits the project's form B, which moved section centres and met range and commonest pitch together. Second, pitch-class entropy is too blunt to catch concentration on one pitch. Two questions the evidence cannot answer. No study in any culture publishes a per-song distribution of commonest-pitch share. No study compares contour, range, interval sizes or pitch concentration between triple and duple metre. So every 3/4 ceiling has to be counted on the project's own data. The consonance work on the Tsimane' bears on melody only weakly. Aversion to roughness and the fusion of harmonic intervals look universal, liking consonance looks learned, and the traces in melody are only the over-use of the fourth and fifth and the rarity of the tritone. Nothing in the world evidence predicts that a 3/4 tune should be narrower or more concentrated than a 4/4 one. The fixlist already shows that the 3/4 path lacks the register plan and the commonest-pitch ceiling that 4/4 has. None of this is a result for the engine. The TESTABLE IDEAS table at the end lists what the maintainer can test, rule first.

Where a source was read only through an abstract, a press release, a secondary source or a summarising web tool, the citation says so, and its exact figures should be checked against the original before they are hard-coded. ## Pitch materials: five to seven notes, steps near 200 cents, consonance learned

### World scales cluster at 5 to 7 notes, and no source gives a pentatonic share

[MEASURED] The largest open scale database, DaMuSc, holds **896 octave scales from 73 societies**, drawn from 60 books and journals: 434 theory scales, 384 instrument tunings and 78 song recordings. A tonic was identified for only 121 of the 434 measured scales ([McBride & Tlusty 2023, ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.00842)). [MEASURED] "Most scales are found to have 7 or fewer notes," and "6-note scales are remarkably rare across all sampling schemes." The paper gives no percentages by scale size. Steps "are restricted to 100 – 400 cents," peak near 200 cents and rarely fall below 100 ([same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.00842)). [MEASURED] Notes **500 and 700 cents above the tonic are the most common**. The 1,200, 700, 200 and 2,400 cent notes occur more often than chance, **600 cents less often than chance**, and 270, 450, 550, 650 and 930 cents are rare. About **70% of real scales lie near equidistant 5- or 7-note scales**, against 10% of grid scales, and humans use about 1% of possible octave scales. Carnatic scales alone are "almost as varied as the total set." Of the 60 sources, 46 support octave significance and 3 contradict it ([same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.00842)). [THEORY ONLY] The paper lists candidate explanations (harmonicity, sensory dissonance, tonal fusion, transmissibility, memory, mathematical properties) but tests none of them.

[MEASURED] Savage and colleagues' statistical universals include "non-equidistant scales," "≤7 scale degrees," "discrete pitches," "small intervals" and "descending/arched contour." None of them is an absolute universal ([HRAF summary](https://hraf.yale.edu/hypotheses/3627); [Savage et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517223/), read only through secondary sources this pass). [MEASURED, press summary] "Five-note ('pentatonic') scales were not universal" ([Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/music-just-about-everyone%E2%80%99s-ears)). Three conflicts in the secondary sources need the original. Harrison gives the sample as 304 recordings coded for 32 features ([Harrison, ch. 13](https://pmcharrison.github.io/intro-to-music-and-science/music-across-the-world.html)) while Science News reports 201. The HRAF page says 21 features but lists 18. And the per-feature prevalence figures were not seen this pass; 304 is the better-supported count. [OUR INFERENCE] Savage's "non-equidistant" and DaMuSc's "near equidistant" fit together if "non-equidistant" means "not exactly equal."

**No primary source gives the share of the world's scales, or of its societies, that are pentatonic, or anhemitonic against hemitonic.** The often-quoted "perhaps 90%" anhemitonic figure is one author's estimate (Keith 1991) repeated on Wikipedia, and the claim that the major pentatonic is "the most commonly used scale in the world" has no source there ([Wikipedia, Anhemitonic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anhemitonic_scale)). Neither is usable as a measured figure. [MEASURED, reference-work level] Japanese tradition sets the anhemitonic *yo* scale against the hemitonic *in* scale ([same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anhemitonic_scale)). [MEASURED, secondary] Slendro divides the octave into five roughly equal steps, and pelog tuning varies widely between gamelans ([Harrison, ch. 13](https://pmcharrison.github.io/intro-to-music-and-science/music-across-the-world.html)). DaMuSc could be counted for a pentatonic share (5-note scales with no step under about 150 cents), but its licence is unconfirmed.

[THEORY ONLY] Gill & Purves ranked about 455,126 pentatonic and about 45 million heptatonic candidate scales by mean harmonic-series similarity of their dyads, scoring each interval x:y as ((x+y−1)/(x·y))·100. Their top pentatonic is the **minor pentatonic**, then Ritusen. The top heptatonic modes are Phrygian, Dorian and Ionian, then Husayni. The paper reports no correlation or test against practice, it admits "it is less clear why tone collections smaller than five notes are not preferred," and it excludes inharmonic instruments such as gamelan ([Gill & Purves 2009, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2779864)). [MEASURED, abstract only] A 1,314-scale study covering 96 countries finds "near-universal support for melodic theories, which predict step-sizes of 1-3 semitones." In the same study harmony explains simple-ratio intervals "mainly in music-theoretic scales from Eurasian societies" and is "a poor predictor of scales measured from ethnographic recordings, particularly outside of Eurasia" ([McBride et al. 2024a, arXiv 2408.12633](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12633); check figures). [MEASURED] Across 62 folk, 39 art and 7 child corpora (about 36,000 melodies), "melodies tend to use a small pitch alphabet, with typically 7 or fewer notes." A parameter-free information model reproduces the frequency of each scale size at r = 0.99 ([McBride et al. 2024b, arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.12635v2), read through a summary; [TheSession-flagged]). The paper's own corpus counts disagree: version 1 says 117 in its introduction while its listed groups sum to 111, and the later version says 113 ([v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.12635v1)).

[OUR INFERENCE] The pitch-set constants an engine can carry into any culture or metre are these: 5 to 7 pitch classes per octave, rarely 6; steps mostly 1 to 3 semitones; a fourth and fifth above the centre present and favoured; the tritone relation to the centre avoided. Pentatonic against heptatonic is a dialect choice. For a notes-only engine, step-size and memory limits travel better than harmonic-similarity rankings, because the latter fit Eurasian theory scales and fit ethnographic recordings poorly. And 6 pitch classes is not narrow by world standards. The 749.227 deficit is octave-specific pitches (6 against the waltzes' 10th percentile of 8), which is a problem of range, not of alphabet.

### Small intervals carry three quarters of melody, and unisons hide inside that share

[MEASURED] In the Natural History of Song (NHS) Discography, "three small intervals (unison, major second, and minor third) accounted for **73%** of the bigrams," and "the tritone (six semitones) was the rarest, accounting for only **0.2%**." The fall-off fits a power law with median R² 0.97 ([Mehr et al. 2019, author PDF](https://dcknox.github.io/files/MehrSinghKnoxEtAl_UniversalityDiversityHumanSong.pdf)). These distributions are pooled over cultures, not computed per culture ([Harrison, ch. 13](https://pmcharrison.github.io/intro-to-music-and-science/music-across-the-world.html)). [MEASURED] McBride et al. find small intervals dominant "consistently in every society" across 62 folk corpora, without giving a step share ([arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.12635v2); [TheSession-flagged]).

[MEASURED] Transmission shrinks intervals further. In iterated singing (1,797 participants, 12 experiments, US and India), intervals above 7 semitones fell from **36.38% to 11.90%**. Mean absolute interval ended at **3.25 semitones in India against 5.71 in the US**, social-transmission attractors sat near −6.94 and +4.33 semitones (US) and −2.90 and +2.47 (India), and "just 5 s of memory interference caused a substantial shift toward melodies with smaller intervals." Copying by voice pushed toward small intervals and copying by slider toward large ones ([Anglada-Tort et al. 2023, Goldsmiths PDF](https://research.gold.ac.uk/36541/1/Anglada-Tort_2023.pdf)). Correction: the abstract's 3,424 melodies was not confirmed in the body, and the paper gives final melody length as "6 to 7 tones" in its Results and "5–6 tones" in its Discussion. It reports no range or pitch count. [MEASURED, secondary] Tsimane' "song melodies are characterized by small intervals, often approximately two or three semitones, and a narrow vocal range" (Riester 1978, cited in [Jacoby et al. 2019](https://piv.medicina.uc.cl/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Jacoby_et_al_2019.pdf)).

[MEASURED, secondary] The **low-skip bias**: skips larger than a major second come more often in the lower part of a melody's range. The bias is strongest in vocal music, which the authors read as low register offsetting the vocal cost of a leap (Ammirante & Russo 2015, via [Harrison, ch. 13](https://pmcharrison.github.io/intro-to-music-and-science/music-across-the-world.html); original figures not read). [MEASURED] A replication on 299 jazz solos found a skip rate of 41.4% that fell with register quintile (Spearman ρ(298) = **−.59**). The bias was much stronger for ascending skips ("jump up and trickle down"), with no U-shape ([Jazzomat tutorial](https://jazzomat.hfm-weimar.de/tutorials/klaus/tutorial_low_skip.html)). [THEORY ONLY] Ammirante & Russo 2023 propose a "vocal constraints model of melodic expectancy" (title and opening only read; [record](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/doaj.art-9d1b44267b2f43728ad070d1380a3e69)).

Vos & Troost 1989, with its well-known claim that large intervals tend to ascend and small ones descend, and Huron's cross-cultural interval tables were **not read**. One correspondent on a 2007 mailing list could not reproduce the Vos & Troost distribution ([auditory.org](https://auditory.org/postings/2007/594.html)). Eerola cites both papers for "small intervals predominate" but gives no numbers ([Eerola 2003 thesis](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstream/123456789/13428/1/9513916553.pdf)). The asymmetry should not be built in until the paper is read.

[OUR INFERENCE] What is universal is the *shape*: probability falls steadily as interval size grows. The *scale* of that fall (mean interval, share of leaps) is a dialect weight, as the India and US results show. One consequence matters for 749.226. **Unisons sit inside the 73% small-interval mass.** An engine that meets a small-interval target partly by repeating notes raises its commonest-pitch share without breaking any interval mark. No source tests interval statistics by metre.

### Roughness aversion and fusion look universal; consonance preference looks learned

[MEASURED, press release; full text not opened] McDermott and colleagues tested more than 100 Tsimane' in 2011 and 2015, beside a Bolivian town, La Paz, and US musicians and non-musicians. Tsimane' preference for consonance was "**undetectable**." The Bolivian groups showed a small preference and the US groups a much larger one. Yet Tsimane' disliked acoustic roughness like everyone else ([MIT News on McDermott et al. 2016, Nature 535](https://news.mit.edu/2016/music-tastes-cultural-not-hardwired-brain-0713); group sizes and statistics to be checked). [MEASURED] McPherson et al. separated fusion from liking. Both Tsimane' and Boston listeners fused consonant intervals more than dissonant ones (Tsimane' Z = 3.27, p = 0.001), with the octave, fifth and fourth fused more than their neighbours. Tsimane' showed no pleasantness preference (synthetic p = 0.57, sung p = 0.90), against Boston t(27) = 6.57, and fusion and preference were uncorrelated across individuals (rs = 0.01) ([McPherson et al. 2020, Nat. Commun.](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1038/s41467-020-16448-6.pdf)). [MEASURED, prior notes; not re-checked] Less-integrated Tsimane' (n = 107) showed no consonance preference (p = .32, BF10 = 0.24), more-integrated Tsimane' did, and roughness aversion held in every group (p < .0001) ([McPherson-McNato et al., Cognition 2026, online Oct 2025](https://mcdermottlab.mit.edu/papers/McPherson_etal_2025_global_integration.pdf); correction: the earlier report dated it 2025). [MEASURED] Log-scale interval reproduction and relative pitch held in all groups, but **octave (chroma) matching was absent in Tsimane'** (p = 0.75; replication p = 0.68). Reproduction was accurate "from 100 to 4,000 Hz." Correction to the prior notes: that is the range tested, not a measured perceptual limit ([Jacoby et al. 2019](https://piv.medicina.uc.cl/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Jacoby_et_al_2019.pdf)).

The critics argue that biology remains, though their samples cannot settle the cross-cultural question. [MEASURED] Bowling, Purves & Gill had 30 listeners rate every dyad, triad and tetrad: 15 Singapore conservatory students with about 13 years of Western training and 15 Vienna students with under a year of lessons. The groups agreed (Spearman 0.93, 0.92, 0.88), harmonic similarity picked the more consonant chord in 96% of significant dyad differences, and a vocal-similarity metric in 98% of them. The authors call it "misleading to characterize the influence of biology on tone perception as 'innate'" and dispute McDermott's framing ([Bowling, Purves & Gill 2018, PNAS](https://chrysalis-foundation.org/PNAS-2017-Bowling-1713206115.pdf); [reply to Goffinet](https://www.chrysalis-foundation.org/wp-content/uploads/pnas.1805570115.pdf)). [OUR INFERENCE] Both samples are Western-trained or Western-exposed, so the study cannot speak to listeners without Western exposure. [MEASURED] Composite models say consonance has several sources. Harrison & Pearce combine interference (negative), periodicity or harmonicity (positive), cultural familiarity and note count. On Bowling's data the composite reached r = .88, against .77 for interference alone and .72 each for harmonicity and culture ([Harrison & Pearce 2020, Psych. Review](https://core.ac.uk/download/228298307.pdf); fitted coefficients only in a figure, not read). [MEASURED] Marjieh et al., in **Nature Communications, not Nature** (a correction to earlier citations), collected 235,440 judgments from 4,272 online participants in the US and South Korea. Stretched spectra moved the octave peak to about 12.78 semitones, deleting harmonics removed peaks, and unitary explanations "seem untenable." The authors themselves note that "musical enculturation seems to be important here" ([Marjieh et al. 2024, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11258268)). [MEASURED, abstract or press release only] Cultural familiarity "affected the correlations drastically" among consonance concepts, while tension stayed tied to consonance ([Lahdelma & Eerola 2020](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1265026/cultural-familiarity-and-musical-expertise-impact-the-pleasantness-of-consonancedissonance-but-not-its-perceived-tension)). Two remote groups in northwest Pakistan did not prefer the major chord, liked the minor chord, and disliked "the most jarring and dissonant" chord as UK listeners did ([Durham news on Lahdelma, Athanasopoulos & Eerola](https://dur.ac.uk/departments/academic/music/about-us/news/is-taste-in-musical-fundamentals-learned-or-innate-/)).

[OUR INFERENCE] For a melody-only engine, consonance enters in three places only: the 500 and 700 cent degrees in the scale, the rarity of the tritone as a melodic interval (0.2%) and as a scale relation, and the melody-to-chord fit, which lies outside this pass. None bears on commonest-pitch share or range. Because octave equivalence is not universal, a commonest pitch counted by *pitch class* is a Western-dialect measure. Counting by *octave-specific pitch* is the culture-neutral choice, and the project should say which one each mark uses. No large cross-cultural study of the pleasantness of *successive* (melodic) intervals was found.

## Shape and hierarchy: arches on average, the centre carried by time

### The arch is a tendency of the average, not a phrase type

[MEASURED] Across 9,467 Essen songs (52,899 phrases, 32 regions), arch contours were significantly commoner than V shapes and descending contours commoner than ascending ones (both P < 0.0001). The same held in 80 bird songs. Phrase-final notes are long: relative duration **1.58 against 0.93** for other notes ([Tierney, Russo & Patel 2011, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3174665), read through a summary; shape percentages only in a figure). [THEORY ONLY] The authors attribute the shared shapes to motor and energy limits of vocal production ([abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PNAS..10815510T)). [MEASURED] In 35 solo Garland songs (387 phrases), a quadratic fitted to phrases on normalised time gave **a = −0.0017 and b = −0.046** overall. The Western subsample had b = −0.0645 and the non-Western b = −0.030, so **non-Western descent is less than half as steep**. The average contour correlates r = .95 with bird song and r = .88 with Essen ([Savage, Tierney & Patel 2017, CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/74204746.pdf), read through a summary). [MEASURED] In iterated singing, five-tone melodies went "from flat melodic contours to distinctive arch-shaped contours," and in 4- to 12-tone melodies the highest pitch tends to fall "near the third tone" ([Anglada-Tort et al. 2023](https://research.gold.ac.uk/36541/1/Anglada-Tort_2023.pdf)).

[MEASURED] Single phrases, though, do not fall into types. Cornelissen et al. tested German Essen songs, a Nova Scotia set, the Essen Chinese subsets and Gregorian chant for clustering after UMAP and found **no clusters in any real phrase set** of typical 5 to 15 notes, although the same test found them in a synthetic set. Chinese phrases are "better described as descending, or horizontal-descending," which the authors call a counter-example to a strict arch hypothesis ([Cornelissen et al. 2026, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13119v1), read through a summary). They also show that Huron's 1996 type frequencies, in which four types are very frequent and the other five are each "≤1.2%," depend on the tolerance ε used to call a step level. Tierney et al. set ε at 0.2 semitones without justification, while the entropy-maximising ε is 1.4 ([same](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13119v1); [Huron 1996 entry](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/huron-themelodicarchinwesternfolksongs-1996)). [MEASURED, descriptive] Regional accounts include Plains "terraced descent," Northwest Coast undulation "with a tendency to move downwards," and level-contour Eastern Woodlands curing songs ([Nettl, Levine & Keillor](https://musc102.blogs.wesleyan.edu/files/2021/04/Bruno-Nettl-Victoria-Lindsay-Levine-and-Elaine-Keillor_Amerindian-Music.pdf)). Cantometrics clusters include "undulating or descending melody" and "melody tends to descend" ([ACE Song Style](https://www.culturalequity.org/research/song-style)). [MEASURED, prior notes; not re-checked] In UK pop, a more common global contour predicted earworm status (coefficient 7.08, p = .03) ([Jakubowski et al. 2017](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1371487)).

[OUR INFERENCE] A scorer should reward arch and descent weakly and at song level (a < 0, b < 0 on the mean over phrases), with a weight that varies by style, and it should never gate single phrases. Any count of contour types must report its ε, or use the continuous (a, b) instead. **No source analyses contour by metre.** The motor account does not depend on metre, so the null expectation is the same signs in 3/4, 6/8 and 4/4.

### Listeners learn a hierarchy from time-on-tone, and the tonic need not be the commonest pitch

[MEASURED] Tonal hierarchies have been found in every culture tested, and in each one, stability ratings track how long each tone sounds. With 8 Indian and 8 Western listeners hearing 10 rags, both groups rated the tonic and fifth highest. Profiles correlated **.82 with tone duration** (Indian .828, Western .805), and the authors found "little evidence that the Western tonal hierarchies were dominant for either group." "The distribution of tones in music is a psychologically effective means of conveying the tonal hierarchy" ([Castellano, Bharucha & Krumhansl 1984, full text](https://core.ac.uk/download/191890562.pdf)). [MEASURED, secondary only] Balinese and Western listeners were both sensitive to tonal function, scale membership and tone frequency in a pelog melody, with results "less clear" for slendro ([Stevens et al. 2013, on Kessler, Hansen & Shepard 1984](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00789/full)). [MEASURED, abstract only] In 18 North Sami yoiks, listeners' responses and neural-network models were both shaped by statistical tone distributions ([Krumhansl et al. 2000](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/10/2075407.htm)). [MEASURED] NHS expert listeners heard a tonal centre in **97.8%** of ratings. In the 73 songs with one clear centre, 85.3% of ratings agreed with the modal pitch class, and in multimodal songs the second centre was often a fourth away ([Mehr et al. 2019](https://dcknox.github.io/files/MehrSinghKnoxEtAl_UniversalityDiversityHumanSong.pdf)). The key-finder used there was "trained on behavioural data from Western listeners" ([Harrison, ch. 13](https://pmcharrison.github.io/intro-to-music-and-science/music-across-the-world.html)).

[MEASURED] **The tonic is not necessarily the most frequent pitch.** In Aarden's Western folk data, as reported by Parncutt, the major-key rank order of degrees is 5, 3, 1, 2, 4, 6, 7: "5ˆ is more prevalent than 1ˆ in the major key, and the two scale degrees are about equally prevalent in the minor" ([Parncutt 2011](https://static.uni-graz.at/fileadmin/_Persoenliche_Webseite/parncutt_richard/Pdfs/Pa11_tonic.pdf); Aarden's proportions not obtained). [MEASURED] The Krumhansl-Kessler major profile is 6.35, 2.23, 3.48, 2.33, 4.38, 4.09, 2.52, 5.19, 2.39, 3.66, 2.29, 2.88 ([same](https://static.uni-graz.at/fileadmin/_Persoenliche_Webseite/parncutt_richard/Pdfs/Pa11_tonic.pdf)). [OUR INFERENCE, arithmetic] Normalised, that profile gives the tonic **0.152** of its total in major and 0.142 in minor. These are ratings, not note shares, so they serve only as a scale reference. [MEASURED, abstract only] Essen's scale-degree frequencies match 38 North American children's songs "near-perfectly," and better than Billboard Hot 100 songs do ([Verosky 2023](https://doaj.org/article/204c0bcf8368424f9de55d7d15c60137)). [MEASURED, read through a summary] Across about 36,000 melodies, "most melodies show a tonic repeated more than other notes," with tonic and fifth on longer durations, but no share is given ([McBride et al. 2024b](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2408.12635); [TheSession-flagged]). McBride & Fitch 2026 report tonic and dominant as the most stable pitches across variant families, but that study is built on TheSession data and is mentioned here only as flagged ([arXiv 2607.12673](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12673)).

[OUR INFERENCE] A tune can have a clear centre with only 0.2 to 0.3 of its notes on the commonest pitch, because finals, strong beats, long notes and (in Indian music) the drone also carry the centre. A ceiling on the commonest pitch therefore does not threaten tonal clarity, provided the centre keeps those positions. Since time-on-tone conveys the hierarchy, the duration-weighted share is the perceptually relevant one, and the rank order of degrees matters more than the height of the peak. **No source reports the tonic's or the commonest pitch's share of notes or time in any non-Western corpus**, and none compares tonal concentration between triple and duple metre.

## Range and concentration: where the 3/4 tunes fall short

### A folk phrase spans nine semitones and a folk song about twelve

[MEASURED] Essen phrases average a range of **8.9 semitones (SD 3.7)**, with 9.5 notes a phrase and 5.6 phrases a song ([Tierney et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3174665)). Garland phrase ranges are **9.8 Western, 7.4 non-Western, 8.7 overall** ([Savage, Tierney & Patel 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/74204746.pdf)). [MEASURED, read through a summary] Dutch vocal melodies span **12.5 semitones** across a whole song, against 15.6 for instrumental tunes ([McBride et al. 2024b](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2408.12635); [TheSession-flagged]; check against the paper). [MEASURED, abstract only] Matched children's and adult songs from 35 areas show children's songs with **fewer scale degrees and narrower ranges**, which the authors tie to motor limits ([PsyArXiv record](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.31234/osf.io/ptvw7)). [MEASURED] NHS love songs have a larger pitch range than lullabies. Pitch range loads positively and "predominance of the most common pitch class" loads negatively on a Melodic Complexity factor, which did not predict song function ([Mehr et al. 2019](https://dcknox.github.io/files/MehrSinghKnoxEtAl_UniversalityDiversityHumanSong.pdf)). [MEASURED] Infant-directed song across 21 societies has less pitch variability than adult-directed song, but the same median pitch ([Hilton et al. 2022](https://projects.iq.harvard.edu/sites/projects.iq.harvard.edu/files/epl/files/hilton_et_al-2022-nature_human_behaviour.pdf)). [MEASURED, prior notes; not re-checked] Across 55 languages, song is higher than speech (D = 1.6) and has more stable pitch (D = 0.7) ([Ozaki et al. 2024](https://research.gold.ac.uk/36542/1/Ozaki_2024.pdf)).

Narrow styles exist and are named. [MEASURED, descriptive] Inuit ambitus "could be limited to … a 2nd or be as wide as a 10th," and most Kwakiutl songs span "a major 6th" ([Nettl, Levine & Keillor](https://musc102.blogs.wesleyan.edu/files/2021/04/Bruno-Nettl-Victoria-Lindsay-Levine-and-Elaine-Keillor_Amerindian-Music.pdf)). Cantometrics cluster 10 has "tonal range restricted to a monotone or minor or major second," and a factor links "limitation of the vocal range" with "repetitiousness of the text" ([ACE](https://www.culturalequity.org/research/song-style); [Wood et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9629617)). [MEASURED] US chart pop has drawn tighter over time. In 1,131 Billboard top-5 melodies, two thirds of pitches lay within about **7 semitones in 1950 to 1974, 6 in 1975 to 1999 and 5.5 from 2000 on**, and mean interval fell from 2.3 to 2.0 semitones. The authors call the study preliminary ([Hamilton & Pearce 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11224395)). No published figure for a comfortable tessitura in untrained singers was found; von Hippel & Huron 2000 was located only by title ([Scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/d61da2e28fb69e0f7a57e22fd7fd1969)).

[OUR INFERENCE] The engine's 3/4 *whole tune* spans 9 semitones ([fixlist 749.227](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)). That is the range of **one** average folk phrase (8.9 in Essen, 8.7 in Garland). The waltzes' 10th percentile of 12 sits almost exactly on the Dutch vocal whole-song mean of 12.5, so a 12-semitone floor in 3/4 is ordinary folk vocal practice, not a pop demand. A whole song covers more than any single phrase because its phrases sit at different heights. The engine's deficit is therefore between phrases, not within them. That fits the project's own first reading of the 3/4 package, where the register plan gave the room but "leaves the centres at 64, so 75 notes in 100 fall on four pitches," and form B, which moved each section's centre into the new room and reached range 17, 11 pitches and commonest pitch 0.269 ([fixlist, 3/4 repair package](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)). The low-skip bias suggests how to widen without harm: leaps low, the top reached by steps. Song 68, a punk waltz for high tenor, belongs at the wide love-and-dance end, not the lullaby end. **No source compares range or register between metres.**

### No culture's per-song commonest-pitch share is in print

**The evidence does not answer the question directly.** No source found in either pass reports the per-song share of notes on the commonest pitch as a distribution for any culture: not Essen German or Chinese, Meertens, Densmore or NHS. [MEASURED] The variable exists: NHS codes "predominance of the most common pitch class" as part of the simple pole of melodic complexity, but the per-song values sit in the open data and were not read ([Mehr et al. 2019](https://dcknox.github.io/files/MehrSinghKnoxEtAl_UniversalityDiversityHumanSong.pdf)). The only outside anchors are the project's own counts: Essen's triple-metre arm at 0.287 and 0.280, and the 538 lead-sheet waltzes at **0.167 / 0.225 / 0.313** at the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles ([fixlist 749.226, 749.227](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)).

Ethnography locates the high end. [MEASURED, descriptive] Northwest Coast songs use "a limited number of pitches, often concentrating on a single pitch" with "recitative-like singing." Arctic songs have "recitative-like passages where words were enunciated on the same pitch." Apache verses may be sung "in a parlando style on one or two pitches" ([Nettl, Levine & Keillor](https://musc102.blogs.wesleyan.edu/files/2021/04/Bruno-Nettl-Victoria-Lindsay-Levine-and-Elaine-Keillor_Amerindian-Music.pdf)). Of 180 Chippewa songs Densmore tabulated, 120 end on the keynote, and many Midé songs use only three or four tones ([Densmore 1910, archive.org text](https://archive.org/stream/chippewamusic00dens/chippewamusic00dens_djvu.txt); OCR partly garbled). [THEORY ONLY, practice description] In Gregorian psalm tones the reciting tone "carries the following words on a single pitch" between short opening and cadence formulas, and reciting tones also carry Yemenite cantillation and Moroccan Qur'anic recitation ([Wikipedia, Reciting tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciting_tone)). [OUR INFERENCE, arithmetic] A 12- to 20-syllable psalm verse with a 2- or 3-syllable intonation and 3- to 5-note cadences puts roughly half or more of its syllables on the reciting tone, a share of about 0.5 or more. No measured psalmody share exists. **In every tradition read, high concentration travels with text delivery and narrow range, and never with dance or metred song.** On that reading the engine's 0.39 to 0.42 sits between folk song (about 0.28) and recitation.

Perception offers no threshold. [MEASURED] Stable pitch makes repeated speech sound like song (song ratings rose from 3.35 to 4.48), in 40 English speakers only ([Chen, Tierney & Pfordresher 2025](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/apal-pfordresher/wp-content/uploads/sites/79/2025/06/ChenTierneyPfordresher_Cognition.pdf)). Listeners made errors on **26.3% of sung against 3.6% of spoken** words, and on 31.4% of melismatic passages, but pitch repetition was not varied ([Collister & Huron 2008](https://d-scholarship.pitt.edu/19444/1/EMR000050a%2DCollister%2DHuron.pdf)). In a survey of 143 musicians, repeated notes were not named among 43 factors in sung-text intelligibility ([Fine & Ginsborg 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00809/full)). [MEASURED, prior notes] Liking follows an inverted U on information content ([Gold et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867811)), and 50 of 57 studies fit an inverted U for complexity or familiarity ([Chmiel & Schubert 2017](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/back-to-the-inverted-u-for-music-preference-a-review-of-the-liter/)). [OUR INFERENCE] Syllabic setting helps intelligibility, but nothing read shows a monotone beating a stepwise line. No experiment varies the commonest-pitch share and measures liking, memory or intelligibility.

### Pitch-class entropy sits in a folk band but misses concentration

[MEASURED, read through a summary] Across 62 folk corpora, chroma entropy falls within **1.7 to 2.8 bits** (95% range). Pitch and rhythm entropy trade off between corpora (**r = −0.66**, or −0.34 without Densmore), and larger pitch alphabets go with more unequal pitch distributions (Gini). Total information runs 80 to 144 bits in folk, 245 to 673 in art and 40 to 93 in child corpora. About 51% of pitch sequences and 71% of rhythm sequences repeat within a song ([McBride et al. 2024b](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2408.12635); [TheSession-flagged]; the Irish data is not among the main analysed corpora, so these figures do not rest on it). [MEASURED] Interval entropy fell over generations of iterated singing ([Anglada-Tort et al. 2023](https://research.gold.ac.uk/36541/1/Anglada-Tort_2023.pdf)), and Billboard pitch information content fell at the 1975 and 2000 "revolutions" ([Hamilton & Pearce 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11224395)). [MEASURED] In a Western pop recognition study (34 participants, 80 phrases), entropy measures loaded positively and mean absolute interval negatively on implicit memorability ([Müllensiefen & Halpern 2014](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/10838/1/PSY-Mu%CC%88llensiefen2014.pdf)).

[OUR INFERENCE, arithmetic] Take six pitches with share p on one and the rest spread evenly. Entropy is **2.33 bits at p = 0.42** and **2.52 bits at p = 0.29**, and both sit inside the folk band. An eight-pitch tune at p = 0.29 reaches about 2.87 bits. So entropy separates tunes mainly by **alphabet size (749.227)**, and moves only about 0.2 bits for a change in **concentration (749.226)**. The commonest-pitch share must stay a separate mark. There is also a structural mismatch: folk corpora pair small alphabets with *flatter* distributions, while the engine pairs a small alphabet with a *peaked* one. And if the between-corpus pitch-rhythm trade-off also holds within songs, a tune that is simple on both (narrow, concentrated pitch with rhythm sung again 0.90 of the time) is unusual for folk. That is untested at song level.

## What the world evidence says about the 3/4 problems

[OUR INFERENCE, all of this section] **The literature gives no reason to expect triple metre to narrow a tune or concentrate its pitch.** Every pitch regularity above, from small intervals and the arch to the tonal hierarchy and phrase range, is explained by vocal-motor, memory or exposure accounts that do not depend on metre. Not one source measures any of them by metre. The fixlist names the likelier cause: "The 4/4 engine has a range floor and a register plan read from kept songs; 3/4 has neither," and 956's ceiling on the commonest pitch is set "only inside `if L["metre"] == "4/4"`" ([fixlist 749.227, 749.255](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)). On this reading the 3/4 defects are plumbing, not metre. For 749.256 the consequence is mixed. Pitch tables may travel between metres more safely than rhythm tables, because the pitch universals are metre-blind in theory. But that is a hypothesis to test (interval shape and contour signs by metre in Essen), not a licence to borrow.

Four mechanisms can be told apart on data the project holds. The **narrowness floor** is small: with k pitches the commonest share cannot fall below 1/k (0.167 for 6 pitches), so narrowness explains little of the 0.42. **Register parking** explains more: the centres sitting at 64 put 75 notes in 100 on four pitches, and moving them (form B) met range and commonest pitch at once. **Repetition by splitting** comes from 749.247, where splitting a note for extra syllables repeats its pitch. That raises the share counted by notes but not the share weighted by duration, and unisons are invisible to the small-interval marks. **Recitation drift** is the profile of text-delivery genres. The world evidence predicts that a correct fix lowers the count-based share more than the duration-based share. It also predicts that the fix moves phrase centres apart rather than widening single phrases, and keeps the tonic on finals and downbeats while its raw share falls.

Two cautions run the other way. Form B's gains cost first-beat stress (0.469 against 0.583), and the earlier passes "were the pick's, not the form's" ([fixlist, form B reading](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)), so a register plan and the stress draw interact. Pop also concentrates pitch more than older song: the post-2000 Billboard two-thirds span of 5.5 semitones means pop 3/4's narrow seed-3 register (range 12, 8 pitches) may not breach a pop norm even if it breaches a waltz one. The Hooktheory clips, not the waltzes, should set pop 3/4's band.

## Conclusion

The world literature does not give Luthier a 3/4 rulebook, because nobody has measured pitch by metre. What it gives instead is a set of metre-blind anchors and one diagnostic picture. The anchors are a phrase of about 9 semitones, a song of about 12, small steps with the unison counted among them, an arch on average, and a centre carried by time and position. The picture is that a tune with one phrase's range and a third or more of its notes on one pitch behaves like recitation. That changes how 749.226, 749.227 and 749.255 should be framed. They are not three faults. They are one register fault (phrases parked at one height) plus one counting fault (split notes adding repeats that are invisible to the interval marks), and the 3/4 path simply lacks the machinery that hides both in 4/4. The project is better placed than the literature to settle the open questions. A per-song commonest-pitch distribution by metre, counted four ways on Essen, the lead sheets and the Hooktheory clips, would itself be a first in print, and NHS (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) is the one open cross-cultural check available for it.
