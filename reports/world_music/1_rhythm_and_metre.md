# Shared grids and style weights shape threes

Across every tradition read, rhythm in threes rests on the same small machine: listeners in all 39 groups tested hear a few small-integer ratios (1:1, 1:2, 2:1 and 1:1:1 above all), they hear a group as ending on its longest interval, and every metre on record, from Balkan aksak to North Indian tāl to the West African bell, is built from beats of 2 and 3 units grouped as evenly as the cycle allows [MEASURED for the priors ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990/)), OUR INFERENCE for the cross-tradition summary; sources in the sections below]. What changes from one tradition to the next is a short list of weights on that shared grid: which slot is densest, whether the cycle's first beat is also its loudest, and how often notes arrive one division early [OUR INFERENCE]. For Luthier this means 3/4, 6/8 and any later metre can share one rule set written by metrical level and felt-beat period (about 600 ms), with style tables supplying a few small weights [OUR INFERENCE]. The three numbers the maintainer asked for have no published answer for triple or compound metre. No source gives a beat-1 against beats-2-and-3 share, a melodic anticipation rate, or a count of distinct bar rhythms per section [no source read reports one]. The nearest anchors are a fully counted Essen 4/4 profile ([Murphy 2020](https://musmat.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/05-Scott.pdf); beat 1 takes 33.6% of on-beat onsets by our arithmetic on its counts) [MEASURED counts, OUR INFERENCE share], rock's 4/4 anticipation shares [MEASURED] ([Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/tan-lustig-temperley.pdf)), and the team's own figures (6 distinct bar rhythms per 16 bars in the pop 3/4 clips against the engine's 10) [TEAM FIGURE]. All three can be counted on data the project already holds. One correction to the last report also stands: Hayes & Kaun's 10.9% "headless" lines are late entries, so the sentence "about 89% begin with a pickup" should be dropped [MEASURED, per the source text as summarised] ([Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)).

**How to read this report.** Every claim carries one label. **[MEASURED]** means a study or corpus count reports it. **[THEORY ONLY]** means a published model or analyst states it with no data behind that point. **[OUR INFERENCE]** means it was worked out in the notes or here and no source states it. **[TEAM FIGURE]** is the notes' label for the project's own unpublished counts from `fixlist_v54.md`. **Every web source behind this report was read through a summarising fetch tool, not as a full original, so every exact figure must be checked against the original before it is hard-coded.** Sources read only through an abstract, or only as relayed by another paper, are marked so inline. No TheSession.org data is used. McBride & Fitch 2026, which relies mainly on it, is mentioned only as flagged. No figure from the project's withdrawn studies appears. The level trees, Hayes's grammar and maximum-entropy weights, the Tan, Lustig & Temperley anticipation table, de Clercq's 6/8 tempi and the SRDC repeat arithmetic are in `Mathematics of fitting words to metre.md` and are only pointed to here. Nothing in this report is a result for the engine: every rule it suggests is to be stated and tested by the maintainer first.

## Every group tested hears small-integer ratios and ends groups on the long note

The strongest cross-cultural evidence comes from iterated tapping. In Jacoby et al.'s study, listeners heard a three-click cycle whose intervals summed to 2,000 ms, tapped it back, and their tapping became the next stimulus for five rounds, with a 1,000 ms replication in 13 groups [MEASURED] ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990/)). **Every one of the 39 groups, across 15 countries, had prior modes at 1:1:1 and 1:1:2**, with the three rotations of 1:1:2 "about equally prominent", and all groups sat closer to small-integer ratios than chance (P < 0.001) [MEASURED] ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990/)). **The rotation with the long interval last was the most frequent in 31 of 39 groups, and first taps came right after the long interval in 33 of 39** [MEASURED] ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990/)). Nine of 22 categories were slightly displaced toward isochrony, with the short interval lengthened; the part read gives no size beyond "slight" [MEASURED] ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990/)). The study used only cyclic three-interval rhythms of about 1 to 2 s, with no melody and no metre longer than one cycle [MEASURED, the authors' stated limitation] ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990/)).

The culture-specific layer is large in standardised terms but small in probability mass. The prior weight on 3:3:2 was 0.043 higher in African and Afro-diasporic groups (d = 2.60), 2:2:3 was 0.011 higher where that rhythm is traditional (Turkey, Botswana, Bulgaria; d = 2.17), and 7:2:3 was 0.0082 higher in three Malian groups (d = 2.33) [MEASURED] ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990/)). Two dimensions explain 85.9% of between-group divergence. The first tracks 3:3:2 (r = 0.90). The second correlates with 1:2:3 and 1:3:2 (r = 0.56 and 0.62), and the authors say it "partially" embodies the strength of 6/8 rhythms [MEASURED] ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990/)). University and online samples looked more like the US than local non-student groups did, musicianship mattered little, and no strong language effect appeared [MEASURED; carried from earlier notes, not rechecked] ([Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990/)). The earlier US and Tsimane' comparison found that both groups had priors at simple integer ratios, but different sets, with the Tsimane' priors matching Tsimane' music and US musicians matching non-musicians [MEASURED; read only through a press summary, as the paper's page returned a CAPTCHA] ([MIT News 2017](https://news.mit.edu/2017/how-brain-perceives-rhythm-1205); [Jacoby & McDermott 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28065607/)). Which ratios differed, and by how much, were not recovered, so no number from that paper should be used. Song corpora agree: across recordings from 86 societies, the ratios 1:1, 2:1 and 3:1 make up 86% of rhythmic bigrams and every other ratio is under 1% [MEASURED; not rechecked this pass] ([Mehr et al. 2019](https://stevenpinker.com/publications/universality-and-diversity-human-song)), and in a 304-recording world sample "fewer than five note lengths" held at a long-term frequency of 0.72, although coding reliability for that feature was weak [MEASURED; from the earlier project report] ([Savage et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517223/)).

Three consequences follow for the engine. First, inside a 3/4 bar or a 6/8 beat the relevant forms are two-interval ones, 1:2 and 2:1 (crotchet-minim and minim-crotchet in 3/4; crotchet-quaver and quaver-crotchet in a 6/8 beat), plus 1:1:1. So a plain waltz or 6/8 vocabulary built from those sits entirely in the universal layer and needs no dialect weight [OUR INFERENCE]. The three-interval 1:1:2 fills four units, so in 6/8 it crosses a beat [OUR INFERENCE]. Second, the "6/8-like" second dimension is the one place the 2024 result speaks to compound time, and because it varies by culture, the weight on mixed long-short-medium cells inside a 6/8 span is a style parameter [OUR INFERENCE]. Third, dialect effects move about 1 to 4 percentage points of prior mass, so style terms should be small additive bonuses on a shared base distribution, not replacements for it [OUR INFERENCE]. The long-last bias also gives a principled answer to a question the last report left open. In a 3/4 bar of minim then crotchet, the long interval comes first, so the beat-3 crotchet is heard as grouping with the next downbeat; in crotchet then minim, the bar closes on its long note. **The bias therefore predicts that beat 3 of 3/4 behaves as an upbeat more than beat 2 does: stressed syllables should avoid beat 3 more than beat 2, and phrase-initial unstressed syllables should favour it** [OUR INFERENCE; untested in any source read]. The isochrony pull adds a performance caveat: a notated 2:1 may be sung near 1.8:1, which a notes-only judge should not penalise [OUR INFERENCE].

## Additive metres build cycles from 2s and 3s, and "one" need not be loud

Four habits recur across Balkan aksak, Turkish usul, North Indian tāl and West African timelines: beats of 2 and 3 units only, groupings as even as the cycle length allows, one privileged return point per cycle, and no guarantee that the return point is the loudest or densest place [OUR INFERENCE, assembled from the sources below].

On aksak, Western 11 to 12-month-olds detected metre-violating changes in Balkan dance tunes only when the tunes were isochronous (looking 22.67 against 18.37 s, P < .01), but after two weeks of home exposure they detected them in tunes of 7 counts in a 3:2 long-short ratio too (24.07 against 19.31 s, P < .01), while adults stayed at chance [MEASURED; not rechecked] ([Hannon & Trehub 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1194930)). An earlier study by the same authors found that Western 6-month-olds detected violations in both simple and complex Balkan metres, Western adults only in simple ones, and Balkan adults did well on complex ones [MEASURED; relayed without numbers by another paper] ([Kalender, Trehub & Schellenberg 2013](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/KalenderEtAl2013.pdf)). In 56 Canadian students hearing Turkish songs in 2/4 and 5/8 (2+3), Western-only listeners did not detect 5/8 violations (2.9 against 3.4 on a 1 to 7 scale, p = .061, wrong direction), while listeners with Indian musical exposure did (3.8 against 3.0, p = .015), and every group processed the simple metre better [MEASURED] ([Kalender, Trehub & Schellenberg 2013](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/KalenderEtAl2013.pdf)). Exposure to any non-isochronous tradition thus transferred to an unfamiliar one. In Turkish art music, Holzapfel's study of 913 notated makam compositions in six usul found that onset distributions follow each usul's stroke pattern closely but do not match a stratified accent hierarchy, so Turkish metre is "less stratified" than Western metre [MEASURED; relayed by another paper, no per-usul numbers] ([Holzapfel 2015](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/09298215.2014.939661), via [London, Polak & Jacoby 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/83231804.pdf)).

North Indian rūpak tāl (7 = 3+2+2) is the clearest case of a light "one". In 42 khyāl recordings, sam, the cycle's first beat and point of return, falls on the khālī ("empty") group, carries no bass stroke, and is marked by a wave rather than a clap [MEASURED] ([Clayton 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/462/60)). Mātrās are near isochronous (largest mean deviation about 1.5%), and the 3-group is not systematically shortened [MEASURED] ([Clayton 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/462/60)). **Mātrās 3, 5 and 7 are subdivided more than 95% of the time, mātrā 1 only about 28%**, and Clayton reads mātrās 1 and 4 as the main emphases with mātrā 3 driving into 4 [MEASURED] ([Clayton 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/462/60)). He also reports that, under maximal evenness, 7 has nine forms (3+4, 3+2+2, 2+2+2+1 and rotations) and that South Asian practice always splits 7 as 3+4 and 5 as 2+3 [THEORY ONLY, as Clayton reports it] ([Clayton 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/462/60)).

The West African standard bell pattern, 2+2+1+2+2+2+1 over 12 pulses, groups 5+7 rather than 6+6, and its felt beat is "at or near the dotted-quarter level", four per cycle, set by "the dancer's feet" [THEORY ONLY, ethnographic] ([Agawu 2006](https://music.arts.uci.edu/abauer/6.2/readings/Agawu_JAMS_06.pdf)). Agawu calls the idea that additive rhythm is the hallmark of African music "in all likelihood a colossal error", rewrites the pattern divisively over four dotted-quarter beats, and reports, following Anku, that Anlo-Ewe and Yoruba listeners may place "one" on different strokes, while finding that evidence thin [THEORY ONLY] ([Agawu 2006](https://music.arts.uci.edu/abauer/6.2/readings/Agawu_JAMS_06.pdf)). He also notes that maximal evenness has "no corroboration in indigenous discourse" [THEORY ONLY] ([Agawu 2006](https://music.arts.uci.edu/abauer/6.2/readings/Agawu_JAMS_06.pdf)). Malian jembe timing gives the measured counterpart. In 15 performances (about 50 minutes, 42,297 onsets), tempo rose from about 120 to about 180 bpm while onset positions stayed stable, subdivisions in Manjanin sat at about 25% and 60% of the beat, and **the beat positions were struck less often than the upbeat positions just before them**, Maraka's beats 3 and 4 excepted [MEASURED] ([London, Polak & Jacoby 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-016-1093-7)). The authors conclude that onset frequency "does not correspond to patterns of metrical accent" [MEASURED] ([London, Polak & Jacoby 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-016-1093-7)). Drummers played those uneven subdivisions as precisely as even ones, with timing SD about 2.5 to 3.5% of the beat [MEASURED; not rechecked] ([Polak, London & Jacoby 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00285/full)).

The cross-tradition invariant can therefore be stated once: beats of 2 or 3 units of the fastest regular pulse, maximally even grouping, one return point, and density on and just before strong points [OUR INFERENCE]. What varies, and should be a style weight, is whether the return point is also the densest or most stressed point (not in rūpak), whether the pre-beat slot outnumbers the beat (yes in jembe, sometimes in rock, essentially never in nineteenth-century English song), and which rotation counts as "one" [OUR INFERENCE]. In the last report's level-tree form, an aksak metre is a tree with unequal children at the beat level (7/8 as 2+2+3), and the long beat of 3 offers the same "beat plus two weak siblings" slot as a 6/8 beat, so a rule written for the 6/8 beat already covers the 3-beat of any aksak [OUR INFERENCE; untested]. This also places the engine's strong first-beat pull in pop 3/4 (the K3 line) as a style choice, not a metric universal [OUR INFERENCE].

The evidence stops short in two traditions the brief named. **No source on Arabic iqāʿāt (maqsūm, samāʿī thaqīl, Arabic aksak 9/8) was read, and no measured stress or onset data for them was found.** No numbers were found for Carnatic adi, misra chapu or khanda chapu, or for Hindustani jhaptāl. A tapping study of complex metres (Snyder et al. 2006) was located but could not be read, so whether North American tappers regularise 3:2 toward 2:1 is unconfirmed.

## Evenness, syncopation and groove: which mathematics survives outside 4/4

Toussaint's Euclidean rhythm E(k,n) places k onsets on n pulses as evenly as possible, built by Bjorklund's algorithm with the same structure as Euclid's GCD algorithm [THEORY ONLY] ([Toussaint 2005](https://cgm.cs.mcgill.ca/~godfried/publications/banff.pdf)). His catalogue matches E(3,8) to the tresillo, E(5,8) to the cinquillo, E(7,12) to the West African bell and E(5,12) to Venda clapping, and attributes E(3,7) to Bulgarian and E(4,9) to Turkish aksak practice [MEASURED as a catalogue, not an experiment] ([Toussaint 2005](https://cgm.cs.mcgill.ca/~godfried/publications/banff.pdf)). His sorting of Euclidean strings by tradition carries no statistics, contradicts itself on E(5,8), and the paper itself calls an empirical test "interesting" future work [THEORY ONLY] ([Toussaint 2005](https://cgm.cs.mcgill.ca/~godfried/publications/banff.pdf)). The first large listener test, 1,252 periodic rhythms and 177 listeners, reports "very strong evidence" that evenness and balance (defined through the discrete Fourier transform of the onset pattern on a circle) and interonset-interval entropy each affect memory and liking [MEASURED; abstract only, so direction and size of each effect are unread] ([Milne & Herff 2020](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/the-perceptual-relevance-of-balance-evenness-and-entropy-in-music/)). Inside one 3/4 or 6/8 bar the Euclidean rule is nearly trivial (E(3,6) and E(2,6) are the beat layers), so evenness is informative only as a per-bar or per-two-bar onset feature and for hemiola, not as a metre definition [OUR INFERENCE]. The DFT magnitudes are defined identically for 6, 12 or 16 pulses, which makes them one rule with a style-dependent target band [OUR INFERENCE].

Syncopation measures differ sharply in how far they travel. Keith's measure is defined only where the beat count is a power of two, off-beatness fails for prime cycle lengths, and Arom's oddity is useless for odd cycles [THEORY ONLY] ([Gómez et al. 2005](https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2005/bridges2005-73.pdf)). On real 12-pulse timelines, WNBD separated classical motifs from the claves and off-beatness did not [MEASURED, model values without listeners] ([Gómez et al. 2005](https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2005/bridges2005-73.pdf)). Against human difficulty and complexity data, all binary or 16-pulse, Toussaint's metrical complexity, LHL and WNBD led with correlations of about 0.75 to 0.83, and Keith "is not appropriate for rhythms of 12 pulses" [MEASURED; every table cell to be checked] ([Thul 2008](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/rn301491t)). The one direct 4/4 against 6/8 comparison is in Song's thesis:

| Model | Spearman ρ, 4/4 patterns | Spearman ρ, 6/8 patterns |
|---|---|---|
| Pressing (PRS) | 0.95 | 0.76 |
| Toussaint metrical complexity (TMC) | 0.92 | 0.67 |
| Sioros & Guedes (SG) | 0.88 | 0.73 |
| Longuet-Higgins & Lee (LHL) | 0.86 | 0.68 |
| Keith | 0.79 | not applicable |
| WNBD | 0.52 | 0.47 |
| Off-beatness | 0.36, n.s. | 0.17, n.s. |

[MEASURED] ([Song, QMUL thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)). The thesis text and its figure disagree on Pressing's 6/8 value (0.95 against 0.76), so the cell must be checked. Listener agreement was modest (mean pairwise Spearman 0.47), **the same patterns were rated more syncopated in 6/8 than in 4/4 (r = 0.81, p < 0.01)**, and the 12-pulse off-beat set "confuses time-signatures" because it is the union of two metres' on-beats [MEASURED] ([Song, QMUL thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)). The tempo range over which syncopation was felt was about 23 QPM narrower in 6/8 than in 4/4 (p < 0.05) [MEASURED] ([Song, QMUL thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)). Povel & Essens's clock-induction score, a weighted count of clock ticks on silence and on unaccented events, underlies Shmulevich & Povel's complexity measure, which correlated 0.83 with judgements, optimistically since it was fitted to the same data [MEASURED] ([MMM 2003](https://www.mcg.uva.nl/mmm-2003/papers/mmm-16/mmm-16.html)). For Luthier the measure to state once is LHL or Pressing on the level-indexed tree (⟨1, 3, 2⟩ for 3/4, ⟨1, 2, 3⟩ for 6/8, ordered sums such as 2+2+3 for aksak); off-beatness scores 3/4 and 6/8 identically and Keith is undefined in 3/4, so neither should be used [OUR INFERENCE]. Because the same pattern reads as more syncopated in 6/8 and models fit worse there, any syncopation band, including the 0.26 off-beat mark, must come from each metre's own corpus distribution [OUR INFERENCE]. On a level-indexed LHL, "one division early" scores the same one-level gap in 3/4 and 6/8 [OUR INFERENCE].

Groove's inverted U is well replicated, but only in 4/4. Fifty 4/4 drum breaks at 120 bpm (N = 66) gave a quadratic fit of R² = .35 for wanting to move and .43 for pleasure [MEASURED] ([Witek et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0094446)), and a 4/4 study at 96 bpm with 201 participants found the same rhythmic inverted U [MEASURED; not rechecked] ([Matthews et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6328141)). In 4/4 melodies at 120 BPM, **anticipating only the phrase-final note scored as well as adding many weak-beat anticipations (effect sizes 0.601 and 0.597), while anticipating every note scored lower (0.439)** [MEASURED] ([Sioros et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01036/full)). The one study testing other metres, a pupillometry study with 26 clips in 4/4 or an uncommon metre such as 7/8, reported in its conference abstract no pupil difference by metre, and its published title says rhythmic complexity "more strongly predicts groove in common meters" [MEASURED; title and abstract only, no numbers read] ([Spiech et al. 2025, RPPW abstracts](https://www.jyu.fi/en/file/rppw20programmeandabstracts-5pdf); [Spiech et al. 2025, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41402552/)). No groove experiment on 3/4 or 6/8 melodies was found. The inverted U should therefore enter threes as a rank target (the middle of that metre's own corpus band), and Sioros's result supplies a metre-free rule worth testing: one phrase-final anticipation per phrase, aimed at beat 1 of the cadence bar in 3/4, or at beat 1 or the dotted-quarter beat 4 in 6/8 [OUR INFERENCE].

## The comfortable beat sits near 600 ms, which decides how 3/4 and 6/8 are felt

Spontaneous tapping in 305 people aged 4 to 95 ran about **630 ms in 18 to 38-year-olds**, about 300 ms at ages 4 to 7 and about 650 ms past 75, with fastest unpaced tapping of 169 ms and slowest of 2,532 ms in young adults, and preferred perceptual tempo tracking spontaneous tempo (r = .75) [MEASURED] ([McAuley et al. 2006](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~rbelew/courses/cogs260_s10/readings/mcauley_etAl_2006.pdf)). Fraisse's "600 ms" was not found stated in any source read, so McAuley's 630 ms stands in for it. Performed tempi from over 70,000 pieces put preferred listening tempo at about 67 to 150 bpm, and preferred tapping at about 120 to 130 bpm [MEASURED; relayed secondhand from Moelants 2002] ([Hine et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952488/full)). In 23 participants, preferred tapping averaged 104.6 bpm (range 54 to 170) and preferred music tempo 99.3 bpm, with test-retest r = .90 [MEASURED] ([Hine et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952488/full)). Synchronisation becomes bimodal and "subjectively difficult" above about 1,800 ms between beats, and subdividing helps timing "only as long as those parts are at least 200–250 ms long" [MEASURED, review] ([Repp & Su 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-012-0371-2)). Expert Malian drummers hold subdivisions near 108 ms at about 185 bpm [MEASURED; not rechecked] ([Polak, London & Jacoby 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00285/full)). London's window for non-isochronous beats, as relayed by Clayton, is 400 to 1,200 ms with cycles of 1,400 to 2,800 ms [THEORY ONLY, relayed] ([Clayton 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/462/60)); London's general bounds could not be extracted because the copy reached has no text layer ([London 2002](https://cdn.carleton.edu/uploads/sites/721/2021/12/London-2002-MP-Cog-Constraints.pdf)), so they are not cited as read. London also holds that the perceived metre of one notated rhythm can change with tempo [THEORY ONLY] ([London, Hearing in Time, introduction](https://musikwissenschaft.univie.ac.at/fileadmin/user_upload/i_musikwissenschaft/Forschung/Vortragsreihen/J_London_Hearing_in_Time_Intro.pdf)), and Indian practice shows it: in slow khyāl the felt beat can drop to the half-mātrā, and subdivision ratios rise from about 4:1 at 120 to 130 bpm to above 8:1 only below 45 bpm [MEASURED] ([Clayton 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/462/60)). In 12/8 bell music the danced beat is the dotted quarter [THEORY ONLY, ethnographic] ([Agawu 2006](https://music.arts.uci.edu/abauer/6.2/readings/Agawu_JAMS_06.pdf)).

Combining these gives a working window: a comfortable beat of about 400 to 1,000 ms (60 to 150 bpm) peaking near 500 to 650 ms, a possible beat of about 250 to 1,800 ms, and subdivisions of at least about 100 ms, with 200 to 250 ms or more needed for them to help timing [OUR INFERENCE]. Applied to 3/4, a bar is felt "in one" only when the bar itself falls in the beat window, which in practice means crotchet at or above about 170 to 180 bpm; crotchet 140 to 180 is ambiguous; and the usual sung waltz or ballad at crotchet 70 to 130 (bar 1.4 to 2.6 s) is felt in three, with the crotchet as the beat [OUR INFERENCE]. So first-beat stress should matter more the faster the 3/4, because at one-in-a-bar tempi beats 2 and 3 become subdivisions, and a tempo-conditioned K3 is more principled than one fixed line [OUR INFERENCE]. Applied to 6/8, pop's dotted-quarter tempi of 50 to 64 bpm (from the last report, [de Clercq 2015](https://www.midside.com/presentations/declercq_2015_ann_arbor_slides.pdf)) put the dotted quarter at 940 to 1,200 ms, near the slow edge, and the quaver at 310 to 400 ms, inside the window, so slow pop 6/8 can behave partly like a slow 3/4 or 9/8 at the quaver level; folk 6/8 at a dotted quarter of about 100 to 120 puts the beat at the preferred period and the quavers near 170 to 200 ms, close to the useful-subdivision floor, so it is felt firmly in two [OUR INFERENCE]. The metre-free variable that falls out is beat period in milliseconds: every level-indexed rule (stress target level, anticipation size, repeat unit) can be keyed to the level whose period is nearest about 600 ms rather than to the notated beat [OUR INFERENCE]. **No study was found that measures the tempo at which listeners switch between 3/4 in one and in three, or 6/8 in two and in six.**

## The three direct questions have no published triple-metre answer

### Beat one against beats two and three

No readable source gives a numeric onset or stress profile by beat for 3/4 or 6/8 songs. The only fully numeric per-slot profile found is Essen 4/4, from Huron and Ommen's Essen tally as quoted by Murphy: of 75,242 onsets, beat 1 has 19,221, beat 2 10,226, beat 3 14,280 and beat 4 13,458 [MEASURED] ([Murphy 2020](https://musmat.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/05-Scott.pdf)). Murphy lists 2/4, 3/4, 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8 as future work. On those counts **beat 1 takes 33.6% of on-beat onsets (1.34 times an even spread), 1.88 times beat 2 and 1.35 times beat 3, and the beat before the downbeat (beat 4) carries 1.32 times as many onsets as beat 2** although the tree weights them equally [OUR INFERENCE, arithmetic on MEASURED counts]. The off-beat quaver inside a weak beat (just before a strong beat) carries 2.6 times the onsets of the off-beat quaver inside a strong beat [OUR INFERENCE, arithmetic]. Listener salience in 4/4 orders beat 2 above beat 3 above beat 4, the reverse of that onset ordering for beats 2 and 4 [MEASURED; from the last report] ([Ladinig & Honing](https://eprints.illc.uva.nl/id/document/1077)). Palmer & Krumhansl's probe ratings and corpus counts for 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 and 6/8 reflected the metrical hierarchy, but their per-position values exist only in figures [MEASURED; relayed] ([van der Weij, Pearce & Honing 2017](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/13011148/fpsyg_08_00824.pdf)). Prince & Schmuckler found corpus position frequencies in Bach, Mozart, Beethoven and Chopin correlated with those ratings at a mean r = .87 across the same four metres, and that tonally stable pitches co-occur with metrically stable positions beyond chance; the per-position values for 3/4 and 6/8 are in their Figure 2 and must be digitised [MEASURED; abstract and figure caption only] ([Prince & Schmuckler 2014](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-tonal-metric-hierarchy-A-corpus-analysis/991005541603407891); [Prince & Schmuckler 2014, figure page](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Metric-hierarchy-values-frequency-of-occurrence-and-Palmer-Krumhansl-1990-goodness-of_fig2_260095089)). Huron and Ommen report a "similarly robust correlation" across 1,537 Essen songs [MEASURED; relayed, no triple numbers] ([London, Polak & Jacoby 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/83231804.pdf)). Essen itself is rich in threes: of 5,592 melodies, 3/4, 6/8, 3/8 and 6/4 together are about 43% [MEASURED; not rechecked] ([Toiviainen & Eerola 2005](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000049.pdf)).

Carrying the 4/4 folk profile across by level gives a prior to be replaced by counts: **beat 1 at roughly 0.40 to 0.49 of on-beat onsets in 3/4**, centred near 0.45 if the 1.34 ratio to an even spread holds [OUR INFERENCE]. That centre coincides with the K3 first-beat line cut from the 3/4 standards, which the 28 November ruling made a watched figure, not a mark, for pop 3/4 [TEAM FIGURE] (`fixlist_v54.md`, "K3's first-beat line is borrowed"). The coincidence is a plausibility check only: it is onsets, not stressed syllables, and one metre, not three [OUR INFERENCE]. The beat-2 against beat-3 split has three competing predictions. The 4/4 beat-4 analogy says beat 3 of 3/4 carries more onsets than beat 2; Jacoby's long-last bias says beat 3 is avoided by stressed syllables but over-represented as a pickup onset; and decaying listener salience says beat 2 outranks beat 3 throughout [OUR INFERENCE]. These are separable only by counting onsets and stressed syllables apart, which the held 3/4 lead sheets and Essen 3/4 allow (749.252) [OUR INFERENCE]. For 6/8, the same reasoning predicts slot 1 above slot 4 well above the rest, and the pre-beat quavers (3 and 6) above 2 and 5, against which the team's 6/8 first-beat stress of 0.26 in the engine sits under the 6/8 lead sheets' median of 0.509 (749.231) [OUR INFERENCE; TEAM FIGURE]. Text-setting adds the only figure resembling beat-1 alignment in triple-adjacent material, and it is weak: one Schubert song set to German and Turkish texts gave beat-1 alignment of 75 to 79% in German against 17 to 33% in Turkish, with the source's abstract and appendix disagreeing [MEASURED, questionable] ([Aspects of pitch in textsetting 2024](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/psicl-2024-0031/pdf)).

### Arriving one division early

No published study reports an anticipation or syncopation rate for melodies in 3/4 or 6/8, in any style. Every measured rate is 4/4 or 2/4. In American popular recordings from 1890 to 1939, one sampled bar from each of 437 works showed syncopations per passage roughly doubling, from just over one to nearly two, with no metre split in the summary read [MEASURED; secondary summary] ([Huron & Ommen 2006](https://shanahdt.github.io/mus-345-445/emp_methods_workshop/syncopation.html)). "Fourth-position" syncopation, which tends to be anticipatory, "appears to be absent from published 19th-century vocal music, British or American", first appearing in notation in ragtime around 1900 [MEASURED; abstract only] ([Temperley 2021](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/popular-music/article/origins-of-syncopation-in-american-popular-music/941F3ADA4C6254F1D26025152F4806F3)). Rock's by-target anticipation shares (beat 1 0.504, beat 3 0.554, beat 2 0.439, beat 4 0.351; 0.772 of quaver-level syncopations early) are in the last report and were measured after 17 of 100 songs were dropped for their metre [MEASURED; not rechecked] ([Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/tan-lustig-temperley.pdf)). Jembe drumming is the measured extreme, with the pre-beat slot out-numbering the beat [MEASURED] ([London, Polak & Jacoby 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-016-1093-7)). The team's figures are the only metre-specific ones: Hooktheory pop 3/4 puts 0.68 of onsets on a beat against 0.84 for the lead-sheet waltz (749.233), the deduced pop 3/4 stress table is 0.50 first beat, 0.29 another beat, 0.21 off the beat, the off-beat mark is 0.26, and the engine's early entry fired at 4/4's 0.2 outside 4/4 until 749.253 [TEAM FIGURE].

The measured range of "one division early" therefore runs from about zero (nineteenth-century song, and by inference Essen folk) through 0.35 to 0.55 of stressed beat targets (rock) to "more often than the beat" (jembe), which is why it must be a style operator with a weight on a fixed tree, not a metre constant [OUR INFERENCE]. This supports 749.253's ruling that early entry outside 4/4 fires only at the metre table's own share [OUR INFERENCE]. The perceptual constraint on that operator is that the anticipation lands one division early at a level the listener tracks, near 150 to 300 ms, so in slow pop 6/8 a semiquaver shift is as plausible as a quaver one [OUR INFERENCE]. By the rock pattern, most pop 3/4 anticipations should land just before beat 1, which would lower the first-beat stress share, consistent with the syncopated pop windows pulling K3 down (749.236) [OUR INFERENCE]. The clips' 0.32 off-beat onset share is not all anticipation; a beat-empty test is needed to separate it [OUR INFERENCE].

### Distinct bar rhythms per section

No published count of distinct bar rhythms per section exists for any melody corpus in any metre. The nearest measured numbers are pooled drum MIDI (48,176 files, 4,765,947 bar tokens, 656,798 bar types, 6.23% one-off types, a roughly scale-free frequency distribution, and no per-metre counts) [MEASURED] ([Mauch & Dixon 2012](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/163_ISMIR_2012.pdf)); pop melodies in which more than 90% of songs have two or three distinct phrases [MEASURED; not rechecked] ([Dai, Zhang & Dannenberg 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.07518)); and older American songs in which 7% of phrases use the same rhythm in every bar [MEASURED; from the last report] ([Dai et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.00182)). Folk-similarity work shows metre notation dominating rhythmic dissimilarity between variants of a tune, with 3/4 and 6/8 subgroups ranking far better than whole tune families (mean rank 6.81 against 45.51; 11.08 against 42.94) [MEASURED] ([Volk et al. 2007](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2007/paper/000293.pdf)), and German children's songs in Essen carry "extensive duplication of musical material" without a figure [MEASURED, qualitative] ([Conklin & Anagnostopoulou 2006](https://www.ehu.eus/cs-ikerbasque/conklin/papers/1526-5528-2006-18-03-0285.pdf)). The team's counts stand alone: a section's first 16 sung bars use a median of 10 bar rhythms in the engine (middle half 8 to 12) against 6 in pop 3/4 clips (4 to 8), bars on a rhythm already sung are 0.375 against 0.625 (749.259), and a two-bar unit repeats the one two back 0.231 of the time in the clips against 0.012 to 0.014 in the engine (749.257) [TEAM FIGURE].

Those paired team figures are one statistic: with B sung bars and D distinct rhythms, the share on a rhythm already sung is (B − D)/B, and D = 6 or 10 at B = 16 gives exactly 0.625 or 0.375, so a target on either fixes the other [OUR INFERENCE]. An SRDC section with shared S and R rhythms, about 2.5 distinct bars per four-bar phrase and one shared cadence bar gives D of about 5.5 to 6.5, matching the clips; an engine drawing each line's window freshly from hundreds of candidates tends toward 8 to 12, matching what is observed [OUR INFERENCE]. Three cross-cultural findings point the same way: priors concentrate on a handful of categories, three ratios cover 86% of world bigrams, and cyclic traditions repeat a fixed stroke pattern, so the engine's 10 against 6 is a departure from a cross-cultural norm, not a quirk of the clips [OUR INFERENCE]. Chance coincidence differs by metre, since a quaver-grid bar has 2⁶ = 64 onset patterns in 3/4 or 6/8 against 2⁸ = 256 in 4/4, so targets must be set per metre [OUR INFERENCE].

## Text-setting across languages makes alignment strength a language weight

**The correction comes first.** Hayes & Kaun's figure, "fully 10.9% (760/6998) of the settings in our data set are 'headless'", describes settings in which the words are "slid over" to the right, "vacating the two initial positions": a late entry, not a line that starts on the strong beat with nothing before it [MEASURED; via summary, check §6.8 and example 38] ([Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)). The paper does not number grid positions, does not discuss anacrusis, and describes only "stealing" a position from the following line, an option "seldom taken" ([Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)). **The last report's sentence "about 89% begin with a pickup" should therefore be dropped.** If Hayes & Kaun's grid opens on a strong position, about one line in nine enters late in English folk chanting, close to the project table's 0.10 for lines beginning late (749.253's mark), but that is a coincidence to check, not a confirmation [OUR INFERENCE]. The same paper's consultants were asked to chant "in whatever seemed to them to be an appropriate binary rhythm", and those who offered ternary rhythms "were gently corrected", so the classic English data exclude triple time by design [MEASURED] ([Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)). Its phrase-edge counts do travel: mismatch is about 11% before a clitic-group boundary, 3% before a phonological-phrase boundary and 1% before an intonational-phrase boundary (334 of 2,973; 75 of 2,512; 12 of 1,114) [MEASURED counts; percentages derived, OUR INFERENCE] ([Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)).

The only formal statement about ternary text-setting is that verse builds ternary measures by splitting a binary beat, while music and dance treat them as "often irreducibly ternary", and that English matches stresses to strong beats "across the board" while French does so only at line ends [THEORY ONLY] ([Kiparsky 2020](https://web.stanford.edu/~kiparsky/Papers/Metrics/LI06_Kiparsky_update.pdf)). In a Spanish 6/8 folk song the tactus sits at the dotted crotchet, strong positions are "never violated" (always filled), and prosodic accent is "disregarded in favour of the musical accentuation", while in English agreement is "virtually total" [MEASURED, two-song case study, no rates] ([Rodríguez-Vázquez 2006](https://f-book.com/arsmetrica/wp-content/uploads/2013/01/Rodriguez-Vazquez_2006_The-metrics-of-folk-song.pdf)). Stress-metre alignment in children's songs (4/4) is 1.518 in English, 1.586 in Russian and 0.53 in Turkish, with Eastern Armenian not aligned [MEASURED; conference abstract] ([Domene Moreno, Kabak & Sazhumyan](https://sfb1252.uni-koeln.de/sites/sfb_1252/user_upload/ICPL3/Abstracts_ICPL3/Prominence-Conference-3_Moreno_et._al..pdf)). French traditional song shows stress mismatch "around 35-40%" [MEASURED; relayed from Temperley & Temperley 2013, whose original could not be fetched] ([Lee, Brown & Müllensiefen 2017](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/20574/1/LeeBrownMullensiefen_MLE_MusicPerception_2017.pdf)). Hindi-Urdu pop at 95 to 115 BPM showed no preference for setting presumed-stressed heavy syllables on longer notes or the strongest beat [MEASURED; preliminary presentation] ([Charan, Rehan & Narkar 2025](https://rehan-muh.github.io/assets/files/presentations/asa_2025_hindi.pdf)). Tone languages trade metre for pitch direction: Cantonese pop has 77% similar tone-melody motion against Mandarin's 38%, with tone constraints "often ignored or relaxed at phrase edges" [MEASURED; metre not analysed] ([Kirby 2023](https://www.phonetik.uni-muenchen.de/~jkirby/docs/kirby2023comparative.pdf)), and Vietnamese and Thai pop sit near 77% [MEASURED; partly derived from printed counts] ([Ladd & Kirby](https://www.phonetik.uni-muenchen.de/~jkirby/docs/ladd2018tone.pdf)).

Two sources speak directly to threes. In 27 Finnish songbook songs with 4/4, 3/4 and 6/8 most typical, **light syllables took only 18% of the long first notes of asymmetric 2:1 pairs (n = 347), against 32% of long notes in symmetric pairs and 50% of short notes** (χ² = 34.11, p < .001), and nucleus weight added 1.18 log-odds of a long note [MEASURED] ([Arjava & Kentner 2022](https://user.uni-frankfurt.de/~kentner/ArjavaKentner2022.pdf)). In Seto runosong (62 recordings, 1,150 verses), singers regroup an eight-note isochronous line as 2+2+3 in a mean 44% of lines (SD 20), varying widely by singer [MEASURED] ([Oras 2019](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/opli-2019-0031/pdf)). Neighbouring evidence on line openings comes from 3,724 Dutch folk songs (176,708 syllables, melisma 4.46%): content words strongly avoid opposite stress and prominence contours (log ratio −4.14 and −3.60), function words do not avoid the stressed-and-prominent pairing (1.08), and in the phrase-initial domain "none of the alignments exceeds the under-representation threshold", so line openings are free [MEASURED; no metre split] ([De Castro-Arrazola 2018, ch. 6](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2951724/view)). Estonian regilaul shows broken (stress-mismatched) lines from about 8% in Kullamaa to about 50% in Karelian and Ingrian song [MEASURED, partly relayed] ([Sarv](https://www.folklore.ee/folklore/vol7/maripar.htm)), and English chart singers mismatch 1.3% of pairs overall (0.6% at the tactus) in Standard Southern British against 3.2% in Multicultural London English [MEASURED; time signatures not stated] ([Lee, Brown & Müllensiefen 2017](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/20574/1/LeeBrownMullensiefen_MLE_MusicPerception_2017.pdf)).

Perception studies are all duple or unstated in metre. British listeners chose stress-matched settings 82.5% and 67.9% of the time by dialect group [MEASURED] ([Lee, Brown & Müllensiefen 2017](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/20574/1/LeeBrownMullensiefen_MLE_MusicPerception_2017.pdf)); 135 Dutch listeners preferred aligned over misaligned settings .72 to .79 of the time [MEASURED] ([De Castro-Arrazola 2018, ch. 7](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2951725/view)); stress-matched sung bisyllables were recognised about 84.7% against 75.5% of the time, a "modest" effect [MEASURED; percentages derived] ([Johnson, Huron & Collister 2014](https://core.ac.uk/download/20329824.pdf)); and EEG at 150 BPM over a duple click showed alignment effects on alpha and beta power [MEASURED] ([Gordon, Magne & Large 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00352/full)). No ERP or EEG study of stress-beat mismatch in triple or compound metre was found.

The engine can read these as weights on two separable constraints, "fill the strong position" (high in Spanish and English) and "the strong position's syllable is stressed" (high in English, low in Spanish and French except at line end), the same split as Hayes's "no empty S" and "no stressless syllable in S" [OUR INFERENCE]. Measured alignment ranks Russian and English above Turkish, French (line ends only), then Armenian and Hindi-Urdu (none), so English weights stay as fitted and other languages scale down [OUR INFERENCE]. Line and phrase edges are where mismatch is tolerated in three independent sources (Dutch, Cantonese, English chanting), which supports scoring a line's first one or two syllables more leniently and keeping the stress score on line-internal stresses (749.252, 749.254) [OUR INFERENCE]. Arjava & Kentner give the ternary form of "strong is long": in 3/4 and 6/8 a stressed heavy syllable prefers the crotchet of a crotchet-quaver pair, and in 6/8 that means the dotted-quarter beat's crotchet, not the following quaver [OUR INFERENCE]. The Seto 2+2+3 regrouping is a documented sung precedent for treating hemiola or regrouping as a style operator applied after placement [OUR INFERENCE]. Since all listening tests used duple grids, whether a stress on beat 2 or 3 of 3/4 is heard as a mismatch at all is untested; a small forced-choice test on engine output would be new evidence [OUR INFERENCE]. No measured text-setting data were found for German Lieder by metre, Turkish aksak songs, Greek or Balkan 7/8 dance songs, Arabic zajal or iqāʿ, Persian song, Hindi film song by tāl, or Yoruba and Akan song.

## Conclusion

The world record changes what the engine's open numbers mean. The beat-1 share, the anticipation rate and the bar-rhythm vocabulary are not properties of 3/4 or 6/8; they are style weights on a grid that every tradition shares, and two traditions (rūpak's light sam, jembe's dense pre-beat) show that a strong, dense "one" is a choice rather than a law [OUR INFERENCE]. That reframes K3 from a metre constant to a style-and-tempo figure, gives the beat-2 against beat-3 question a concrete mechanism to test (the long interval closes a group, so beat 3 leans forward), and turns the engine's 10 bar rhythms against pop's 6 into a departure from a cross-cultural habit of few categories rather than a local fault [OUR INFERENCE].

The literature read cannot close any of these gaps, because its triple-metre numbers are either unpublished or locked in figures that were not digitised. The fastest route is the project's own data: the beat profiles and beat-empty anticipations in Essen 3/4 and 6/8, the Hooktheory clips and the lead sheets are hours of counting, not months of reading, and they would supply triple-metre figures that no source read publishes [OUR INFERENCE]. Indexing every level rule by the level nearest 600 ms is the single change most likely to let one rule set carry from 4/4 to 3/4, 6/8 and later to aksak without new hand-set numbers [OUR INFERENCE].
