# Music shares language's grid, not its dictionary

## Has anyone studied music as a language, and explored it fully?

Yes, many times and in depth, but nobody has explored it fully, and the answer that came back is mixed. The work runs from Deryck Cooke's 1959 book *The Language of Music*, through Leonard Bernstein's 1973 Harvard lectures and Lerdahl and Jackendoff's 1983 *Generative Theory of Tonal Music*, to brain scans in 2023 and a recording study of song and speech in 55 languages in 2024 [MEASURED that these programmes exist and what they tested; sources in the table]. Music turns out to share one thing with language in detail. That thing is a nested pattern of strong and weak beats, the same kind of pattern that places stress in spoken words ([Katz & Pesetsky](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/000959/current.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY for the formal identity; MEASURED that listeners and composers treat beats by graded strength ([van der Weij, Pearce & Honing 2017](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/13011148/fpsyg_08_00824.pdf))]. Music does not share a dictionary. No study has shown that a musical phrase means one thing the way a word does ([Wikipedia, *The Language of Music*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Language_of_Music)) [MEASURED, weak tests]. The brain mostly handles music beside language, not with the same machinery ([Chen et al. 2023](https://www.mit.edu/~hopekean/files/Music.pdf)) [MEASURED]. Across cultures, people agree on a song's energy, dance against lullaby, far better than on its mood, and "major sounds happy, minor sounds sad" is learned ([Yurdum et al. 2023](https://escholarship.org/content/qt69d86235/qt69d86235.pdf); [Smit et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9242494)) [MEASURED]. So "music is a universal language with dialects" is right about rhythm's skeleton and about energy, half right about feeling, and wrong if it means music has words [OUR INFERENCE].

## Fifty years of grammars kept the beat grid and dropped the meanings

### Every testable claim about time held up; every claim about meaning failed

Bernstein mapped music onto Chomsky's three levels. He derived a "musical phonology" from the harmonic series, cast syntax as deep structure turned into surface structure by transformations (deletion above all), treated semantics as metaphor, and called tonal pitch relations an innate "substantive universal" ([Wikipedia, The Unanswered Question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Unanswered_Question_(lecture_series))) [THEORY ONLY]. Keiler (1978) judged the lectures not "a well-conceived or rigorous contribution", noting that "there is absolutely no relationship of intervallic content between [the Balinese] scale and the overtone series" ([same page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Unanswered_Question_(lecture_series))) [MEASURED counter-example, relayed by an encyclopedia]. Lerdahl says he and Jackendoff started from Chomsky's framing in the early 1970s but rejected "a literal transfer of linguistic concepts", which he says Bernstein attempted ([Lerdahl 2009](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/58812885e6f2e1da63d1291b/t/589177819f745677005560ea/1485928321911/Genesis+and+Architecture+of+the+GTTM+Project.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY].

Cooke's book is "an often cited representative of the expressionist theory of art" ([Wikipedia, The Language of Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Language_of_Music)) [THEORY ONLY]. The tests are thin. A pilot with **22 non-musician students** (Spender 2004) found they did not hear Cooke's phrases as he described them, relayed in garbled form ([same page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Language_of_Music)) [MEASURED, weak; check the study against its original]. A second test drew four bipolar dimensions from Cooke's descriptions (Sorrow to Joy, Continuation to Finality, Constancy to Outburst, Submissiveness to Assertiveness) and found significant listener agreement "generally consistent" with Cooke, but concluded the results give "only limited support" to the theory ([scite record](https://scite.ai/reports/the-language-of-music-bENPx1)) [MEASURED, no numbers on the page; authorship unconfirmed, possibly Kaminska & Woolf 2000]. Gabriel's 1978 test, the main early one, was not reached.

GTTM split rhythm into separate grouping and metre components and pitch into time-span and prolongational reductions, with well-formedness rules defining possible structures and preference rules choosing among them. Lerdahl calls the grouping/metre split the innovation most theorists adopted, and says the theory sought principles "unvarying beneath a capacity's many different cultural manifestations" ([Lerdahl 2009](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/58812885e6f2e1da63d1291b/t/589177819f745677005560ea/1485928321911/Genesis+and+Architecture+of+the+GTTM+Project.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY]. He reports that grouping preference rules behave as predicted (Deliège 1987; Frankland & Cohen 2004), that rhythm and pitch dissociate cognitively, and that tension models have been validated, while the interaction principle "may still be too technical to test" ([Lerdahl 2009](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/58812885e6f2e1da63d1291b/t/589177819f745677005560ea/1485928321911/Genesis+and+Architecture+of+the+GTTM+Project.pdf)) [MEASURED as he reports it; the cited studies were not opened]. A retrospective credits GTTM with "introducing the 'cognitive paradigm'" but says it "has not been widely acknowledged by analysts", that statistical models have largely replaced rule-based ones, and that its "claims of universality and innateness [are] based on intuition rather than cross-cultural research" ([Legacy of GTTM, *Danish Yearbook of Musicology*](https://tidsskrift.dk/dym/article/view/165844)) [THEORY ONLY; abstract only]. Lerdahl's later list of writings shows no rhythm-specific revision of the metre rules ([Lerdahl, writings](https://fredlerdahl.com/writings)) [MEASURED as a list; none of the later works read].

Jackendoff's own verdict, relayed by Katz and Pesetsky, is that language and music share many general traits but only one detailed formal one, "namely metrical structure", and that "most of what they share does not indicate a particularly close relation" ([Katz & Pesetsky](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/000959/current.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY; Jackendoff 2009 not read directly]. Katz and Pesetsky go furthest the other way. Their identity thesis holds that "all formal differences between language and music are a consequence of differences in their fundamental building blocks", that music has a syntax of "iterated, recursive, binary Merge" identified with GTTM's prolongational reduction, that GTTM's time-span reduction is a musical prosody, and that GTTM's metrical grid is formally the linguistic stress grid of Liberman & Prince and Hayes, with only the "phonetic implementation" differing ([Katz & Pesetsky](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/000959/current.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY]. They concede that language's building blocks, "arbitrary pairings of sound and meaning", "have no obvious counterpart in music", and the data they cite support GTTM's prolongational structure, not the identity as such ([Katz & Pesetsky](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/000959/current.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY]. Their footnote 14 flags that strophic tunes fitted to several verses raise a "text setting problem" parallel to the musical reduction problem ([Katz & Pesetsky](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/000959/current.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY], which is the engine's open problem in 749.251 to .254 ([fixlist](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)).

Fabb and Halle hold that "the defining feature of verse is not rhythm, but lineation", and build metre bottom-up as bracketed grids under parameters with readjustment rules on top; ternary groups must be left- or right-headed at every level, which excludes amphibrachs ([Kiparsky review](https://web.stanford.edu/~kiparsky/Papers/Metrics/closure/fabb_halle_review.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY]. Kiparsky finds the readjustment rules make the theory "largely unfalsifiable" and finds nothing in it that "cannot be expressed as well or better" in constraint-based frameworks ([Kiparsky review](https://web.stanford.edu/~kiparsky/Papers/Metrics/closure/fabb_halle_review.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY]. Their music chapter ([Halle & Fabb, Strathclyde record](https://pureportal.strath.ac.uk/en/publications/grouping-in-the-stressing-of-words-in-metrical-verse-and-in-music/)) could not be read; the abstract gives none of its claims.

The constraint tradition did collect data. Hayes credits Halle & Lerdahl (1993) with "the first explicit proposal for modeling" textsetting and recasts it as ranked conflict among stress-to-strong, lapse, compression and phrase-to-line alignment ([Hayes, Textsetting](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)) [THEORY ONLY]. His maximum-entropy version, fitted to Hayes & Kaun's chanted English folk lines (9 consultants, 670 lines; 425 lines in the simulation, about 2.2 distinct settings and 117 candidate alignments per line), uses constraints that Hayes calls restatements of GTTM's metrical preference rules 3, 4 and 5 ([Hayes, maxent textsetting slides](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesTuftsPresentation.pdf)) [MEASURED; figures to check]. The constraint list, the 70.6% line match and the formula are in the [Mathematics report](/home/claude/wm_context/Mathematics%20of%20fitting%20words%20to%20metre.md) and are not repeated.

Ruwet's paradigmatic analysis cut a piece into small units to let its syntax emerge "without reference to any external sources or norms" ([Wikipedia, Nicolas Ruwet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Ruwet)) [THEORY ONLY]. Nattiez, his student, split any musical sign into a poietic level (the maker's process), a neutral level (the trace, the notes) and an esthesic level (the listener's reconstruction): "an immanent description is an analysis of the neutral level" ([Wikipedia, Neutral level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_level)) [THEORY ONLY; secondary source]. His critique of GTTM's "pertinence" (1997) is listed but was not read ([Wikipedia, GTTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_theory_of_tonal_music)). Cross gives the strongest positive account of why music lacks a lexicon: its meaning has "floating intentionality", so participants "abstract the meanings that they feel are expressed" without collision, making it "a foundationally non-conflictual medium" for shared intentionality, to which model-theoretic semantics "have little relevance", while "the boundaries between language and music are probably illusory" ([Cross 2012, CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/159579333.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY].

The pattern across these programmes is clean. Every programme that made a testable claim about *time* (GTTM's metre, Hayes's textsetting, the metric hierarchy) has held up, and every programme that made a claim about *meaning* (Bernstein's semantics, Cooke's vocabulary) or about *one shared syntax* (the identity thesis) is theory only or contradicted [OUR INFERENCE, from the sources above]. For Luthier the literature sorts itself: borrow the grid and the preference-rule format, not the semantics [OUR INFERENCE].

### The grid makes beats 2 and 3 of a waltz equal, so preference rules must decide

GTTM's metrical well-formedness rules say every attack sits on a beat of the smallest level, every beat at one level is a beat at all smaller levels, "at each metrical level, strong beats are spaced either two or three beats apart", and beats at the tactus and above are equally spaced ([Wikipedia, GTTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_theory_of_tonal_music)) [THEORY ONLY]. Its preference rules then choose: MPR1 parallelism, MPR2 strong beat early ("weakly prefer"), MPR3 event, MPR4 stress, MPR5 length (a strong beat at the start of a long note), MPR6 bass, MPR7 cadence, MPR8 suspension, MPR9 time-span interaction, and MPR10 binary regularity ("every other beat is strong") ([Wikipedia, GTTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_theory_of_tonal_music)) [THEORY ONLY]. Lerdahl and Jackendoff call MPR2 weak, and Temperley keeps it ([Biamonte 2024](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.4/mto.24.30.4.biamonte.html)) [THEORY ONLY].

Read literally, the grid decides less than the engine assumes. A 3/4 bar built by those rules has three levels (eighth, crotchet, bar): **beat 1 carries three dots and beats 2 and 3 carry two each, so the grid makes beats 2 and 3 exactly equal**. In 6/8, eighth 1 carries three dots, eighth 4 two, and eighths 2, 3, 5 and 6 one each [OUR INFERENCE, derived from the rules; no source states it for 3/4 or 6/8]. That fits the one fitted English weight table in triple time, from the Mathematics report: in Haydn's "The Wanderer" (one 3/4 song, 130 syllables) maximum-entropy weights came out at 0.00 for either kind of syllable on beats 2 and 3 ([Girardi & Plag 2019](https://www.anglistik.hhu.de/fileadmin/redaktion/Fakultaeten/Philosophische_Fakultaet/Anglistik_und_Amerikanistik/Ang3_Linguistics/Dateien/Detailseiten/Plag/2019/Girardi_Plag_Textsetting_2019-09-19.pdf), via the [Mathematics report](/home/claude/wm_context/Mathematics%20of%20fitting%20words%20to%20metre.md)) [MEASURED, one song, values that look pinned; check]. The preference rules then give four separable predictions [OUR INFERENCE for all four]. MPR5 says a 2:1 bar (minim then crotchet) is the stable 3/4 figure, and that a long note starting on beat 2 pulls strength there, which is the triple sarabande's "dual-aspect" metre with extra weight on beat 2 ([Biamonte 2024](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.4/mto.24.30.4.biamonte.html)) [sarabande claim THEORY ONLY]. MPR2 with grouping says short pickups beat long ones, so a one-beat pickup on beat 3 should outnumber a two-beat pickup on beats 2 and 3, and eighth 6 should outnumber eighths 5 and 6 in 6/8. MPR10 is broken at the crotchet level in every 3/4 bar, so GTTM treats triple as marked and predicts duple grouping above the bar, two- and four-bar groups rather than three. MPR1 says a line sung again should keep its source's metrical placement.

The MPR2 prediction lands where report 1's long-last bias landed by a different route: **stressed syllables favour beat 2 over beat 3, and unstressed pickups favour beat 3** ([report 1](/home/claude/zeneng-pipeline/reports/world_music/1_rhythm_and_metre.md), from [Jacoby et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132990/)) [OUR INFERENCE; two independent theories agreeing is not data]. The data that could settle it exist but were not read. Palmer & Krumhansl (1990) found listener ratings and corpus event frequencies in 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 and 6/8 reflecting the metrical hierarchy, but the per-position values exist only in their figures ([Palmer & Pfordresher](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/PalmerandPfordresher2000.pdf); [van der Weij et al. 2017](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/13011148/fpsyg_08_00824.pdf)) [MEASURED, relayed]. In Bach, Mozart, Beethoven and Chopin, tonally stable pitch classes and metrically stable positions co-occur beyond chance "across composers, time signatures, and modes" ([Prince & Schmuckler 2014](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-tonal-metric-hierarchy-A-corpus-analysis/991005541603407891)) [MEASURED; abstract only; which metres were counted is not stated there].

Statistical work shows the grid is learned per culture. A probabilistic metre model trained on **4,966 German Essen songs** classified metre at **71%** (from about 38% with no context), with lower information content on its own culture's rhythms than on Chinese Essen rhythms; on 211 one-bar patterns, the largest disagreement between a 3/4-biased and a 6/8-biased prior was between those two metres themselves, with 83 rhythms read differently ([van der Weij, Pearce & Honing 2017](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/13011148/fpsyg_08_00824.pdf)) [MEASURED; figures to check]. The authors argue, citing Malian drumming, that "metrical accent need not equal onset likelihood" ([same](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/13011148/fpsyg_08_00824.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY on that point]. Hayes's maxent fit adds two findings carried over from verse: a stressed-then-stressless pair must match the grid more strictly inside one word, and a stressless-then-stressed pair more strictly at the end of a major phrase ([Hayes slides](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesTuftsPresentation.pdf)) [MEASURED; not split by metre]. Andrade reads syncopation as "a true rhythmic anticipation of the thesis", and Temperley ties second-position syncopation to British and fourth-position syncopation to African-American music; Biamonte gives no counts and "can find no studies of similar patterns in instrumental art music" ([Biamonte 2024](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.4/mto.24.30.4.biamonte.html)) [THEORY ONLY]. Read into 6/8, the fourth-position analogue is eighth 6 (before the bar) and eighth 3 (before beat 2), and the second-position analogue is eighths 2 and 5 [OUR INFERENCE].

### Hierarchy is universal; recursion is an argument about Western harmony

The review that set the agenda lists music's structure-building operations as "hierarchical organisation and tree structure, and nested dependencies and insertions", argues these are "more naturally expressed using CFGs than with FSAs", and reads tonal modulation as recursive embedding, but does not place music on the Chomsky hierarchy ([Zuidema et al., arXiv 1901.05180](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.05180)) [THEORY ONLY; the revised version was read because the 2015 *Phil. Trans. B* page was blocked]. It concedes that finite data make finite-state and context-free models hard to tell apart, that strictly binary grammaticality "is problematic in the light of real-world data", that the discussion has "focused on Western classical music", and that "more cross-cultural research... is required" ([same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.05180)) [THEORY ONLY]. For birdsong it reports "little evidence" of abilities beyond finite-state, with claims of context-free learning in songbirds made and refuted ([same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.05180)) [MEASURED, relayed]. Whether listeners track long-range dependencies in everyday listening "is a debated topic" ([same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.05180)) [MEASURED, relayed]. Honing and Zuidema reject Fitch's "faith in trees as all-compassing model of language and music", say true hierarchy is "very difficult to demonstrate" in either domain, that metrical analyses can be circular, and that a continuous system "may approximate the behavior of a discrete idealization to an arbitrary degree", with rhythm as their example ([Honing & Zuidema 2014](https://mcg.uva.nl/mcg-2023/papers/honing-zuidema-2014.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY; Fitch 2014 read only through this comment].

Three hierarchies should be kept apart [OUR INFERENCE]. The metrical grid is hierarchical but shallow, periodic, and evidenced across cultures (report 1). Grouping, from motive to section, is hierarchical and shared with vision, with surface cues shared across cultures (next subsection). Prolongational or harmonic recursion is argued only for Western tonal and jazz harmony. Only the first two are candidates for rules stated once across metres and cultures. Luthier's form layer (verse, chorus, bridge, lines sung again) is a two- or three-level hierarchy that a finite-state device with memory can generate, so the recursion debate barely touches it, and Honing and Zuidema's continuous-approximation point supports graded weights over binary well-formedness, which is already the project's practice [OUR INFERENCE].

### Outside the West, grouping travels and the weights flip

Applications of GTTM outside Western tonal music are few and mostly analytic. Clarke applied it to one ālāp in rāg Yaman: grouping well-formedness "clearly has a potentially universal application"; time-span reduction works once harmonic and metrical rules are "switched off" and replaced by "Ālāp Preference Rules"; prolongational reduction is "debatable"; the metrical rules do not apply to "the ametrical, monophonic context of an ālāp"; and "we should be wary of construing this as evidence for wholesale universalism" ([Clarke 2017, MTO 23.3](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/101/107)) [THEORY ONLY; no listener or corpus data]. The one measured cross-cultural grouping study had **16 Arab listeners (11 professionals) and 10 Europeans** segment a 1973 ʿūd taqsīm. The median number of segmentations was 7.5 against 4.5, and 40% of Europeans made two or fewer against 19% of Arabs. Both groups cut at pauses and register changes, Europeans also at contour turns, and Arabs at "subtle modal changes that often go unnoticed by the Europeans" ([Ayari & McAdams 2003](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/v118rj30z)) [MEASURED; figures to check; GTTM's rules not scored formally].

Metre's preference weights do not travel. "Most Balinese music has an end-accented character", with the first note of each trompong pair "in a subordinate metric position" ([Tenzer 2024](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.4/mto.24.30.4.tenzer.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY, descriptive]. In rūpak tāl the first beat is the least subdivided main beat, about 28% against over 95% for the others ([Clayton 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/462/60), via [report 1](/home/claude/zeneng-pipeline/reports/world_music/1_rhythm_and_metre.md)) [MEASURED]. Kippen and Bel's QAVAID inferred tabla qāʿida grammars, regular and context-free, from expert performances and revised them by informants' accept or reject judgements, but the page read reports no acceptance rates, notes "a limited number of examples", and a reviewer warns that segmenting tabla phrases is "not analogous to segmenting words in spoken languages" ([Bol Processor, QAVAID](https://bolprocessor.org/qavaid/)) [MEASURED, weak]. Becker & Becker's 1979 grammar of the Javanese srepegan was searched for and not reached.

So grouping transfers (proximity, pauses, register, a long note at the end), metre's well-formedness transfers wherever music is metred (spacing in 2s and 3s), metre's preference weights do not (MPR2 flips in Balinese music, beat one is light in rūpak), and pitch reduction needs idiom-specific rules [OUR INFERENCE, from the four sources above]. That split is the one the writer proposes: rules once for the grid and grouping, weights by style for where the weight sits and how strongly [OUR INFERENCE]. Ayari and McAdams's experts segmenting more, and on subtler cues, suggests line-boundary rules should rest on the culture-free cues (rest, long note, register change) with style cues such as cadence pitch and mode added by weight [OUR INFERENCE].

### Music has a lexicon of forms, not of meanings

Music has no lexicon of sound-meaning pairs ([Katz & Pesetsky](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/000959/current.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY]. It does have a vocabulary of forms. In the Meertens Annotated Corpus (360 Dutch folk melodies in 26 tune families), experts rated motifs "obviously similar" between family members in **88.3%** of cases, ahead of lyrics (84.8%), local rhythm (64.9%), global contour (63.4%) and local contour (54.9%), and concluded that "the recurrence of short characteristic motifs is most relevant for the perception of similarity". Yet motifs were the single classification key in only 9.3% of cases and global rhythm in 22.4%, agreement was κ = 0.74, and judgements were "in most cases a multi-dimensional process" ([Volk & van Kranenburg](https://scispace.com/pdf/melodic-similarity-among-folk-songs-an-annotation-study-on-596tmwscyb.pdf)) [MEASURED; figures to check; journal and year not given in the copy read]. Treitler applied the Parry-Lord oral-formulaic model to plainchant in 1974 ([Carleton bibliography](https://jazztheory.ssac.carleton.ca/view.php?id=617); [Wikipedia, Leo Treitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Treitler)) [THEORY ONLY; title only, content not read]. Bod's memory-based parser, which learned folk phrasing from stored fragments, is in the earlier report ([Grammar of great music](/home/claude/reports/Grammar%20of%20great%20music.md)). The engine's own phrasebook failed where kept windows were few; its "place and step" grammar got averages but not rhythms, the pickup bar behaved as "a word of its own", and "bars as words" passed in Essen 6/8 cells 4 to 11 but missed in pop 3/4's middle ([fixlist 749.239](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)) [MEASURED, team count].

The evidence supports a two-layer lexicon: short rhythm cells as a family's stable key, and motifs (pitch plus rhythm) as its strongest resemblance [OUR INFERENCE, from Volk & van Kranenburg]. An oral formula is defined by its slot in the line, not by meaning, which fits the engine's window better than a word does [OUR INFERENCE]. A formula lexicon implies a small vocabulary used repeatedly within one song, which restates 749.259's 10 bar rhythms against 6 ([fixlist](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)): a phrasebook with no in-song memory behaves like a speaker who never repeats a word [OUR INFERENCE]. Because music lacks a semantic lexicon, a window can be judged only by form (metrical fit, stress fit, reuse), so stress fit belongs inside the window's score, as 749.236 proposed, not in a filter afterwards [OUR INFERENCE].

## The brain runs music beside language, not through it

### Interference studies say "shared", and imaging says "separate"

Patel's shared syntactic integration resource hypothesis (SSIRH) holds that music and language keep separate representations while "the mechanisms that perform online computations on those representations are partially overlapping" ([Chen et al. 2023](https://www.mit.edu/~hopekean/files/Music.pdf), relaying Patel) [THEORY ONLY]. The interference tests mostly support some overlap. Slevc, Rosenberg and Patel (2009) found that garden-path sentences interacted with out-of-key chords and not with a timbre change ([Perruchet & Poulin-Charronnat 2013](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/000942-challenging-prior-evidence-for-a-shared-syntactic-processor-for-language-and-music.pdf)) [MEASURED; original not read]. In sung sentences, the cost of object-extracted relative clauses was larger when the critical note was out of key than when it was in key or louder, a control for "any salient unexpected event" ([Fedorenko et al. 2009](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/64631)) [MEASURED; abstract only]. Harmonic closure judgements changed after a syntactically hard word but not after an arithmetic control or a semantic garden path, read as evidence for syntax-specific shared resources ([Kunert, Willems & Hagoort 2016](https://forms.mpi.nl/node/45380)) [MEASURED; abstract only]. A further paper reports by title that music and language syntax "interact in Broca's area" ([Kunert et al. 2015](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2225968_5/component/file_2225967/journal.pone.0141069.pdf?mode=tme)) [MEASURED claim; title and metadata only].

The challenge is direct. With **96 French undergraduates**, Perruchet and Poulin-Charronnat reproduced the Slevc interaction using *semantic* garden paths (F(1, 2169.7) = 4.017, p = .045; 576 against 549 ms), concluded this "invalidate[s] the experimental argument" for a syntax-specific resource, and proposed an attentional account, while noting several marginal effects ([Perruchet & Poulin-Charronnat 2013](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/000942-challenging-prior-evidence-for-a-shared-syntactic-processor-for-language-and-music.pdf)) [MEASURED]. Kunert's semantic control did not produce the effect ([Kunert, Willems & Hagoort 2016](https://forms.mpi.nl/node/45380)), so the two results conflict head on [MEASURED, conflicting]. A direct ERP replication of Patel et al. (1998), with **35 participants against 15**, found a similar late positivity at 500 to 800 ms for ungrammatical words and out-of-key chords (midline data favoured "no difference" about 6 to 1, lateral support weak at 1.46), but the original's right anterior temporal negativity for music did not replicate ([de Leeuw et al. 2019](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/1481/2089)) [MEASURED]. No large preregistered multi-lab replication of Slevc 2009 was found.

Imaging of individual brains points the other way. Language regions defined per subject (12 in the music experiment, 48 overall) responded weakly to intact against scrambled music, none surviving correction, and the left inferior frontal response was "barely above fixation" ([Fedorenko, Behr & Kanwisher 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3182706)) [MEASURED]. Across four experiments with **48 participants**, Chen and colleagues found music at or below fixation in language regions, songs *lower* than lyrics alone (d = −0.377), intact melodies lower than scrambled ones (d = −0.238), and a small sour-note effect (d = 0.186) that survived in no region after correction; the language-selective parts of Broca's area "did not respond to music", and music structure drove the domain-general multiple-demand network only under a well-formedness task ([Chen et al. 2023](https://www.mit.edu/~hopekean/files/Music.pdf); [UCL record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10164075/)) [MEASURED; figures to check]. **Three people with severe aphasia, at or near chance on sentence grammar, scored 89.4%, 94.4% and 97.8% on a melody well-formedness task against a control mean of 87.1%** ([Chen et al. 2023](https://www.mit.edu/~hopekean/files/Music.pdf)) [MEASURED; n = 3]. Koelsch places the generators of the ERAN, a response to irregular chords at about 150 to 350 ms, in pars opercularis (BA 44), and reports that it reduces the LAN to irregular words ([Koelsch 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00110/full)) [MEASURED claims relayed in a review]. Both can hold if BA 44 contains neighbouring domain-general and language-selective subregions that group averages blur, which is Chen et al.'s reading [THEORY ONLY].

### Song has its own cortex, and words ride on time while tunes ride on pitch

Decomposing fMRI responses to 165 natural sounds gave separate music-selective and speech-selective components in "distinct regions of non-primary auditory cortex" that acoustic features do not explain ([Norman-Haignere et al. 2015](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/112175)) [MEASURED; abstract only]. Intracranial recordings from **15 patients** found a component responding "nearly exclusively to music with singing", whose response to song exceeded the *sum* of its responses to speech and to instrumental music (p < 0.001), placed between the speech and music zones, poorly predicted by acoustics, and barely driven by modulation-matched synthetic song; only 7 single electrodes in 3 subjects were song-selective, so it is a population finding ([Norman-Haignere et al. 2022](https://mcdermottlab.mit.edu/papers/Norman-Haignere_etal_2022_song.pdf)) [MEASURED]. Albouy and colleagues crossed 10 sentences with 10 melodies into 100 a cappella songs in French and English: degrading **temporal** modulation impaired sentence recognition but not melody, degrading **spectral** modulation impaired melody but not sentences, in both languages (27 and 22 listeners), with sentence content decoded from left and melody from right auditory cortex; they flag that tone languages may differ ([Albouy et al. 2020](https://www.zlab.mcgill.ca/publications/docs/albouy_et_al_science_2020.pdf)) [MEASURED].

Lesion cases point the same way. The composer Shebalin kept composing after aphasia, and acquired amusia with intact speech is reported ([review, Psychology & Neuroscience](https://scielo.br/j/pn/a/qqKkdjxcYyQpQRcwdfnZvKS/?lang=en)) [MEASURED, case reports, relayed]. Congenital amusics need pitch changes of about 2 semitones against about 0.5 for controls while their timing detection is normal, about 30% have trouble with sentence-final pitch changes in speech, and a selective beat deficit with rhythm discrimination intact is described ([same review](https://scielo.br/j/pn/a/qqKkdjxcYyQpQRcwdfnZvKS/?lang=en)) [MEASURED, relayed; a familial recurrence figure there is garbled and must be checked against Peretz et al. 2007].

Four consequences follow [OUR INFERENCE for all]. Pitch and time are separable in the brain (amusia with rhythm intact, beat deafness with rhythm intact, Albouy's spectral and temporal split), so Luthier's separate pitch rules and rhythm rules, each with its own style weights, have a biological rationale. In song, the channel words and tune compete for is **time**, where syllables fall and how long they last, while pitch is mostly the tune's own, which makes stress-to-beat placement (K3, 749.231, .236, .251 to .254) the main word-tune interface and leaves pitch-word coupling mainly to tone languages. Song processed as more than speech plus music weakly supports judging a sung line as one unit, words and notes together, which is the form 749.236 named ("a window scored by its kept count and its stress fit together") ([fixlist](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)). And whether shared costs come from syntax or attention, a candidate soft rule is "don't stack a strong harmonic surprise on the hardest point of a sentence", untested in song.

## Song is slower and steadier than speech in 55 languages, not leapier

### The two universal markers of song are rate and pitch stability

Ozaki and colleagues had **75 coauthor-participants in 55 languages from 21 families** each record a song, a recitation of its lyrics, a spoken description and an instrumental version (300 recordings). In the confirmatory comparison (73 sets), song was **higher in pitch (D = 1.6), slower in onset rate (D = 1.6) and more stable in pitch (D = 0.7)**, all p < 1 × 10⁻⁵, while **timbral brightness and pitch interval size were statistically equivalent (D < 0.15)** ([Ozaki et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11095461); [Goldsmiths PDF](https://research.gold.ac.uk/36542/1/Ozaki_2024.pdf)) [MEASURED; figures to check]. Mean pitch was 289.9 against 199.9 Hz for women and 161.3 against 114.2 Hz for men ([same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11095461)) [MEASURED], about 6.4 and 6.0 semitones higher in song [OUR INFERENCE, 12·log₂ of the ratios]. Recited lyrics differed little from speech; instrumental melody differed from speech even more than song did, except in temporal rate, where singing and instrumental melody nearly matched ([same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11095461)) [MEASURED, exploratory]. Within singing, interval regularity rose as rate fell (r = −0.53), and **song nPVI correlated with the same person's speech nPVI at only r = 0.087**, against r = 0.52 between song and instrumental ([same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11095461)) [MEASURED, exploratory]. A replication on 418 recordings in 16 languages ran the same direction with weaker effects, for example pitch stability D = 0.3 ([same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11095461)) [MEASURED]. No absolute syllables per second were reported in the parts read.

Modulation spectra say the same in frequency terms. Speech peaks at 4.3 to 5.4 Hz across nine languages and music near 2 Hz; within-domain spectra correlate at 0.97 and 0.98 but speech and music at only 0.51, and in beat-driven excerpts peaks sit at 1, 2 and 4 times the beat rate ([Ding et al. 2017, preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/059683v1.full-text)) [MEASURED]. In 738 recordings from 21 societies, song carried more fine spectral (pitch) modulation and speech more temporal modulation near 6.2 Hz, each effect significant in **20 of 21 societies**, and a classifier cross-validated by fieldsite reached 84.5% using those features alone, with 98 other acoustic features adding nothing (p = 0.91) ([Albouy et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11156671)) [MEASURED]. Infant-directed song differs from adult-directed song across 21 societies by being softer, less rough and less variable in pitch at comparable median pitch, with classification at AUC 82% against 91% for speech; naive listeners in 187 countries detected it with d′ = 0.51 against 1.19 for speech ([Hilton et al. 2022](https://projects.iq.harvard.edu/sites/projects.iq.harvard.edu/files/epl/files/hilton_et_al-2022-nature_human_behaviour.pdf)) [MEASURED]. Sensitivity to song rose with linguistic relatedness (d′ 0.37, 0.65, 0.92 for unrelated, same family, same language) while staying above zero in every fieldsite; the two notes summarise this differently, one calling accuracy "robust to linguistic relatedness", so the reading must be checked ([same](https://projects.iq.harvard.edu/sites/projects.iq.harvard.edu/files/epl/files/hilton_et_al-2022-nature_human_behaviour.pdf)) [MEASURED; check]. Speech turns to song with repetition in all five stimulus languages tested, but **tonal-language listeners barely transformed** (Mandarin 0.08, Thai 0.20, against German 0.56 and Italian 0.88), and no acoustic parameter predicted the illusion, in a sample of only 20 listeners ([Jaisin et al. 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00662/full)) [MEASURED; preliminary].

For notated melody, the metre-free universal is **notes per second, not notes per bar** [OUR INFERENCE, from Ozaki and Albouy 2024]. If sung modulation sits near 1 to 3 Hz and syllable-bearing notes are mostly beat or half-beat events, a sung line carries roughly 1 to 4 syllables a second against speech's 5 to 8; the engine's 3/4 density of 2.09 notes a bar ([fixlist 749.226](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)) at a 0.6 s beat is about 1.16 notes a second, slow but inside that band [OUR INFERENCE; modulation rate is not note rate, so the band needs counting on notation]. Song is not "speech with bigger leaps": what makes a line sound sung is held, level notes and slowness, so the 3/4 range shortfall (749.227) should not be fixed by widening intervals, which agrees with report 2's move of phrase centres ([report 2](/home/claude/zeneng-pipeline/reports/world_music/2_melody_pitch_psychoacoustics.md)) [OUR INFERENCE]. Recitation sits with speech on both of Ozaki's main axes, and song's stability is *within* a note, not repetition *across* notes, so the 749.255 commonest-pitch excess is best read together with syllable rate: many syllables on one pitch, quickly, is recitation [OUR INFERENCE].

### Speech rhythm leaves a faint national accent on music, and almost none on a singer

English instrumental themes are more durationally contrastive than French ones (nPVI 46.91 against 40.90, p < 0.01, 318 themes from 16 composers), echoing the speech difference (66.99 against 49.27); about 76% and 79% of themes were in binary metre, and **binary and ternary themes did not differ (42.98 against 45.25, p = 0.28)**; the authors warn that the link "is not obligatory" ([Patel & Daniele 2003](https://ccc.inaoep.mx/~villasen/bib/ritmo%20lenguage%20y%20musical%20-Patel_Daniele_Cognition.pdf)) [MEASURED]. In 269 songs, English children's songs scored 40.43 against 33.8 for French (p = 0.026) while folk songs did not differ (43.6 against 40.1, p = 0.10); the set includes 3/4 and 6/8 songs but reports no nPVI by metre ([Hannon et al. 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00939)) [MEASURED]. Historical trends replicate for Austro-German composers and not for Italian or later French ones, and VanHandel called the language explanation "a post-hoc hypothesis" ([Hansen, Sadakata & Pearce, SysMus14](https://bjmh.gold.ac.uk/index.php/sysmus14/article/view/232)) [MEASURED, relayed]; her art-song work found French and German composers used largely the same metres ([London 2009](https://core.ac.uk/download/159576215.pdf)) [MEASURED, via commentary].

So nPVI is a **dialect weight**, a few points between national repertoires, varying with genre and era and barely tracking individual speech [OUR INFERENCE, from Patel & Daniele, Hannon and Ozaki]. Luthier should not derive its rhythm tables from the speech rhythm of the lyric's language; it should read them from sung corpora of the style [OUR INFERENCE]. Song and instrumental rhythm correlate at 0.52 and share rate, so the wordless Hooktheory clips are a defensible but imperfect proxy for sung rhythm, and 749.236's syncopated wordless pop windows fit Ozaki's finding that instrumental melody departs further from speech than song does [OUR INFERENCE]. Because nPVI did not separate binary from ternary themes, it is one of the few rhythm variables that might carry across 4/4, 3/4 and 6/8 unchanged, though long-short 6/8 figures will raise it mechanically, so any target needs checking by metre first [OUR INFERENCE].

### The felt beat is the one the body moves to

Seven-month-olds bounced on every second or every third beat of an ambiguous rhythm later preferred the version accented to match their bouncing (t(15) = 4.00, p = .001), the effect held blindfolded (t(15) = 2.93) and **vanished when they only watched an adult bounce** (t(15) = 0.51, p = .62) ([Phillips-Silver & Trainor 2005](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/PhillipsSilverScience05.pdf)) [MEASURED]. Duple and triple are equally learnable at that age, and the accent that defines the metre is the one that coincides with the body's movement period [OUR INFERENCE]. For 6/8 at song tempo that is eighths 1 and 4; for 3/4 at moderate tempo it is beat 1 of each bar, a sway per bar, which complements report 1's tempo-dependent felt level ([report 1](/home/claude/zeneng-pipeline/reports/world_music/1_rhythm_and_metre.md)) [OUR INFERENCE]. No study tested movement-induced metre in compound against simple triple time.

## Every evolutionary account puts the steady beat first and metre on top

Darwin proposed that a sexually selected capacity for song preceded meaning; Fitch revises this to a *prosodic* protolanguage of sung syllables and argues that "discrete pitched notes and steady isochronic rhythm", which speech lacks, are probably later developments ([Fitch 2013](https://web.uvic.ca/~aschloss/course_mat/MUS%20511/articles/Musical%20protolanguage_chap24.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY]. Brown's "musilanguage" posits a shared ancestor with discrete pitches and isochronic rhythm, needing group selection, which Fitch calls its most doubtful part ([same](https://web.uvic.ca/~aschloss/course_mat/MUS%20511/articles/Musical%20protolanguage_chap24.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY, relayed]. Savage and colleagues argue that musicality is a coevolved gene-culture system for social bonding, and in reply to 60 commentaries by 109 authors they hold that **"isochrony acts as an entry point for newcomers, while meter and cultural embellishment allow skill display and shibboleths"**, concede that their cost claims rest on "very little empirical data", and fold credible signalling in as a sub-component ([Savage et al. 2021, response](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10333130); [target article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/F1ACB3586FD3DD5965E56021F506BC4F/S0140525X20000333a.pdf/music_as_a_coevolved_system_for_social_bonding.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY]. Mehr and colleagues argue instead that music evolved as a credible signal of coalition quality and parental attention, signalling "existing social cohesion that was obtained by other means" ([Mehr et al. 2021](https://anthro.vancouver.wsu.edu/documents/684/Mehr2021CredibleSignaling.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY; read only through Savage et al.'s account]. Data cited in the exchange: lullabies are about 7% of coded texts in the NHS Ethnography (89 of 1,273) against 25% of the Discography ([Savage et al. 2021, response](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10333130)) [MEASURED, relayed].

Animal work shows the components can be pulled apart. The cockatoo Snowball synchronised to tempo-shifted music in bouts (6 of 31 trials in one study, 3 of 9 in another), leading the beat slightly, with his tempo tracking the music's (slope 0.88, r² = .95) ([Patel et al. 2009](https://web.uvic.ca/~aschloss/course_mat/MUS%20511/ARTICLES%20AND%20REFS%20FOR%20320/Patel.pdf)) [MEASURED]. A sea lion, not a vocal mimic, was trained to bob to novel tempos, presented as "a real problem" for the vocal-learning hypothesis ([UCSC news 2013](https://news.ucsc.edu/2013/04/sea-lion-beat/)) [MEASURED; press release]. Patel's 2021 revision reports macaques sensitive to isochrony but tapping reactively unless rewarded, the sea lion's phase shifting with tempo (about 90° lag at the fastest, 60° lead at the slowest) unlike humans, and recasts vocal learning as a preadaptation for *sporadic* synchronisation that human gene-culture coevolution made *sustained* ([Patel 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8380969)) [MEASURED for the animal results relayed; THEORY ONLY for the hypothesis]. Singing indri lemurs (39 adults, 636 song contributions) show significant **1:1 and 1:2 rhythm categories, with 2:1 not significant** ([De Gregorio et al. 2021](https://www.unito.it/sites/default/files/articolo_current_biology.pdf)) [MEASURED]. Human taps lead a click by 20 to 80 ms in untrained people and 10 to 30 ms in musicians ([London et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13414-019-01747-y.pdf)) [MEASURED, relayed], and taps to speech land about 13 to 19 ms after syllable onset, near the vowel ([Rathcke et al. 2024](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/c6069206-9747-41dc-842c-1fa3ae15f6b3/content)) [MEASURED].

Every account treats isochrony as the base and metre as a culturally elaborated layer on top, and Savage's "skill display and shibboleths" is close to "one language, many dialects" stated as theory [OUR INFERENCE, from THEORY ONLY sources]. So a rule about evenness of onsets is the safest to state universally, and a rule about *which* beat in the bar is strong is a metre-layer rule more likely to vary by style, as rūpak already shows [OUR INFERENCE]. The indri's 1:2 asymmetry shows a ratio bias is biologically possible, but its mapping to crotchet-quaver or quaver-crotchet in 6/8 is unclear and human data stay the reference [OUR INFERENCE; weak]. Motor anticipation is milliseconds and invisible in notation, so it gives no floor for a notated anticipation of an eighth or a quarter, which supports 749.253's ruling that early entry outside 4/4 fires only at the metre table's own share ([fixlist](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)) [OUR INFERENCE]. No animal study of grouping in threes was found.

## Energy crosses cultures; mood by mode stays home

### The theories split meaning into inside and outside, and only one outside kind travels

Koelsch divides musical meaning into extra-musical, intra-musical and musicogenic, and splits extra-musical meaning, after Karbusicky, into **iconic** (sound resembles a thing), **indexical** (movement- and prosody-like patterns that signal an emotion or intention) and **symbolic** (convention, such as an anthem); he maps Meyer's "designative" and "embodied" meanings onto the first two classes ([Koelsch 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00110/full)) [THEORY ONLY]. Music primes word meaning in Western lab studies: unrelated words after musical excerpts elicit an N400 like that after sentences, even after excerpts of about a second ([same](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00110/full)) [MEASURED; no sample sizes in the text read]. Juslin's BRECVEMA lists eight induction mechanisms, from brain-stem reflex and rhythmic entrainment to contagion, expectancy and aesthetic judgement, without claiming which are universal ([Juslin 2013](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhLRv..10..235J)) [THEORY ONLY; abstract only]. Huron's ITPRA names five expectation responses and treats expectation as a source of pleasure ([Aiello 2007, review](https://core.ac.uk/download/159563876.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY; review only]. Topic theory is the nearest thing to a Western lexicon linking metre to meaning: the military march is duple with dotted rhythms and two-bar incises, the sarabande slow 3/2 or 3/4 starting on the downbeat, Mattheson assigned affects to metres, and Mirka's evidence is treatises, with **no listener tests** described ([Mirka 2014](https://music.arts.uci.edu/abauer/4.3/readings/Oxford_handbook_of_topic_theory_Mirka_Intro.pdf)) [THEORY ONLY]. No test of topic recognition by modern or non-Western listeners was found.

For a notes-only engine, Nattiez's neutral level *is* the whole world, and the theories agree that meaning is completed by the listener, so the engine controls only what listeners reconstruct reliably from notes [OUR INFERENCE]. Koelsch's indexical class and Juslin's contagion are one channel seen from two sides (the music moves like a voice or body in a state), and it is the channel with cross-cultural data; topics, Cooke's figures and major or minor are symbolic, "dialect vocabulary" learned per tradition [OUR INFERENCE].

### Listeners everywhere hear arousal; they agree much less on love, peace and valence

Yurdum and colleagues had **5,516 listeners** (28 languages, 49 countries) and **116 listeners in three smaller-scale societies** rate 118 Discography songs from 86 societies. Dance songs were recognised above base rate (β = 0.90; 0.66 in small-scale societies), lullabies too (1.09; 0.75), healing songs less (0.49; 0.47), love songs not significantly (0.30; 0.15). The first principal component in both cohorts separated dance from lullaby, high from low arousal; dance was above base rate in 27 of 28 language groups and love in only 12; and sharing a language family raised accuracy by only β ≈ 0.05 ([Yurdum et al. 2023](https://escholarship.org/content/qt69d86235/qt69d86235.pdf)) [MEASURED; figures to check]. Earlier, 750 listeners in 60 countries separated dance from lullaby strongly and love songs barely, and in a second experiment dance songs were heard as faster (z 1.09), steadier in beat (0.84) and higher in arousal (1.17), lullabies slower (−1.04) and simpler in melody (−1.12) and rhythm (−1.06); contextual features alone explained 74.6% of "for dancing" ratings ([Mehr et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5805477)) [MEASURED]. Naive listeners named four song types at 42.4% against 25% chance, performance context had a dimension of arousal (6.2% of variance) beside formality and religiosity, and within-society variation was about six times between-society variation ([Mehr et al. 2019](https://dcknox.github.io/files/MehrSinghKnoxEtAl_UniversalityDiversityHumanSong.pdf)) [MEASURED]. Adults picked the foreign lullaby as "simpler" even with words filtered out ([Unyk et al. 1992](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735692201002)) [MEASURED; abstract only].

Emotion studies rank the same way. A meta-analysis of 104 vocal and 41 music-performance studies put decoding accuracy at π = .90 within culture and **.84 across cultures** for voice and .88 for music performance, with sadness (.91) and anger (.88) easiest and tenderness (.71) hardest; cross-cultural accuracy ran about 7% below within-culture, and cross-cultural music data were "lacking" in 2003 ([Juslin & Laukka 2003](https://distantreader.org/stacks/carrels-legacy/bluesGuitar-from-scholar/cache/work_fos67nw6zjb6lblxc72ggtzakq.pdf)) [MEASURED; figures to check]. A 2021 meta-analysis of 37 vocal studies found in-group advantage growing with cultural distance, discussed under the "dialect theory" of emotion ([Laukka & Elfenbein 2021](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/cross-cultural-emotion-recognition-and-in-group-advantage-in-voca/)) [MEASURED; abstract only]. Western listeners named nonverbal vocalisations from India, Kenya, Singapore and the US at 39 to 45% against 11% chance ([Laukka et al. 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00353/full)) [MEASURED]. Raga excerpts conveyed joy, sadness and anger, though apparently not peace, to Western listeners through tempo, complexity and range ([Balkwill & Thompson 1999](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/a-cross-cultural-investigation-of-the-perception-of-emotion-in-mu/)) [MEASURED; abstract paraphrase only]. Mafa listeners with no Western exposure matched Western piano pieces to happy, sad and scared faces above chance, with tempo tracking "happy" (r = 0.570) and "scared" (r = −0.625) but not "sad"; mode mattered far less to them than to Westerners (χ²(4) = 142.5 against 794.8) ([Fritz et al. 2009, supplement](https://cognitionandculture.net/wp-content/uploads/mmc1.pdf); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/emotions-music-are-universally-recognised)) [MEASURED; Mafa percentages not read].

Sievers and colleagues gave 50 US students and 85 Kreung villagers five sliders (rate, timing jitter, direction, step size, consonance) driving both a melody and a bouncing ball. In the US, emotion drove settings (partial η² = 0.79) and modality barely did (0.09); the US and Kreung mean vectors were 4.24 apart against 8.11 for random pairs, fewer than 30 in a million random pairs as close; sad settled near 46 BPM with small, falling steps; but Kreung angry and peaceful settings did not match US prototypes ([Sievers et al. 2013](https://eamusic.dartmouth.edu/%7elarry/published_articles/pnas_article/PNAS_artiicle.pdf)) [MEASURED]. US and Chinese listeners shared at least 13 dimensions of musical feeling, with cross-cultural correlations of 0.75 for valence and 0.81 for arousal ([Cowen et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6995018)) [MEASURED; the listener counts in the notes do not sum (2,777 against 1,591 plus 1,258), so check]. Forty Mbenzélé Pygmies and forty Canadians rated the same excerpts: Pygmy arousal followed **tempo only**, Canadian arousal ratings predicted Pygmy arousal for Western excerpts, and **valence showed no cross-group similarity** ([Egermann et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01341/full)) [MEASURED].

The measured size of the "dialect" is therefore small on top of a large shared core: about 6 points of π in vocal emotion and β ≈ 0.05 for shared language family in song function [OUR INFERENCE, arithmetic on Juslin & Laukka and Yurdum]. Cowen's valence r = .75 is probably the high end, because both groups now share global pop, while Egermann's isolated listeners share no valence at all [OUR INFERENCE]. Words are an amplifier rather than the carrier of function (lullabies recognised with words filtered out), but most of the NHS "for dancing" signal was contextual, which a single-line score lacks, so a notes-only engine has a weaker dance signal than a field recording [OUR INFERENCE].

### Rate, register spread and steps travel; mode and "higher is happier" are learned

Pooled across voice and music, the cue profiles are consistent: anger fast, loud and rising; fear fast and irregular; happiness fast, high and variable, staccato in 13 studies against legato in 2; **sadness slow, soft, low, with little pitch variability, a falling contour and legato (14 studies)**; tenderness slow, soft, low, falling and regular ([Juslin & Laukka 2003](https://distantreader.org/stacks/carrels-legacy/bluesGuitar-from-scholar/cache/work_fos67nw6zjb6lblxc72ggtzakq.pdf)) [MEASURED; the summary flagged one internal inconsistency]. In Western listeners, crossing six cues over 200 stimuli gave median unique contributions of **mode .29, tempo .14, register .08**, dynamics .04, articulation .02 and timbre .01; mode dominated happy (.48) and sad (.54), and **none of 84 two-way interactions survived correction**, so cues add ([Eerola, Friberg & Bresin 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/full)) [MEASURED; Western only]. In Papua New Guinea, 169 Uruwa participants over 24,445 trials showed a major-over-minor "happy" effect of 1.06 and 0.90 in churchgoing groups and **−0.14 (no convincing effect) in the minimally exposed group**, against 3.47 and 8.96 in Sydney; "higher is happier" was large in Sydney and absent in the minimal group, which the authors call "essentially cultural" while not ruling out a small universal effect ([Smit et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9242494)) [MEASURED]. With tempo and pitch held roughly constant, acoustic roughness affected emotional expression alike in Khow, Kalash and UK listeners, while the major-minor link and consonance preference were culture-dependent ([Athanasopoulos et al. 2021](https://doaj.org/article/6ca54a84b154405fa3e6d975093acd85)) [MEASURED; abstract only].

The universal-core variables a notes-only engine can compute are notes per second, beat tempo, inter-onset regularity, mean absolute interval, falling share and phrase-final direction, register and pitch spread, articulation, and accent contrast on the metre's own beat tree [OUR INFERENCE]. By evidence weight, rate comes first (the only cue isolated listeners used), then regularity and accent, then register and step size [OUR INFERENCE]. Register needs splitting: pitch spread and height as an energy signal look shared, but "higher is happier" is learned, so the engine may use register to raise or lower energy everywhere, not to make a tune happier [OUR INFERENCE, from Hilton and Smit]. Fritz's Mafa mode effect conflicts on its face with Smit's null, but Fritz's stimuli changed mode together with tempo, range, density and regularity, so the co-varying cues can carry it [OUR INFERENCE]. Eerola's additivity directly supports writing each cue as a separate term whose weight changes by style [OUR INFERENCE].

### No study ties triple metre to any function or feeling

No cross-cultural study read shows that 3/4 or 6/8 is tied to lullabies, rocking, dance or any affect. Song type in the NHS Discography turns on accent, tempo and macrometer consistency ([Mehr et al. 2019](https://dcknox.github.io/files/MehrSinghKnoxEtAl_UniversalityDiversityHumanSong.pdf), via [report 3](/home/claude/zeneng-pipeline/reports/world_music/3_form_and_repetition.md)) [MEASURED]; "triple micrometer" appears among its predictors, but duple against triple by song type is not reported ([Mehr et al. 2019](https://dcknox.github.io/files/MehrSinghKnoxEtAl_UniversalityDiversityHumanSong.pdf)) [MEASURED as an absence]. Mehr 2018, Yurdum 2023 and Unyk 1992 report nothing on metre ([Mehr et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5805477); [Yurdum et al. 2023](https://escholarship.org/content/qt69d86235/qt69d86235.pdf); [Unyk et al. 1992](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735692201002)) [MEASURED as an absence]. The common claim that "lullabies are usually in triple meter or 6/8 time" cites a BBC News feature, and "consistent across cultures" carries no citation ([Wikipedia, Lullaby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lullaby)) [THEORY ONLY, unsupported]. What makes a lullaby a lullaby (slow, steady, soft, low accent contrast, low pitch variability, simple and repetitive) can be written in 2, 3 or 6, and within a dialect triple carries convention: in German folk song the Ländler and waltz make triple as much a dance metre as a cradle one, and a rocking 6/8 lullaby and a fast 6/8 jig share a metre and mean opposite things [OUR INFERENCE]. If rocking shapes lullaby metre, the prediction concerns period (one rock per dotted crotchet or per bar at a slow rate), not grouping, and is untested [OUR INFERENCE].

## What this implies for Luthier's rules for fitting words to melody in any metre

The programmes, read together, offer one architecture [OUR INFERENCE throughout this section, from the sources cited]. A **metre layer** holds only GTTM-type well-formedness: the beat tree, spacing in 2s and 3s, equal spacing at the tactus, and the bar-level beat's position. A **grouping layer** holds the culture-free boundary cues (rest, long final note, register change) that Ayari and McAdams's listeners shared ([Ayari & McAdams 2003](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/v118rj30z)). A **weighted constraint layer**, Hayes-style maximum entropy over GTTM's preference rules, places syllables, with weights read from the style table ([Hayes slides](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesTuftsPresentation.pdf)). A **core affect vector** (rate, regularity, accent contrast, step size, falling share, pitch spread, register, legato) means the same in every metre, and a **dialect layer** holds mode-to-valence, register-to-valence, consonance and topic meanings ([Eerola et al. 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/full); [Smit et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9242494)). The two-step "place, then shift" model and the hold-out test that would check this are in the [Mathematics report](/home/claude/wm_context/Mathematics%20of%20fitting%20words%20to%20metre.md) and are not restated.

Five concrete readings follow for open findings [OUR INFERENCE for each]. First, 749.233's metre-layer text, "three beats with the first strongest" ([fixlist](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md)), mixes two things. The grid does put the bar-level beat on beat 1, but how much stress or density goes there is a style weight, as rūpak's light sam and Balinese end-accent show, so "first strongest" in the accent sense belongs in the style table and the metre layer should say only "three beats, equal spacing, the bar's beat on the first". Second, the engine's devices already have GTTM names: 879's first-beat pull is MPR4 (stress), 834's early entry is a displacement off MPR3 (event), 818's singing a line again is MPR1 (parallelism), and the padded fourth bar is MPR10 above the bar ([fixlist](/home/claude/wm_context/fixlist_v54.md); [Wikipedia, GTTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_theory_of_tonal_music)). Naming them that way lets one weight table per style set all of them, and turns 749.256's "4/4 figures acting outside 4/4" into a list of MPR weights not yet fitted outside 4/4. Third, the grid leaves beats 2 and 3 of 3/4 equal, so any beat-2/beat-3 asymmetry in the stress score must come from fitted weights, not from the tree; the likelihood-ratio test already set out in the Mathematics report (its row 5) decides whether such a weight earns its place. Fourth, the metre-free density target is notes per second, which bears on 749.226's bars-per-line choice and 749.243's section density, and the rhythm tables should come from sung corpora of the style, not from the lyric language's speech rhythm. Fifth, a lyric's genre, which already chooses the style table in 749.233, can also set an affect target on the core vector (a lullaby: low rate, low accent contrast, low pitch spread), checkable from notes alone.

Three cautions run the other way [OUR INFERENCE]. A judge trained on Western ratings will reward mode, which carries roughly half the happy/sad signal for Westerners and none for minimally exposed listeners ([Eerola et al. 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/full); [Smit et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9242494)), so "fit to mood" from notes can be trusted across cultures only on the energy axis. The strongest results here (Ozaki, Albouy 2024, Hilton) are on audio, and their translation to notation (notes per second, held notes) is the project's own step, to be counted before use. And none of the programmes measured textsetting in 3/4 or 6/8 with listeners or corpora, so every triple-metre prediction above is derived from duple data or from rules.

## Where the evidence does not answer

The direct triple-metre questions stay open. No source read gives a GTTM, Temperley, Halle & Lerdahl or corpus statement for beat 2 against beat 3 in 3/4, or for the slots within a 6/8 beat; the equal-strength result above is derived, not quoted. Palmer & Krumhansl's 1990 per-position values, the most useful unread primary source on that question, exist only in figures, and Prince & Schmuckler's per-metre results are in a full text not read. No study tests whether listeners hear a stress on beat 2 or 3 of 3/4 as a mismatch, and no ERP or ERAN study compares triple with duple metre or crosses cultures beyond one closure study cited by Koelsch ([Koelsch 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00110/full)). No application of GTTM's *metrical* rules to a metred non-Western repertoire with data was found (tāl, uṣūl, gamelan, West African timelines), and Becker & Becker's Javanese grammar was not reached. Arabic iqāʿāt were not reached on this branch at all ([report 1](/home/claude/zeneng-pipeline/reports/world_music/1_rhythm_and_metre.md)).

On the brain and acoustics side, the originals of Slevc et al. 2009, Fedorenko et al. 2009 and Kunert et al. 2015 were not obtained, so their sample and effect sizes are missing, and no multi-lab replication was found. No cross-cultural study gives sung notes per second from notation, absolute syllable rates for song against speech, or isochrony of sung against spoken syllables. Ozaki's exploratory effect sizes for regularity, phrase length and range were not reached. The speech-to-song result for tonal listeners rests on 20 people. No nPVI by metre in a sung corpus was found, and the methodological critiques of nPVI were not fetched.

On meaning, no study measured the *lexical* level (whether one melodic figure means the same thing in two cultures), no study isolates interval size or contour from tempo in real melodies across cultures with numeric effects, and Eerola's cue-crossing design has not been run outside the West. **No corpus count of duple against triple metre by song function exists in any source read**; the NHS Discography's expert annotations could answer it for 118 songs, but access to the NHS repository from this session was refused, so the count was not made. Laments are covered by no study read.

On the programmes themselves, the primary books listed in "How to read" were not read, Jackendoff 2009 was read only through two citing papers, Mehr et al. 2021 only through its critics' reply, Fitch 2014 only through a comment, and Fabb & Halle's music chapter not at all.

## Conclusion

The question "is music a language?" turned out to have a sharper answer than either side of the old debate expected. What music shares with language is not meaning or syntax but timing structure: a shallow, periodic grid of graded strength onto which stressed syllables are mapped by weighted rules, while pitch runs on its own machinery and meaning floats [OUR INFERENCE, from the sections above]. That moves the engine's hardest open problems, K3 and the 749.25x line of stress fixes, out of "missing grammar" and into "unfitted weights on a grid every metre shares". It also means the beat-2 against beat-3 question is not settled by theory at all: the grid makes them equal, so whatever asymmetry the kept waltzes show is a style weight to be counted, which is a cheaper and more honest position than borrowing one [OUR INFERENCE].

The cross-cultural record also gives the writer's "dialect" a measured size for the first time on this branch: a shared core that carries energy, rate and evenness, with in-group advantages of a few percentage points on top, and the one feature Western listeners weight most for mood (major against minor) sitting wholly in the dialect [OUR INFERENCE, from Juslin & Laukka, Yurdum, Eerola and Smit]. For songwriting that is good news and a warning together. The engine can state its word-fitting rules once and expect them to travel; but any judge it uses to say a song "fits its mood" is speaking a dialect unless it is restricted to the energy axis [OUR INFERENCE].
