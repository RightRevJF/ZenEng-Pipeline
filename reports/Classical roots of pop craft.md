# Pop kept the classical sentence, loosened its harmony

Popular song took its phrase plan from classical music almost intact and its harmony only in part. The classical "sentence" (a short idea, the idea again, then a continuation twice as long that drives to a cadence, 1:1:2) is the same shape pop analysts call SRDC, and it is counted: **more than a quarter of 700 Top-20 hits from 1955-89 and 111 of 207 Great American Songbook songs contain one**. The baroque bass patterns (lament bass, circle-of-fifths sequence, Pachelbel/Romanesca bass, stepwise descending bass) are documented in pop by named example, but pop harmony as a whole runs in a different direction from classical harmony: IV→I outnumbers V→I in rock, the reverse of the classical ratio of about five to one. Word-setting rules and rhythm models both extend to 3/4 and 6/8 in a form a rule engine can code today, with one hard rule (never let stress fall while metrical strength rises between neighbouring syllables) and one well-tested model design (a note's bar position predicted from the previous note's bar position). The evidence has four plain holes: nobody has counted galant schemata in pop, nobody has published stress-alignment percentages or phrase-model counts by metre, the ABBA claims rest on interviews with no bar-level analysis found, and no study shows that any of these patterns makes a song better liked or longer-lived. Most of what follows is therefore usable as a generator and as a countable check, and very little of it is yet validated as a predictor of quality, least of all outside 4/4.

## A short tune is built 1:1:2, and pop strophes use that shape about half the time

The two classical models differ by one switch. A **sentence** states a basic idea, repeats it at once, then continues to a cadence, in the ratio 1:1:2 (2+2+4 bars in the model case). A **period** states an idea plus a contrasting idea ending on a weak cadence (usually a half cadence), then restarts the same idea and ends on a stronger one (usually a perfect authentic cadence), normally 4+4; the second half is the same length or longer, rarely shorter, and if both halves end with the same cadence the result is a repeated phrase, not a period ([Open Music Theory, Phrase Archetypes](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/phrase-archetypes-unique-forms/)). The sentence's repeat can be exact, sequential (transposed), or a statement-response pair that re-harmonizes the idea toward the dominant or back to the tonic. The continuation opens with material derived from the idea, cuts it into half-length units, speeds up the chord changes, and spends its last one or two bars of four on the cadence ([A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)).

These are centres of gravity, not gates. A recount of Schoenberg's own 37 sentence examples finds **fewer than half in strict 1:1:2**, six with an unrepeated idea, and a typical basic idea of 7 to 15 notes; a 214-sentence corpus from Baroque to pop finds the plain 1:1:2 type "by far the most common" in every style, with expanded and truncated sentences rarer in pop than in classical music ([A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)). Caplin, whose book defines these forms for Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven, concedes that few themes match a definition exactly and gives adverbs in place of counts ([Grave, MTO 4.6](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.98.4.6/mto.98.4.6.grave.php)). **No source found gives the share of classical themes that are sentences versus periods versus hybrids.**

In pop the sentence goes by SRDC: statement, restatement or response, departure, conclusion, Walter Everett's labels. Its defining feature is short-short-long: departure plus conclusion must outlast either opening unit, the standard case being 4+4+8 bars. Momentum comes from the same devices as the classical continuation. In "Dream Lover" chords last two bars in S and R, one bar in D, half a bar in C. Summach's count of 700 Billboard Top-20 songs finds strophes sentential "almost half of the time", peaking at 60% of AABA and strophic songs in 1964, and shows the prechorus growing out of the D unit as sentences stretched: verse = S+R, prechorus = D, chorus = C ([Summach, MTO 17.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)). Callahan's Songbook count adds how words sit on the shape: in 35% of sentential phrases the lyric lists items over the presentation and fragments and "reveals" at the cadence; in 16% the title bookends the phrase ([Callahan, MTO 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)). Nobile supplies the harmony underneath: a section that closes must complete a tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, tonic circuit, with the cadence at the circuit's end ([Barna, review of Nobile, MTO 28.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.1/mto.22.28.1.barna.html)). One generator with a scale parameter therefore covers the 8-bar phrase, the 16-bar strophe and the whole verse-prechorus-chorus cycle. The period is much less documented in pop: the nearest counted item is Callahan's "2n + 2n" lyric type at 20% of sentential phrases. "Pop uses the sentence" is well supported; "pop uses the period" is not, on the sources reached.

Around the phrase plan sit several counted defaults. Arch contours (middle third higher than both outer thirds) beat V-shapes across 52,899 folk phrases, and phrase-final notes are longer than the rest by **1.58 to 0.93**, about 1.7 times ([Tierney, Russo and Patel, PNAS 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3174665)). But a phrase contour needs nine cosine components to capture 95% of its variance, so the arch is a bias, not a template ([Cornelissen et al., ISMIR 2021](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2021/paper/000016.pdf)). In 909 pop songs, repeated phrases cover 50-90% of the running length, over 90% of songs use only two or three distinct phrases, V-I has probability 0.89 at phrase ends against 0.47 elsewhere, and 72% of notes a whole note or longer sit at section-final phrase ends ([Dai, Zhang and Dannenberg](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.07518)). Local melodic peaks land on stressed syllables more than chance in 679 pop lead sheets, though that corpus is 4/4 only ([Nichols et al., ISMIR 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)). Three things often assumed have no support here: the position of the single highest note (the "two thirds through" claim is lore), how often a repeated idea returns transposed rather than at pitch, and the usual transposition interval.

On metre the theory is silent but permissive. The sentence is defined by proportion, so halved or doubled bar counts still qualify, and triple-metre pieces sit in the 214-sentence corpus without special treatment ([A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)). A fast waltz-time show tune, "I Could Have Danced All Night", is one 64-bar sentence ([Callahan, MTO 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)). The working rule, which is a design inference and not a published finding, is to count felt bars: 2+2+4 as written in moderate 3/4, 4+4+8 or more in one-in-a-bar waltz time, and either reading in 6/8 depending on tempo. **No source states how sentences or periods behave differently in 3/4 or 6/8, and none says how to fragment below a three-beat bar.**

## Stock basses crossed over by name; the galant schemata are codeable but uncounted in pop

The galant schemata are defined as paired bass and melody scale-degree lines of two to five stages, each with a usual place in the phrase, which makes them directly codeable as records of name, position, bass degrees, melody degrees and figures. The table gives the ones most useful to a song engine, as transmitted by an open textbook following Gjerdingen; the book itself was not opened ([Open Music Theory, Galant Schemas Summary](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/galant-schemas-summary/)).

| Schema | Place in phrase | Melody degrees | Bass degrees |
|---|---|---|---|
| Romanesca | Opening | 1-5-1-1 | 1-7-6-3 |
| Do-Re-Mi | Opening | 1-2-3 | 1-7-1 |
| Meyer | Opening | 1-7-4-3 | 1-2-7-1 |
| Prinner | Answer to an opening | 6-5-4-3 | 4-3-2-1 |
| Fonte | Sequence, step down (minor then major) | 5-4-4-3 | #1-2-7-1 |
| Monte | Sequence, step up (IV then V) | sources disagree on notation | 3-4-#4-5 |
| Ponte | Dominant pedal | 5-7-2 | 5-5-5 |
| Cadenza semplice | Full cadence | 1-2-2-1 | 3-4-5-1 |
| Cadenza composta | Full cadence with 6/4 | 1-2-3-2-1 | 3-4-5-5-1 |
| Quiescenza | After the cadence, tonic pedal | b7-6-7-1 | 1-1-1-1 |

The Meyer family is the most reusable: one bass (1-2 then 7-1) accepts five different melodies, giving a ready-made open-half, closed-half pair ([Wikipedia, Galant Schemata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galant_Schemata)). What the published pop literature documents, though, is the baroque bass half, not the galant set by name. The lament bass (1-b7-b6-5, typically I-bVII-bVI-V) and the circle-of-fifths sequence (four or more roots descending by fifth, often ending on the relative major) are defined and exemplified in pop by music theorists ([Open Music Theory, Classical Schemas in a Pop Context](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/classical-schemas/)). The chromatic lament, the single-chord "line cliché", the stepwise descending major bass of "A Whiter Shade of Pale" and "Piano Man", and the occasional cadential 6/4 are listed with examples on a theorist's teaching blog ([Ethan Hein](https://ethanhein.substack.com/p/identifying-harmonized-basslines)). The Pachelbel bass is traced through about thirty charting songs from 1966 to 2019 on a Wikipedia list ([List of variations on Pachelbel's Canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_variations_on_Pachelbel%27s_Canon)). These are example lists of mixed scholarly weight, not counts. **No published count of Prinner, Meyer, Fonte or Monte in any pop corpus was found**, and the reading of the pop progression IV-I6-V-I as a Prinner, or of the Pachelbel loop, the galant Romanesca and the stepwise descending bass as one schema with three bass shapes, is the research team's inference.

Corpus counts show what was not carried over. Classical and rock share the same top three chords and a tonic share near one third, but classical harmony is directional and rock is not.

| Measure | Common practice | Rock / pop |
|---|---|---|
| Top chord roots | I 34.6%, V 23.3%, II 11.3%, IV 7.6% | I 32.8%, IV 22.6%, V 16.3%, bVII 8.1% |
| V→I against IV→I | 167 to 32 (about 5.2 to 1) | 788 to 1,162 (about 1 to 1.5) |
| Root motion down a fifth against up a fifth | 308 to 167; Bach chorales 35% to 10% | 2,266 to 2,220 |
| Root-position chords | about 60% | 94.1% |

Sources: [Temperley, Kostka-Payne statistics](https://davidtemperley.com/kp-stats/); [de Clercq and Temperley 2011](https://rockcorpus.midside.com/2011_paper/declercq_temperley_2011.pdf); [Tymoczko](https://dmitri.mycpanel.princeton.edu/files/publications/tonaltheories.pdf). The ratios in the second row are arithmetic on the published tables.

A hidden-Markov study confirms the difference in kind: in Bach chorales three states reproduce tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, tonic, while in the Billboard corpus IV, not V, is the tonic's opposite pole ([White and Quinn 2018](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/545183b3e4b0f4d5bea12a07/t/5c9d08d2e79c70d789ae3e9a/1553795368989/mty021+(1).pdf)). This yields four countable dials for how "classical" a chord chart is: the down-fifth to up-fifth ratio, the V→I to IV→I ratio, the share of inverted chords, and the share of ii and vii° against bVII and bVI. Tymoczko's Bach table (I goes to V 41%, IV 30%, ii 14%, vi 11%; V goes to I 86%; ii goes to V 61%) is usable as a first-order generator for a classical mode, and the rock counts for a rock mode. Two cautions apply. The classical figures come from a 919-chord textbook sample and what Tymoczko calls a crude MIDI survey. And root-only corpora cannot see schemata at all, because the bass line lives in the inversions those encodings drop.

On whether any of this makes songs better, the evidence is absent, not negative. The one relevant study finds top-quartile Billboard songs use slightly rarer chords in the verse (5.26 against 4.60 bits) and drop further in surprise into the chorus (2.15 against 0.95 bits), a small effect (d = 0.289) from a measure that ignores chord order and a sample that excludes songs that change key ([Miles, Rosen and Grzywacz 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00263/full)). That supports contrast between sections, not any classical device.

## Queen is documented bar by bar; ABBA's classical debt rests on interviews

For Queen the best bar-level source is an exam-board guide to "Killer Queen". It documents passing modulations each confirmed by a perfect cadence, a chorus that moves through B-flat, D minor and C and then runs a circle of fifths (bars 20-21) back home, an inner chromatic descent, a pedal, irregular phrases (a 4-bar verse phrase answered by 5 bars, stretched by a 6/8 bar; a chorus of 5+3), a mainly syllabic melody that mixes steps with a signature rising major sixth, and a climax saved for the end with the voices at their highest ([Pearson Edexcel set-work guide](https://qualifications.pearson.com/content/dam/pdf/GCSE/Music/2016/teaching-and-learning-materials/Killer_Queen_set_work_suport_guide.pdf)). "Bohemian Rhapsody" moves between sections by fourth (B-flat to E-flat, prepared by turning the old tonic into a dominant seventh) and uses a descending chromatic bass as the connector into a new key; Mercury called it a "mock opera", and Brian May said he wanted the solo to be "a little tune that would be a counterpart to the main melody" ([Wikipedia, Bohemian Rhapsody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemian_Rhapsody)). Mercury described "The Millionaire Waltz" as "a Strauss waltz" laid out as though an orchestra had been hired ([queensongs.info](https://www.queensongs.info/quotes/queen/a-day-at-the-races/the-millionaire-waltz)). These translate into rules: modulate by fourth or fifth through a re-coloured tonic, confirm each visited key with its own V-I, connect keys by chromatic bass descent, permit one inserted half-bar per phrase, write counter-melodies that answer and do not double, and reserve the highest notes for the last section.

The claim should not be overstated. The scholarly work on Queen's style, Braae's 90-song thesis, names hard rock, progressive rock, glam, cabaret, 1960s pop and 1930s American popular song as the sources, and its abstract does not name classical music ([Braae 2016](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/f5fdeeca-fcfe-43a2-89c2-5ed903cefc31)). Only abstracts of Braae and of McLeod's study of operatic influence were read; the open-access thesis is the best next source. On this evidence the classical element in Queen is deliberate pastiche of genres and textures laid over harmonic habits that owe more to cabaret and the Songbook, which is itself a sentence-building tradition. Reading the "Killer Queen" chorus as a Fonte- or Monte-like chain of tonicizations is an inference; the guide does not use schema terms.

For ABBA the verifiable record is biographical. Benny Andersson grew up on accordion, Swedish folk music and schlager ([Wikipedia, Benny Andersson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benny_Andersson)) and told Classic FM in 2021, "I listen to Bach every night" ([Classic FM](https://classicfm.com/music-news/videos/benny-andersson-abba)). "The Winner Takes It All" was rescued from a version judged "much too stiff and metrical" by a chanson-style arrangement with a descending piano line and a looser structure ([Wikipedia, The Winner Takes It All](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Winner_Takes_It_All)). **No accessible peer-reviewed bar-level analysis of any named ABBA song was found**: no sourced keys, progressions, sequences, counter-melodies or key changes for "Dancing Queen", "Chiquitita", "I Have a Dream" or "Thank You for the Music". The familiar claims of baroque sequence and counterpoint in ABBA are plausible and undocumented here, and no corpus has measured either band against the rock baseline. The engine can take rules from the Queen case; from ABBA it can take only one direction of travel, toward folk-dance metres and a descending line under a repeated melodic cell.

## In 3/4 and 6/8, words follow one hard rule and a handful of stress-to-beat schemas

The rule that survives every corpus is about direction between neighbours, which is why it transfers to any metre. In 176,708 syllables of Dutch folk song, stress falling while metrical strength rises occurs at **0.26 of the expected rate**, and stress rising while strength falls at 0.36; the avoidance is stronger still at phrase ends ([De Castro-Arrazola et al. 2015](https://vdca.netlify.com/pdf/decastro-arrazola2015_fma-dutch-textsetting.pdf)). In English song, the stressed syllable of an unstressed-stressed pair inside a word was metrically stronger in **94 of 94** cases, and stressed syllables average metrical level 3.08 against 1.93 for unstressed. French is looser, but 97% of French lines still end on a stressable syllable placed on a very strong beat, and later verses align worse than first verses because the tune is fitted to verse one ([Temperley and Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)). Word-level stress is honoured about 90% of the time in English art song and operetta, while phrase-level stress (adjective plus noun) is honoured only 42-83% ([Palmer and Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)). So lexical stress inside polysyllables is a hard constraint, monosyllable and phrase stress a soft one, and the last stress of each line belongs on a downbeat. Hayes frames the whole task as ranked, violable goals: match stress to strong positions, avoid long gaps with no syllable, avoid cramming, and align phrase boundaries with line boundaries ([Hayes, Textsetting](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)).

Compound metre enters only through the strength table. Temperley's coding makes the dotted quarter the beat in 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8 and otherwise treats triple and duple alike ([Temperley and Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)). The derived table, a design inference, is: in 6/8, eighth 1 strongest, eighth 4 at beat level, eighths 2, 3, 5 and 6 weakest; in 3/4, beat 1 strongest, beats 2 and 3 equal, off-beat eighths weakest. One consequence is specific to 3/4: a stressed syllable on beat 3 followed by an unstressed one on the next downbeat is the violation, while stressed on 2 then unstressed on 3 is neutral.

The lied literature supplies the layouts. The default is one poetic stress per beat, so a four-stress line takes two bars of 6/8 and a couplet makes a four-bar phrase; a three-stress line fills three beats and leaves the fourth as the line-end rest or long note. Malin's notation records which beat each stress takes: [1 2 / 1 2] for four stresses in a two-beat bar, [1, 2 / 1 –] for three. For triple metre two schemas are reported: the upbeat-oriented [3 / 1 – 3 / 1], stresses alternating beat 3 and beat 1, and the downbeat-oriented [1, 2, 3 / 1 – –], three stresses in one bar, the fourth on the next downbeat, then two empty beats ([London, review of Malin, MTO 17.2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.2/mto.11.17.2.london.php); [Rodgers, MTO 17.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.1/mto.11.17.1.rodgers.html)). Krebs's norm for Schumann adds that stress-to-stress spans should be roughly equal, that a pause is "obligatory at the end of each line" and longer after even lines, and that iambic verse in 3/4 takes either even notes or half note (stressed) plus quarter (unstressed) ([Krebs, MTO 20.4](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.14.20.4/mto.14.20.4.krebs.php)). Composers add a beat or a bar where the singer would otherwise have no breath, and folk song sets about 95.5% of syllables one note each. The specific foot-to-rhythm fillings an engine would want (trochee as quarter-eighth in 6/8, dactyl as three eighths, one dactyl per bar in 3/4, a one- or two-eighth pickup for verse that starts unstressed) are constructions from these principles, not quotations.

Three limits apply. **No opened source reports alignment percentages by metre**; every number above pools metres or comes from 4/4. Malin's book and his full schema inventory were seen only through reviews, with no frequency table. And the genre rhythms a songwriter would reach for first (siciliana, barcarolle, Ländler, Scotch snap) had no source opened at all. The first gap is closable by counting. OpenScore Lieder is CC0, holds about 1,350 art songs with syllable-level lyrics, and by a local count of first time signatures has **265 in 3/4 and 216 in 6/8** (plus 33 in 9/8 and 20 in 12/8); a rough language guess puts about 369 songs in English, which by extrapolation means some 70 in 3/4 and 60 in 6/8 ([OpenScore Lieder](https://github.com/OpenScore/Lieder)). The Open Hymnal adds 44 English tunes in 3/4 but only 5 in 6/8 ([Open Hymnal](http://openhymnal.org/OpenHymnal2014.06.abc)). Neither has stress marks, so stress must come from a pronouncing dictionary. The Meertens MTCFeatures release is the only resource with per-note stress, beat strength, time signature and phrase position ready to cross-tabulate, but it is Dutch and licensed CC BY-NC-SA: usable as design evidence, not as shipped or training data ([MTCFeatures](https://pvankranenburg.github.io/MTCFeatures/melodyrepresentation.html)). English 6/8 with aligned text is the scarce resource.

## Rhythm grammars reduce to one tested design: bar position given the last bar position

The formal rhythm literature splits into rule systems with no probabilities (GTTM, Longuet-Higgins and Lee, Hayes, Euclidean rhythms), grid models, sequence models over bar positions, and tree grammars. Only the grid and sequence models have been scored on held-out music, so only they can serve as a probability core; the rest are features and penalties. In Temperley's six-way test on 1,235 Essen folk melodies and 63 Haydn-Mozart quartet movements, the best model draws each note's bar position conditional on the previous note's bar position, and the runner-up fills weaker beats conditional on whether the stronger beats on either side carry notes.

| Model | Essen cross-entropy (per song) | Parameters |
|---|---|---|
| Uniform position | 62.37 | 1 |
| Zeroth-order duration | 54.45 | 15 |
| Metrical position | 42.47 | 4 |
| Fine-grained position | 40.21 | 8 |
| Hierarchical position | 38.76 | 13 |
| First-order metrical duration | 37.36 | 56 |

Source: [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf).

The hierarchical model's numbers are the "anchoring" rule in countable form: in Essen a quarter-level note has probability **.889 when only the following stronger beat has a note, .713 when both neighbours do, .265 when only the preceding one does, and .062 when neither does**; the downbeat carries a note with probability .998. That study tested 4/4 only and merely sketches a 3/4 extension. The all-metre evidence comes from a variable-order model over metrical position on 4,966 German folk songs: **1.34 bits per onset with metre against 1.54 without**, the largest gain coming from order 0 to order 1, with 3/4 against 6/8 the most frequent confusion because nothing in the model knows both are twelve-sixteenth bars ([van der Weij, Pearce and Honing 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)). On 4/4 pop melodies the same model family scores 2.01 bits per note at first order and 1.87 at second, and a whole-bar "rhythm word" model scores 1.97 with no context at all ([Nakamura and Yoshii](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.06969)). The researcher's conversion of Temperley's per-song figures to roughly 1.2 bits per note is an estimate, not his number.

Repetition is the largest factor the classic models omit. Temperley says a model with repetition "could achieve a substantial improvement". Nakamura and Yoshii show each song reuses a sparse set of bar patterns, model that with a per-song cache drawn from the generic model plus two edits for near-repeats (split a note, shift an onset), and find the cache "often helped more than raising the model order" ([Nakamura and Yoshii](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.06969)). No source gives the gain in bits, and no model captures which earlier bar gets copied. The tree grammars (Rohrmeier's time-spans with upbeat, body and coda; Harasim's upbeat-downbeat splits) handle the pickup most elegantly, as a slot attached to the following strong event, but carry no fitted probabilities for melody and no repetition rule ([Rohrmeier 2020](https://program.ismir2020.net/static/final_papers/368.pdf)). **Phrase-final lengthening is modelled by none of the probabilistic models**, and Temperley's test simply drops the pickup and most of the last bar. No pickup-length distribution by metre was found.

Syncopation is where pop rhythm departs from its classical and folk parents, and it is counted. In 80 rock songs **one third of stressed syllables are syncopated and 76% of those anticipate a strong beat**; in ten 19th-century English songs the count is zero. The downbeat is anticipated about as often as it is hit (tendency .504), beat 3 slightly more often (.554), and eighth- and sixteenth-level syncopation are alternatives chosen by tempo ([Tan, Lustig and Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)). Density is not the difference: Essen's .51 onset probability per eighth gives about four notes per 4/4 bar, the same as 1955-97 rock melody at 4.12, with the jump to 7.19 arriving only in 2015-19 hits ([White, Pater and Breen 2021 preprint](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/545183b3e4b0f4d5bea12a07/t/6095435cf00ef61c437de243/1620394846707/PostMillenialAnalysis.pdf)). Every one of these syncopation figures is from 4/4. The Longuet-Higgins and Lee index (a rest or tie preceded by a sounded note of lesser weight, scored by the weight difference) correlates with listeners' pulse-tracking error at r = .82 and is defined by a metrical weight table, so it can be written for 3/4 and 6/8, but its validation is also 4/4 ([Fitch and Rosenfeld 2007](https://web.uvic.ca/~aschloss/course_mat/MUS%20511/ARTICLES%20AND%20REFS%20FOR%20320/FitchRosenfeld20071.pdf)).

## What the engine can count today, and how far each check is trusted

The table sorts the usable material by what the engine does with it and how solid the footing is. "Counted" means a corpus figure exists; "defined" means a precise rule exists without a frequency; "inferred" means the rule is the research team's construction from cited principles.

| Use | Codeable form | Footing | Outside 4/4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phrase plan | 1:1:2 units; repeat type (exact, sequential, statement-response); continuation in half-length units with doubled chord rate; cadence in last quarter to half | Counted in pop and Songbook | Defined by proportion; no counts by metre |
| Period check | Two halves, same opening, weaker then stronger cadence, second half not shorter | Defined; thinly counted in pop | Same |
| Phrase ends | Final note about 1.7 times mean length; V-I probability 0.89 at phrase end; longest notes at section end | Counted (folk, Chinese pop) | Folk corpora mix metres; pop figures 4/4 |
| Contour | Middle third highest; local peaks on stressed syllables | Counted; peak-stress in 4/4 only | Untested |
| Repetition budget | Two or three distinct phrases; repeats over half the length | Counted (Chinese pop) | Untested |
| Voice-bass frames | Schema records: bass degrees, melody degrees, phrase position | Defined; uncounted in pop | Metre-free |
| Harmonic style dials | Fifth-direction ratio, V→I to IV→I ratio, inversion share, ii and vii° against bVII and bVI | Counted, both repertoires | Metre-free |
| Section contrast | Verse chord surprise above chorus chord surprise | Counted, small effect, non-modulating songs only | Metre-free |
| Stress direction | Forbid stress down with strength up, and the reverse, between neighbours | Counted (0.26, 0.36; 94 of 94 in English words) | Rule transfers; strength tables for 3/4 and 6/8 inferred |
| Line layout | Stress-per-beat schemas; line-end pause, longer after even lines | Defined from lieder analysis | Stated for triple and compound; no frequencies |
| Rhythm likelihood | Bar position given previous bar position, backed off to anchoring model; per-song bar cache | Counted, held-out | 1.34 bits per onset across metres on folk; no pop figure |
| Syncopation | Stressed syllable on weak position with nothing on next strong; anticipation share | Counted | 4/4 only |

Two cross-cutting limits govern how the engine should read its own scores. A model trained on one style mis-scores another by about 0.2 to 0.4 bits per onset on the German and Chinese folk comparison ([van der Weij et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)), so a folk-trained rhythm model will mark good pop as unlikely. And every corpus comparison here depends on encoding choices: two expert annotators agreed on chord roots only 92% of the time ([de Clercq 2017](https://creaa.unistra.fr/websites/gream/Activites/Euromac_2017_-_Postprint_-_Article_-_DeCLERCQ_Trevor.pdf)). A check built on these figures can flag a song as unlike its models. It cannot certify the song as good, because no source ties any of these measures to liking or survival.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. "What did pop take from classical music" has a compact answer, one phrase plan and a few bass lines, and the phrase plan is the better-evidenced half. The harder finding is that the classical inheritance is strongest exactly where a note-only engine can count it (proportions, cadence placement, stress direction, bar-position statistics) and weakest where the commentary is loudest (galant schemata in pop, ABBA's baroque habits, the lasting power of "classical-sounding" hits). An engine should therefore generate from the classical models freely and judge with them cautiously, treating each as a centre of gravity with a measured spread.

For metres other than 4/4, almost every rule transfers by definition and almost no number transfers by measurement. That gap is closable with data already located, and three counting jobs would convert most of the inferred rows above into counted ones: stress against beat position by metre on MTCFeatures (as design evidence only), syllables per bar and stress-to-beat schemas on the English 3/4 and 6/8 songs of OpenScore Lieder, and a per-metre fit of the two Temperley models with a per-song bar cache on Essen. A fourth, reading Braae's open thesis for chord-level Queen findings, would settle how much of the Queen case is classical harmony and how much is cabaret wearing its clothes. Until those are done, the engine's 3/4 and 6/8 checks rest on sound principles and borrowed numbers, and its reports should say so.
