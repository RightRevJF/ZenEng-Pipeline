# Songs in three lean on downbeats

The sources agree on three things that match the engine's three faults. First, stressed syllables belong on strong beats, and in 3/4 that means mostly beat one, and in 6/8 the first and fourth eighth notes. Second, English song lines are built to a fixed size of four strong beats. A line that is short of that leaves its spare time at the end, as a held note or a rest, and that gap is what gives the next line room to come in on time. Third, the engine's 3/4 tunes (9 semitones, 6 different pitches) are narrower than every yardstick we have: 12 semitones is the middle value for European folk song, and your own waltz lead sheets sit at 14 to 16. The big caveat is that almost nobody has measured 3/4 or 6/8 songs directly. The main studies of English word-setting looked only at 4/4, or forced singers into a two-beat pattern. So your own counts (538 waltz lead sheets, six 6/8 hymns) are the best numbers on this question that we found, and the engine advice below is our reasoning from general rules, not a published recipe.

**How to read this report.** Each point carries one of three labels. **Established** means a study or a count supports it. **Craft advice** means teachers say it, with no test behind it. **Our inference** means the researchers worked it out and no source states it.

## Beat one carries most stresses, not all

**Established.** Composers put stressed syllables on strong beats, and metres in three follow a strong-weak-weak pattern ([Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)). The rule is close to unbreakable inside a word of two or more syllables. In a large set of English folk lines, a wrong-way stress at the end of a word was "avoided without exception" in the original settings ([Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)).

**Established.** The rule is looser for one-syllable words and for which word in a phrase gets the weight. In art songs, compound words kept their stress on the strong beat **91%** of the time, but two-word phrases only **42%** ([Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)). Small words (it, I, in) sit on weak positions in rock songs ([Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)).

**Established.** Style changes how strict the rule is. In ten nineteenth-century English songs, **no** stressed syllable fell off the beat. In 80 rock songs, **23%** did, and about three quarters of those arrived early, just ahead of the beat, not late ([Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)). Both sets were in 4/4.

**Established.** A stressed syllable on a weak beat bothers listeners more than a weak syllable on a strong beat. Listeners preferred the matched setting in about 73 to 75% of paired trials ([De Castro-Arrazola 2018](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2951725/view)). That test used Dutch listeners and a four-beat pattern.

**Established, and smaller than you might expect.** Matching stress to beat helps listeners catch the words, but only a little: about **85% against 75%** of sung words identified. Whether the word is a familiar one matters far more ([Johnson, Huron & Collister 2014](https://core.ac.uk/download/20329824.pdf)). So stress placement is mostly about sounding natural, and less about being understood.

**Established.** Singers avoid long gaps inside a line. A stressed syllable is allowed onto a weaker beat when the other choice is a long stretch with no new syllable ([Hayes, Textsetting as Constraint Conflict](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)).

**Craft advice.** Main stresses go on the downbeat. Articles, pronouns and linking words should stay off it ([Pattison, Setting Words to Music](https://www.patpattison.com/setting-words-to-music)).

**Our inference.** Your own counts fit all of this. In your 538 waltz sheets, 65% of stresses sit on beat one, 28% on beats two or three, and 7% off the beat. In your six 6/8 hymns, 52% sit on the first main beat and 33% on the second. Nothing published tells us whether 65% is high or low. It is simply the best target available. The 7% off-beat share sits sensibly between old song (0%) and rock (23%).

## Lines start early and finish early

**Established.** The most common English folk line has eight syllables, alternating weak and strong, starting with a weak syllable placed *before* the first strong beat ([Hayes 2005](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)). That weak opening syllable is the pickup. Listeners hear short notes leading into a longer note as a pickup ([London, Himberg & Cross 2009](https://cdn.carleton.edu/uploads/sites/721/2021/12/London-Himberg-Cross-2009-MP.pdf)).

**Established.** Every line gets the same amount of time: four strong beats. A short line does not get a shorter slot. It fills three beats and leaves the fourth empty or held. English lines are trimmed at the end, never at the start ([Hayes & Schuh, Quatrain I](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12QuatrainI.pdf)).

**Established.** In 1,028 Appalachian folk songs, the end of the line goes to silent beats that are "clearly noticeable as an obligatory pause". Only about **10%** of songs let a line take time from its neighbour ([Hayes & MacEachern 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachern1996AreThereLinesInFolkPoetry.pdf)).

**Established.** The two commonest four-line verse shapes in that folk collection are four full lines (203 songs) and long-short-long-short (188 songs). A shape with the short lines first barely appears ([Hayes & Schuh, Quatrain I](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12QuatrainI.pdf)). Shortening is a closing gesture.

**Established.** In classic American popular song the basic unit is two bars, and phrases build in twos, fours and eights. The last word of a phrase often holds through the final two bars ([Callahan 2013](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)). Across 1,268 German folk songs, a long note or a gap marks about half of all phrase joins, while a leap in pitch marks about a third ([Rodríguez-López & Volk 2013](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/289643/MRodriguez-CMMR13-corrected.pdf?sequence=1)).

**Craft advice.** Lines that start on the downbeat, or pick up into it, feel solid. Lines that start after the downbeat feel unsettled, and more so the later they start ([Pattison, The Art of Phrasing](https://www.patpattison.com/art-of-phrasing)).

**Our inference: how many bars a line takes.** No source states this for 3/4 or 6/8. Working from the four-strong-beat line: 3/4 has one strong beat a bar, so a normal line takes **four bars**. 6/8 has two strong beats a bar, so a line takes **two bars**, or four in a slow song. 12/8 takes one bar, or two when slow. This matches your waltz sheets, which average about 2.3 notes a bar with roughly one bar in five a single held note. That is what a four-bar line with a long last note looks like. The engine's two-bar waltz lines explain its 3.5 notes a bar.

**Our inference: pickups are not optional.** If the first stressed syllable must land on the bar line, any weak syllables before it must fall before the bar line, inside the tail of the previous line's slot. So the previous line has to stop in time to leave that room.

## The title line needs a clear bar before it

**Established (industry count).** In Billboard top-ten hits of 2022 and 2023, the title most often sits in the first or last line of the chorus ([Blume, BMI](https://www.bmi.com/news/entry/the-best-place-for-your-song-title)). In classic American song, the commonest lyric shape is a list that ends in a reveal, the reveal "is often the title", and it arrives at the end of the phrase where the last word can be held ([Callahan 2013](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)).

**Established.** A break in time is what makes a line stand out. The silent beat at the end of one line is what sets off the next ([Hayes & Schuh, Quatrain I](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12QuatrainI.pdf)).

**Craft advice.** For a line you really mean, "look for downbeats and look for strong bars". Coming in slightly early makes the punch stronger ([Pattison, The Art of Phrasing](https://www.patpattison.com/art-of-phrasing)).

**Our inference.** A title line stands out through three things together: a gap before it, a start on the bar line, and a long last note. When the engine lets the line before the title run to the end of its slot, it removes the gap and pushes the title late. We found nothing in any source that supports a hook entering late because the previous line overran. Even rock, the loosest style measured, brings stresses in early, not late.

## Loved tunes in three range past an octave

**Established.** The middle value for range in European folk songs is **12 semitones**, a full octave. The commonest melody shape is an arch, up then down ([Baker, MeloSol Corpus](https://www.osu.tests.sfulib4.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/EMR/article/view/7645/6267)). That figure covers all metres together. No study we found separates songs in three from songs in four.

**Established: the feel of each metre.** 3/4 has three beats that each split in two. 6/8 has two beats that each split in three. 12/8 has four such beats ([Open Music Theory](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/compound-meters-and-time-signatures/)). In jig-type tunes the typical 6/8 bar is long-short, long-short, or six running eighth notes ([Wikipedia, Jig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jig)).

**Established: tempo.** Slow ballroom waltz runs at about 88 beats a minute and Viennese waltz at about 168 ([Stanford Social Dance](https://socialdance.stanford.edu/Syllabi/DJtips.htm)). Pop and rock songs in 6/8 average about 60 main beats a minute and none in the samples passes 80. Listeners settle on bars lasting about **two seconds** ([de Clercq 2016](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.declercq.php)).

**Established: by style, what little was confirmed.**

| Style | What a source shows |
|---|---|
| Folk-rock in three | "Norwegian Wood": an eight-bar hook falling a full octave over one held chord, repeated in every verse ([Pollack](https://www.icce.rug.nl/~soundscapes/DATABASES/AWP/nw.shtml)) |
| Indie waltz | Elliott Smith's "Waltz #1": long held notes with lots of space ([Soundfly](https://flypaper.soundfly.com/write/exploring-the-lydian-mode-with-elliott-smiths-waltz-1/)) |
| Country waltz | Eighth notes are usually swung, long-short, not even ([de Clercq 2017](https://www.midside.com/presentations/declercq_2017_pip_poster.pdf)) |
| Lullaby | Listeners pick out lullabies by repetition (43% named it) and falling lines ([Trehub, Unyk & Trainor 1993](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/1993_Trainor_Unyk.pdf)) |
| Slow blues, early rock | Slow 12/8 at about 55 to 92 beats a minute ([de Clercq 2016](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.declercq.php)) |

**A warning about labels.** Sources disagree on which metre well-known songs are in. "Norwegian Wood" is heard as 3/4, 6/8 and 12/8 by three different analysts ([de Clercq 2016](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.declercq.php)). Our inference: your 538 "3/4" sheets very likely include some tunes with a 6/8 feel, and the counts are worth re-checking with a tempo filter.

**Our inference, from craft knowledge and not from a source.** Waltz tunes tend to spell out the chord: with one chord a bar and three beats, the tune naturally walks root, third, fifth. Rhythm repeats almost exactly from bar to bar while the pitches move up or down a step each time. An engine that keeps a waltz to small steps inside a narrow span will come out with too few pitches and too little range, which is what you are seeing.

## What this means for the engine's three faults

Everything in this section is **our inference**. No source describes how to build a songwriting engine.

**Fault (a): stresses on any beat.** Aim for your own measured mix, not for every stress on beat one. That means roughly 65 / 28 / 7 in 3/4 (beat one, beats two or three, off the beat), and roughly 52 / 33 / 15 in 6/8 (first main beat, second main beat, elsewhere). Inside that mix, apply these rules, strictest first:

1. Never put the weak syllable of a longer word on a stronger beat than that word's stressed syllable. Treat this as close to a hard rule.
2. Beat one should almost always carry a stressed syllable or a held note. Not every stress needs beat one.
3. Punish a stressed syllable on a weak beat more than a weak syllable on a strong beat.
4. Prefer a stress on beat two or three to leaving a long gap with no syllable.
5. Make off-beat stress a style dial: near zero for hymn and folk, higher for pop and rock.

In 6/8, treat the bar as two levels: first eighth strongest, fourth eighth next, the other four weak.

**Fault (b): the long line overruns and the title enters late.** Four changes follow.

- Size a line's slot by its count of stressed syllables, not its syllable count. A three-stress line gets the same slot as its four-stress partner.
- In 3/4, make four bars the default for a full line. Keep two-bar slots for half-lines. Avoid three-bar slots except as a deliberate effect, since every source describes phrases built in twos and fours.
- Fill from the front and let the line end early. Measure the "free tail": the time between the start of the last syllable and the end of the slot. It should normally be at least one strong beat.
- Before a title line, the previous line should land its last syllable by the first beat of its final bar, then hold or rest, leaving room only for the title's pickup. If the previous line has too many syllables for that, give that line more bars or pack it tighter. **Never move the title.**

Your own example shows the problem. Thirteen syllables in three bars of 6/8 is not too fast (about four a bar, under the ceiling of six). It is too long for a slot that must also hold its own ending. Four bars leaves a bar and a half free. A title line with five stresses in seven syllables wants a strong beat for each stress, so in 6/8 it needs three bars with the last stress held, or four.

**Fault (c): narrow 3/4 range.** Aim for the 14 to 16 semitones and about 11 different pitches in your own waltz sheets, with 12 semitones as a floor. The cheapest way there is to let the tune walk through the chord across more than an octave, allow a leap at the start of a phrase, and keep one bar-rhythm repeating while the pitches shift up or down. That adds range without making the rhythm busier. End phrases with a long note, not a leap.

## What we could not find

These gaps are real, and they limit how much weight the advice above can bear.

- **No published count of where stresses fall, beat by beat, in English 3/4 or 6/8 songs.** The biggest chanting study corrected singers who offered a three-beat setting ([Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)). The rock study dropped songs outside 4/4 and 2/4.
- **No count of how often lines start with a pickup**, in any metre or style.
- **No published line length in bars for waltzes or 6/8 songs**, and no figures for syllables per bar. The four-bar waltz line is our mapping plus common knowledge among musicians.
- **No test of whether a rest before a hook makes it more memorable.** That idea rests on teaching and on the folk-verse theory.
- **No study comparing range, leaps or repetition between songs in three and songs in four.** The wider range in your waltz sheets could be a commercial-song effect and not a waltz effect. Comparing against your own 4/4 sheets would settle it.
- **No analyses retrieved for Tin Pan Alley waltzes, country waltzes, doo-wop ballads, triple-time hymns or sea shanties.** The style table above is thin for that reason. Lists of song titles by metre contradict each other and should be treated as leads only.
- **Your 6/8 figures rest on six hymns**, which is a very small sample.
- **Several standard books and papers were not read directly** (Lerdahl & Jackendoff, Temperley 1999, Halle & Lerdahl, Palmer & Krumhansl's beat-strength numbers).
- **Checks still owed.** The researchers read pages through a summarising tool, so exact quotes and figures should be checked before reuse. The meaning of the 10% "borrowing" figure in the folk study was not confirmed in detail. The one study that included 3/4 songs is an unreviewed preprint with figures that do not agree with each other, and we left it out.

## Conclusion

The three faults are one fault seen from three sides. The engine treats a bar as a container to fill evenly. The sources describe a bar in three as a strong beat followed by room, and a line as four strong beats followed by a gap. Give the strong beat its stress and the line its gap, and on-time title entry follows without a separate rule.

The other lesson is that your own corpus is ahead of the published research on this question. The most useful next step is more counting in your own lead sheets, not more reading: how often lines start with a pickup, how long the last note and rest take, how often a bar's rhythm repeats, and how range compares with your 4/4 songs. Those four counts would turn most of the inferences above into measured targets.
