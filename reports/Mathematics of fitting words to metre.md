# Level-indexed weights solve the missing cell

The engine's gap is also a gap in the published research: every pop study of stressed-syllable placement dropped songs outside 4/4 and 2/4 before counting, so no measured figure exists for 3/4 or 6/8 pop in any source we read. The mathematics to bridge it does exist. Metrical strength is defined by one tree-building rule that works in every metre, so a rule stated by *level* (bar, beat, division of the beat) and not by slot number is automatically defined in 4/4, 3/4, 6/8 and 12/8. The formal word-setting models (Hayes's ranked constraints, and the maximum-entropy formula that turns them into weights) are written in exactly those level terms, although nobody has published fitted weights for English pop in any metre, or for triple metre at all. The missing metre-by-style cell cannot safely be solved by simple algebra on rates, because the songbook style has a rate of zero for the pop habit (0 of 602 stressed syllables off the beat, against 33% in rock), and a ratio built on zero blows up. The defensible form is a two-step model: place syllables by the metre's own songbook rule, then apply a "pop shift" that moves a stressed syllable one division early with a probability measured in 4/4 pop (about 0.35 to 0.55 depending on the target beat). Hiding a cell the engine does have, predicting it, and scoring the miss gives the honest error bar. For repetition, the engine's 1-in-100 exact two-bar repeat sits 20 to 30 times below every published yardstick and below the team's own 23-in-100 count. Two openly licensed sources could fill the gap directly: 214 hand-transcribed pop songs with syllables under the notes (CoCoPops-Billboard, CC BY 4.0) and roughly 8,000 karaoke-style MIDI files with English words (Lakh MIDI, CC BY 4.0 on the collection), neither yet counted by metre.

**How to read this report.** Each claim carries one of three labels. **Measured** means a study or corpus count reports it. **Theory only** means a published model states it without data behind that specific point. **Our inference** means the researchers worked it out by arithmetic or design reasoning and no source states it. The researchers read most papers through a summarising tool, so exact table cells should be checked against the originals before they are hard-coded. No data from TheSession.org was used and none is recommended.

## The formal models supply the equation but no triple-metre numbers

### Hayes's grammar is ten ranked constraints on a 16-slot line

**Measured.** Hayes models a sung line as 16 slots in three strength classes, S (strong), M (medium) and W (weak), in the repeating pattern S W M W. A setting is a choice of which slots start a syllable. Ten constraints, strictest first, pick the winner: no syllable in an extra-weak slot; no overflow past the line; no lapse (three empty slots in a row); no empty S; no stressless syllable in S; no stressed syllable in W; no stressless syllable in W; "strong is long"; no stressed syllable in M; no empty M. The "strong is long" count is V = Σ over the four strong slots of (3 − e_k), where e_k is the number of weak-level slots after strong slot k that stay free of a new syllable. On 364 folk lines where at least four of nine speakers agreed, this ranking matched the favourite setting on **70.6%** of lines, against **23.1%** for the older Halle and Lerdahl procedure ([Hayes, Textsetting as Constraint Conflict](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)). The paper reports a ranking, found by hill-climbing, and no numeric weights. Speakers themselves do not agree perfectly: ten speakers produced 3.41 different settings per line on average, and the top setting took 6.7 of 11 votes ([Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)). So about 70% line agreement is near the ceiling for any stress-only rule, and there is no single right answer to hit.

**Measured.** None of this work covers triple metre. Speakers who offered a three-beat chant were corrected to a two-beat one ([Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)), and the quatrain study's only mention of compound time is a single 9/8 song ([Hayes & MacEachern, appendices](https://brucehayes.org/papers/AppendicesToHayesAndMacEachern1998.pdf)).

**Measured.** The only published numbers that behave like weights in Hayes's song work are the eight "strictness ranges" of the quatrain model. Each constraint draws a random value from its range on every trial, the draws set a ranking, and the predicted frequency of a verse shape is the share of trials it wins. Fitted ranges run from COUPLETS ARE SALIENT at .900 to 1.900 down to PREFER LONG-LAST at .009 to 1.009. The model placed 601 of 627 quatrains (95.9%) inside its predicted types, against a 4.2% chance baseline, and predicted the two commonest shapes almost exactly (202.7 against 203 observed; 194.3 against 188) ([Hayes & MacEachern 1998](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachernQuatrainForm1998.pdf)). It has no closed-form probability.

### The maximum-entropy formula turns constraints into fitted weights

**Measured (for spoken verse, not song).** Hayes, Wilson and Shisko give the template. The probability of a line L is

p(L) = exp(−Σᵢ λᵢ·χᵢ(L)) / Z, with Z = Σⱼ exp(−Σᵢ λᵢ·χᵢ(Lⱼ)),

where χᵢ(L) is the number of times L breaks constraint i and λᵢ ≥ 0 is that constraint's weight. Weights are fitted by maximum likelihood with a Gaussian prior. A constraint earns its place by a likelihood-ratio test, −2·ln[P(data | simpler) / P(data | full)], checked against chi-square with one degree of freedom. Fitted magnitudes for Shakespeare run from about 0.3 for mild preferences to about 3 for "no stress in a weak position" and 11 for word-boundary alignment ([Hayes, Wilson & Shisko 2012](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesWilsonShiskoMaxentMetrics2012.pdf)).

**Measured, on six art songs only.** Girardi and Plag fitted this kind of model to English art songs using the level scheme described in the next section. For the one 3/4 song (Haydn's "The Wanderer", 130 syllables) the weights came out at **5.00** against a stressed syllable below beat level, **5.00** against an unstressed syllable on the downbeat, and **0.00** for either kind on beats 2 and 3. Word stress explained 68% of the variance in metrical strength in that song, against 52% and 39% in two duple Haydn songs ([Girardi & Plag 2019](https://www.anglistik.hhu.de/fileadmin/redaktion/Fakultaeten/Philosophische_Fakultaet/Anglistik_und_Amerikanistik/Ang3_Linguistics/Dateien/Detailseiten/Plag/2019/Girardi_Plag_Textsetting_2019-09-19.pdf)). These are the only fitted weight tables for English song that we found. The identical 5.00 and 0.00 values look like weights pinned by all-or-nothing data, not graded estimates, and the column alignment in the copy read was partly uncertain.

**Our inference: the model the engine can fit.** Combine Hayes's candidates with the 2012 formula. For a line x with n syllables on a grid of P slots, the candidates are the C(P, n) order-preserving placements (Hayes's own bound of "about 13,000" for 16 slots is C(16, 8) = 12,870). Then

P(setting s | line x) = exp(−w·v(x, s)) / Σ over all s′ of exp(−w·v(x, s′)),

where v counts violations. The log-likelihood is concave, so fitting is ordinary multinomial logistic regression. Two bars of 3/4 or 6/8 at eighth-note resolution is 12 slots (at most C(12, 6) = 924 candidates); a four-bar waltz line is 24 slots (at most about 2.7 million, still easy with dynamic programming). Because the constraints name strength classes and not slot numbers, moving to triple metre needs a relabelling of slots plus a restatement of two constraints whose constants are tied to a four-slot beat: the lapse threshold (two empty divisions between beats is normal in 6/8) and the "3" in strong-is-long. Whether weights fitted in 4/4 survive that move is untested in print. That is precisely the hold-out test set out below.

One working precedent shows the approach handles "fit new words to a fixed bar structure": a ranked grammar for Kaytetye song, on an S W M W grid, with candidates generated as ways of spreading syllables across measures and constraints including FINAL IS LONG (3 minus the slots held by the last syllable), picked the attested setting for 71 of 89 texts, **79.7%** ([Textsetting in Kaytetye](https://journals.linguisticsociety.org/proceedings/index.php/amphonology/article/download/4911/4648/9197)).

## Strength is a tree, so rules written by level travel between metres

### One construction gives the weights for every metre

**Theory only.** Every formal scheme builds metrical weights the same way. Give each level a subdivision factor and a weight. Start with the bar. To go down a level, each existing slot keeps its weight and gains (factor − 1) new children carrying the new, lower weight. The subdivision lists are 4/4 = ⟨1, 2, 2⟩, 3/4 = ⟨1, 3, 2⟩ and 6/8 = ⟨1, 2, 3⟩. With the Longuet-Higgins and Lee convention (weight = minus the level number) the tables are as follows ([Song, QMUL thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)).

| Metre | Tree | Weight of each eighth-note slot | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3/4 | 3 beats, each split in 2 | 0, −2, −1, −2, −1, −2 | Printed in source |
| 6/8 | 2 beats, each split in 3 | 0, −2, −2, −1, −2, −2 | Printed in source |
| 4/4 | 2 × 2 beats, each split in 2 | 0, −3, −2, −3, −1, −3, −2, −3 | Our application of the rule (source prints the quarter-note level 0, −2, −1, −2) |
| 12/8 | 2 × 2 beats, each split in 3 | 0, −3, −3, −2, −3, −3, −1, −3, −3, −2, −3, −3 | Our application of the rule |

The same six eighth-note slots carry different weights in 3/4 and 6/8 only because the tree differs. In every tree scheme, **beats 2 and 3 of 3/4 are exactly equal**; only data could separate them.

**Theory only.** The syncopation score built on these weights: write a rhythm as notes and rests (or ties), each with its slot weight; for every rest whose weight is at least that of the nearest preceding note, add (rest weight − note weight) ([Song, QMUL thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)). In words, a syncopation is a sound on a weaker slot held through a stronger, silent one, and its size is the gap in levels ([Ladinig & Honing](https://eprints.illc.uva.nl/id/document/1077)). This score needs no lyrics, which matters later.

**Measured.** Temperley and Temperley's level scheme is the one stress-measuring scheme already defined for compound time. Level 2 (the felt beat) is the quarter note in 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 and the dotted quarter in 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8. Level 3 is the whole bar in 2/4, 3/4, 6/8 and 9/8 but the half-bar in 4/4 and 12/8. Level 4, the whole bar, exists only in 4/4 and 12/8. Level 1 is everything below the beat. Their summary number is SMAV = mean level of stressable syllables − mean level of unstressable syllables. It came out at **1.15** for ten English songs (stressable 3.08, unstressable 1.93) and 0.77 for 54 French songs. Scrambling the words against the tune dropped it from 0.99 to 0.24 in eight-syllable lines ([Temperley & Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)). The paper pools all metres and gives no split by time signature.

### Measured profiles agree with the tree in 4/4 and are missing for threes

**Measured.** In 4/4 the tree is roughly right but not exact. A count of 75,242 note onsets in Essen 4/4 folk songs puts 25.5% on the downbeat, 19.0% on beat 3, 17.9% on beat 4 and 13.6% on beat 2 ([Murphy 2020](https://musmat.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/05-Scott.pdf)). Listener-derived salience shows a different wrinkle: beat 2 (1.86) above beat 3 (1.69) above beat 4 (1.33), a strength that decays through the bar ([Ladinig & Honing](https://eprints.illc.uva.nl/id/document/1077)). Palmer and Krumhansl charted profiles for 3/4 and 6/8 in 1990, but only as figures; no numbers were recoverable ([London, Polak & Jacoby 2016](https://core.ac.uk/download/83231804.pdf)). No published numeric profile for 3/4 or 6/8 was found.

**Our inference.** Whether beat 2 of 3/4 outranks beat 3 is therefore an open number, not a settled one. The 4/4 decay suggests beat 2 is a little stronger; beat 3's job as a pickup suggests the reverse. The engine's worded songbook tunes in 3/4 can settle it with one extra model feature and a likelihood-ratio test.

### Level-based models lose little fit and keep parameters that exist everywhere

**Measured.** Temperley compared six ways of predicting where notes fall in 4/4, scoring each by cross-entropy on held-out songs, H = −(1/N)·Σ ln P(item). On 1,235 Essen melodies (988 to train, 247 to test) the results were as follows ([Temperley 2010](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)).

| Model | What decides a note | Parameters | Cross-entropy per song |
|---|---|---|---|
| Uniform | Same chance in every slot | 1 | 62.37 |
| Metrical position | One probability per level | 4 | 42.47 |
| Fine-grained position | One probability per slot | 8 | 40.21 |
| Hierarchical position | Level, plus whether the neighbouring stronger beats sound | 13 | 38.76 |
| First-order duration | Slot of next note given slot of this one | 56 | 37.36 |

Four level parameters capture most of the structure. Adding the two neighbour questions nearly matches a model with 56 parameters. At the half-bar level the fitted note probability is .713 when both stronger neighbours sound, .889 when only the one before does, .265 when only the one after does, and .062 when neither does.

**Theory only.** Temperley notes, without testing it, that the hierarchical model in 3/4 would need about 19 parameters of which **6 could be shared with 4/4** (the two lowest levels), while the slot-to-slot model would need about 132 new ones with nothing shared ([Temperley 2010](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)). This is the one published seed of metre transfer.

**Our inference.** For an empty cell, choose the level-indexed form. Index every slot by its level relative to the felt beat: T+1 (bar or half-bar), T (beat), T−1 (first division), T−2 (second division). In 4/4 pop T is the quarter with two divisions; in 6/8 and 12/8 T is the dotted quarter with three; in 3/4 T is the quarter, three to the bar. The only genuinely new structures in threes are a level with three children, which gives two "weak siblings" where duple time has one, and the question of whether those two behave alike.

### Raw "share on strong beats" does not compare across metres

**Measured.** The one study that splits by metre used 56 piano-book songs (26 in 3/4, 30 in 4/4). Stressed syllables fell on strong beats **50.8%** of the time in 3/4 against **65.7%** in 4/4; keywords 71.1% against 85.5% ([Keywords and Strong Beats, arXiv 2412.04202](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04202v1)). The paper is a small unreviewed preprint, its own figures disagree in places, and it does not say exactly which 3/4 beats count as strong.

**Our inference.** Part of that 15-point drop is plain arithmetic. If only beat 1 counts as strong, 3/4 offers one strong slot in three beats where 4/4 offers two in four. The top level also differs (level 3 in 3/4, level 4 in 4/4), so even SMAV is not directly comparable between metres. Two quantities do compare fairly: the *weights* of a maximum-entropy model, which already allow for how many slots of each level a metre offers, and the *ratio of real to scrambled* alignment within each metre.

## Solving for the missing cell needs a two-step model and a hold-out test

### The algebra, and why the simple version breaks

**Measured.** No published model factorises a rhythm statistic into a metre effect and a style effect, or completes a genre-by-metre table. The nearest pieces are Temperley's parameter-sharing remark and a regression of syncopation on tempo and year in rock ([Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)).

**Our inference.** The textbook way to solve for a missing cell is an additive model on the log-odds scale. Let p(m, s) be the chance that a stressed syllable lands in some context (say, "one division before a beat, with the beat left empty") in metre m and style s, and let logit p = ln[p / (1 − p)]. Assume logit p(m, s) = μ + α(m) + β(s). Then

logit p(3/4, pop) = logit p(3/4, songbook) + logit p(4/4, pop) − logit p(4/4, songbook),

or in odds, odds(3/4 pop) = odds(3/4 songbook) × [odds(4/4 pop) / odds(4/4 songbook)]. The bracket is the "pop factor". This is three knowns and one unknown, exactly as in school algebra.

It fails on the real numbers. Eighth-level stress syncopation is **0.228** in rock and **0 of 602** in nineteenth-century English song ([Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)). Zero has no log-odds. Adding half a count gives 0.5/603 = 0.00083, and the pop factor becomes (0.228/0.772)/0.00083 ≈ **356**. A multiplier that size, applied to any small songbook rate, either saturates or returns nonsense. When the base style has almost none of a behaviour, a multiplicative style factor cannot be identified. The pop habit has to be modelled as a separate process laid on top.

### The two-step model: metre places, style shifts

**Measured inputs.** In 80 rock songs (all 4/4), a stressed syllable that either sits on a beat or anticipates it by an eighth with nothing on the beat splits as follows ([Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)).

| Target beat | On the beat | An eighth early | Share early, q |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beat 1 | 1,028 | 1,046 | 0.504 |
| Beat 3 | 639 | 794 | 0.554 |
| Beat 2 | 389 | 305 | 0.439 |
| Beat 4 | 438 | 237 | 0.351 |

The four "early" counts sum to 2,382, which is the whole eighth-level numerator: 2,382 of 10,433 stressed syllables, SQ = 0.228. A further 0.105 are syncopated at the sixteenth level, 0.333 combined. About three quarters of all syncopations arrive early and not late (0.772 at the eighth level). The rate rises with tempo and with year (logistic coefficients 0.0119 per BPM and 0.0337 per year at the eighth level). A typical shift is about 250 ms. In chart pop across the twentieth century, anticipatory syncopation rose from 1.5% of syllables in 1900 to 19.3% in 1999 ([VanderStel & Temperley](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/vanderstel-temperley-jnmr.pdf)).

**Our inference: the model.** Step 1, plain placement: assign each stressed syllable a position using the metre's own songbook-style distribution, which the engine has for 4/4, 3/4 and 6/8. Step 2, pop shift: with probability q(target level, tempo) move that syllable one division early and leave the target beat unsung. If π_b is the songbook share of stresses aimed at beat b and q_b the shift probability, the predicted pop share arriving early is Σ_b π_b·q_b. Carried across by level, the targets in 6/8 are the two dotted-quarter beats and "one division early" is the third eighth of the previous beat; in 12/8 the four beats map one-for-one onto 4/4's; in 3/4 the targets are the three quarter beats and the early slot is the off-beat eighth. In Hayes's terms the shift is a demotion of "no empty strong slot" and "no stressed syllable in a weak slot" in the one case where the weak slot sits just before the empty strong one. One caution on the inputs: the q table conditions on a subset of stressed syllables (4,876 of 10,433), so the engine should recompute q from the public 80-song stress files under its own definitions before using the values.

**Our inference: four choices the literature cannot settle.** (a) Whether q attaches to the notated level or to clock time. Pop bars last about two seconds in any metre, with 6/8 averaging 50 to 64 dotted-quarter beats a minute ([de Clercq 2015](https://www.midside.com/presentations/declercq_2015_ann_arbor_slides.pdf)). At 120 BPM in 4/4 an eighth lasts 250 ms; at 62 in 6/8 an eighth lasts about 320 ms and a sixteenth 160 ms. If anticipation is a habit of arriving 125 to 250 ms early, slow 6/8 pop will anticipate by a sixteenth as often as by an eighth. (b) Whether, in a three-way split, the shift goes only to the third division or also to the second. (c) Whether beats 2 and 3 of 3/4 take the 4/4 rate for beats 2 and 4 (about 0.35 to 0.44) or something else. (d) Whether 12/8 pop behaves as 4/4 with triplets or as doubled 6/8.

### Hold-out design: hide a cell you have, predict it, score the miss

**Measured practice.** The standard test in this literature is a train and test split scored by cross-entropy and checked by k-fold cross-validation ([Temperley 2010](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)). For a two-way table with one value per cell, the additive model leaves no spare degrees of freedom to test a general interaction; Tukey's one-degree-of-freedom test checks the specific form y = μ + a + b + λ·a·b ([Tukey's test of additivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tukey%27s_test_of_additivity)).

**Our inference.** With four known cells (4/4 pop, 4/4 songbook, 3/4 songbook, 6/8 songbook) and two unknown (3/4 pop, 6/8 pop), three tests are possible now. First, a metre-transfer test with no style change: hide 3/4 songbook, predict it from 4/4 songbook through the level labels alone, and score cross-entropy per stressed syllable against the real data, beside two baselines (uniform over slots, and the theoretical tree). Repeat hiding 6/8, and predict 3/4 from 6/8. Second, a style-transfer test inside 4/4: fit q on earlier pop, predict later pop, since a year effect is already measured. Third, an additivity test, which needs spare degrees of freedom. Get them by treating levels as repeated measures (a metre × style × level table) and by adding a third style with all metres filled, such as hymn or art song, making a 3 × 3 table with only pop-in-threes empty. Hide each known cell in turn and predict it. **The spread of those leave-one-cell-out errors is the error bar for the pop-in-threes prediction.** If a Bayesian form is wanted, put a Beta prior on q in the new metre centred on the 4/4 value with a width set from that spread; with a prior Beta(a, b) and k early arrivals among n annotated targets, the updated mean is (a + k)/(a + b + n). With no data the answer is the transfer estimate with its error bar, and every annotated song sharpens it.

**Our inference: the wordless pop melodies are not useless.** The engine's 3/4 and 6/8 pop tunes have notes, and the syncopation score above needs only notes. A stressed syllable shifted early leaves a note on a weak slot with silence on the next beat, which is visible without words. So the rate of "note on the last division before a beat, beat empty" can be counted in the wordless pop tunes and in the worded 4/4 pop tunes alike. In 4/4, where both exist, the ratio of stressed-syllable anticipations to all note anticipations can be measured, and that ratio carried over. This turns half of the unknown cell into a measurement.

**Measured, and a reason the speech side transfers.** The long-short contrast that English speech hands to melody does not depend on metre: the rhythm-contrast index (nPVI) of instrumental themes is 42.98 in binary and 45.25 in ternary metre, p = 0.28 ([Patel & Daniele 2003](https://ccc.inaoep.mx/~villasen/bib/ritmo%20lenguage%20y%20musical%20-Patel_Daniele_Cognition.pdf)). One prosody layer can serve every metre; only the grid changes.

## Songs repeat a two-bar rhythm one time in four; the engine, one in a hundred

### Measured rates

**Team figures (supplied, not published).** Engine builds repeat a two-bar rhythm exactly about 1 time in 100. The project's corpus of pop melodies in 3/4 does so about 23 times in 100.

**Measured.** No study reports that exact statistic, but every neighbouring number points the same way. In 909 Chinese pop songs, 65% to 85% of a song's duration is repeated material for 79% of songs, and more than half of phrases repeat immediately. In 348 older American popular songs, 7% of phrases use the same rhythm in every bar and a further 28% follow a strict bar layout such as abab. The best predictor of the opening notes of a repeat weights the song's own earlier material at 0.7 and the general corpus at 0.3, P(x) = 0.7·P_song(x) + 0.3·P_corpus(x). A neural generator trained on the same songs used a larger rhythm vocabulary than real songs, so under-repetition is a known machine fault ([Dai et al., arXiv 2209.00182](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.00182)). In 700 chart songs, the statement, restatement, departure, conclusion layout (SRDC, usually four bars each) describes about 60% of strophes from 1964 on ([Summach, MTO 17.3](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.php)). Recent hits reuse their top rhythmic cells more heavily than older ones: the rank-frequency slope of rhythmic four-note cells steepens from −4.22 (1955 to 1997) to −6.93 (2015 to 2019) ([White, Pater & Breen 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10356498)). All of these studies kept only 4/4.

**Our inference.** In a 16-bar SRDC unit of eight two-bar cells, the restatement repeats the statement, so 2 of 8 cells (25%) repeat even if the rest is new, rising toward 4 of 8 when the conclusion returns to the opening. With about half of strophes built this way, the form literature predicts a within-section exact repeat rate of roughly 20% to 35%. The team's 23% sits inside that range. A working band of 15% to 35% exact, plus a further share of near repeats, is defensible; no source states it. A bar of 3/4 also has fewer slots than a bar of 4/4 (2¹² possible two-bar onset patterns at eighth resolution against 2¹⁶), so chance alone makes exact repeats commoner in 3/4. Targets should be held per metre.

### The mathematics of "same rhythm" and "nearly the same"

**Theory only (definitions).** A rhythm cell is a string of 1s and 0s on the slot grid. Hamming distance counts the slots that differ. Swap distance is the fewest exchanges of a neighbouring note and rest that turn one cell into the other; a directed version handles unequal note counts. Chronotonic distance draws each rhythm as a step function whose height over each gap equals that gap's length and takes the area between the two curves, K = ∫|f₁ − f₂| dx; it did best in Toussaint's tests, with swap distance close behind ([Toussaint, ISMIR 2004](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2004/paper/000134.pdf)). For whole songs, compression measures repetition: percent reduction = 1 − compressed length / original length ([Morris, The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/2017/05/song-repetition/)). In Meredith's point-set version, a pattern P with its set V of repeat offsets has compression ratio CR = |covered points| / (|P| + |V| − 1); classical pieces typically score between 2 and 4 ([Meredith, COSIATEC](https://core.ac.uk/download/60675404.pdf)). Information measures give the listener's view: surprise of an event is −log₂ p(event | context); for a Markov chain with transition matrix a, the predictive information rate is h(a²) − h(a), which is zero both for fully fixed and fully random sequences and peaks in between ([Abdallah & Plumbley](https://core.ac.uk/download/30696123.pdf)).

**Measured.** That middle peak has support in listeners. Of 57 studies, 50 fit an inverted-U between liking and familiarity or complexity ([Chmiel & Schubert 2017](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/back-to-the-inverted-u-for-music-preference-a-review-of-the-liter/)). In direct tests, liking shows a negative quadratic term in information content (β = −0.09, then −0.18 in a second study), and listeners like surprise most when they are confident, and predictability most when they are unsure ([Gold et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867811)). No study converts this into a percentage of bars to repeat. The practical reading is a placement rule (our inference): repeat exactly where the listener is unsure (new section, new words) and depart where the listener is confident (after two statements, at a cadence).

### Refitting a tune to later verses

**Measured.** The linguistic models treat the tune as a fixed grid and each verse as a fresh setting on it. Empty slots are "freely tolerated"; every syllable must get a slot; only 10.9% of chanted settings start on the first strong beat with nothing before it, so about 89% begin with a pickup ([Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)). In 1,028 Appalachian songs, about **10.4%** borrow slots from a neighbouring line to hold extra syllables; borrowing backward takes at most the previous line's last strong beat and stays fixed through the song, while borrowing forward takes only weak or medium slots and is occasional ([Hayes & MacEachern 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachern1996AreThereLinesInFolkPoetry.pdf)). Melisma (one syllable over several notes) is rare in older song: 4.46% of syllables in 3,724 Dutch folk songs ([De Castro-Arrazola et al. 2015](https://vdca.netlify.com/pdf/decastro-arrazola2015_fma-dutch-textsetting.pdf)), under 1% of notes in French traditional song, and about 7.8% of notes in twentieth-century chart pop (derived from the published counts) ([VanderStel & Temperley](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/vanderstel-temperley-jnmr.pdf)).

**Measured, French only.** The single table comparing verses note by note covers 48 French songs and 1,899 matched syllable pairs. Of 598 stressable syllables in verse 1, **54%** are matched by a stressable syllable on the same note in verse 2; of 1,301 unstressable ones, 21% receive a stressable one. Alignment weakens in later verses: SMAV 0.51 in verse 1 against 0.33 in verse 2 ([Temperley & Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)). So real later verses give up some stress matching to keep the tune.

**Theory only.** Dell and Halle's "positional parallelism" says the same line of every stanza has the same syllable count and the same melisma layout; it holds in traditional French song and is "quite common[ly]" broken in English ([Dell & Halle 2009](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/lfab.2.03del/html)). No English counts were found, and no study counts how many notes verse 2 changes in pop, hymn or folk song.

**Our inference.** Hayes's grammar has no term for "stay like verse 1". Add one: a weight per kind of departure from the verse-1 rhythm (onset added, onset removed, onset moved), with a much larger weight when the departure touches a strong slot. With melisma under 8%, one extra syllable almost always means one extra note, so the choice is where the note goes: a pickup before the first strong beat, a split of a longer note on a weak slot, or a tie where a syllable is missing. Two repetition measures follow: exact identity on the full grid where syllable counts match, and "skeleton identity" (same onsets on strong and medium slots only) where they differ. The engine's habit of refitting most lines is not itself the fault, since ten speakers gave 3.41 settings per line. The fault is refits that move strong-slot onsets.

## Lyric-to-melody systems give reward shapes; register studies give a direction

**Measured (published settings, all 4/4).** Three machine systems expose rules a rule engine can copy. ReLyMe adds reward terms to its base score, s′ = log P + λ_t·R_t + λ_r·R_r + λ_s·R_s (tone, rhythm, structure), with published weights λ_r = 1.5 and λ_s = 1 in one host system and 1 and 1 in another. The rhythm reward is 1 when the lyric's strong and weak positions match the melody's and when pauses fall at word or sentence breaks; the structure reward is 2 when a note continues the same contour as the matching spot in a parallel phrase ([ReLyMe](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.05688)). TeleMelody maps a full stop to a full cadence and a comma to a half cadence, and reports measured note lengths in sixteenths: 1.74 inside a line, 8.54 at a half cadence, 10.34 at a full cadence. Its hand-rule fallback scored worse than its learned rhythm model (35.60 against 43.86 on pitch-distribution similarity) ([TeleMelody](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09617)). LYRA calls a note "stressed" if it is longer than the average note; real songs obey that on only **73.3%** of lines, and a hard constraint overshoots to 89.5% ([Tian et al., arXiv 2305.19228](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.19228v2)). None of these systems treats 3/4 or 6/8; TeleMelody's beat token runs 0 to 3 within a 4/4 bar.

**Measured.** Corpus counts rank the cues. In 679 pop lead sheets (4/4 bars only), 55% of primary-stress syllables sit on longer-than-local-average notes and stressed syllables are likelier to be melodic peaks ([Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)). In composed song, metrical position separates stressed from unstressed syllables while duration and contour do not ([Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)). In pop, a heavy stressed syllable gets a quarter-note slot 42% of the time against 24% for a light one ([Ryan 2022](https://www.meluhha.com/articles/ryan_2022_textsetting_preprint.pdf)). Function words are not uniformly weak: in rock, "in" lands on a quarter beat 56% of the time and "it" 12% ([Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)). Our inference: weight beat-strength mismatch well above duration mismatch, aim for 70% to 75% on the long-note rule and not 100%, and hold "what sits on a strong beat should be stressed" more tightly than "every stress must reach a strong beat".

**Measured.** On register, no source gives the chorus lift in semitones. Audio analysis of 649 Billboard songs shows a higher pitch centre raises the odds a section is a chorus (coefficient 0.10, 95% interval 0.07 to 0.12) ([Van Balen et al. 2013](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000180.pdf)). In 371 Billboard melodies with section labels, choruses have fewer notes per second than verses (2.15 against 2.38), slightly larger steps (2.15 against 2.02 semitones) and the same pitch spread (2.96 against 2.97 semitones standard deviation) ([BiMMuDa, TISMIR](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/10.5334/tismir.168)). The lift is a shift of the whole band, not a widening. On pitch concentration, the published figures are for folk and art song: scale-degree entropy H = −Σ pᵢ log₂ pᵢ typically falls between 1.7 and 2.8 bits, and about 71% of rhythm sequences against 51% of pitch sequences are repeated material ([melodic diversity study, arXiv 2408.12635](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.12635v2)). Our inference from that band: a melody whose commonest pitch takes half its notes, with four others sharing the rest, has H = 2.0 bits and sits inside it; one at 70% falls to about 1.36 bits, outside it. Neither figure is split by metre anywhere.

## Conclusion

The request to "solve for the missing cell as in algebra" turns out to have a precise answer, and the answer is that plain algebra on rates is the wrong tool for this particular table. One of its known cells is zero, so the style effect cannot be a multiplier. It has to be an operation: a shift applied after placement. That changes what the engine should store. In place of a table of where stresses fall in each metre and style, it needs one level-indexed placement rule per metre and one shift rule per style, with the shift's probability, its size in divisions or milliseconds, and its target levels as the only free numbers. Three of those numbers can be bounded from data the team already holds, because a shifted syllable leaves a trace in the notes alone.

The other change is in how the team should treat its own counts. The published literature has never measured stress placement, repetition rate or verse refitting in pop outside 4/4, and has no fitted word-setting weights for English pop in any metre. The first dozen rows of the table above would each be a new result. The cheapest of them, a search of 214 openly licensed transcriptions for their metre records, could show within an hour whether the missing cell needs to be inferred at all.
