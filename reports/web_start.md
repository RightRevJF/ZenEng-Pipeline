# Start here {#start}

This is the final edition of the report, and nothing more will be added before v8. This page holds everything the songwriting session learned and wrote while Luthier was offline (14 to 15 November 2026), and since: songs 54 and 55, run on Luthier v7 on 16 November, with their findings (749.24 to 749.31) and test reports (Parts 15 and 16), and an audit of what the engine itself writes, with twelve research studies for the harmony, band, tension, metre, melody, word-setting, harmony-voice, song-edge, ornament, key-choice, tempo and solo writers v8 needs (Parts 17 to 29). Here: the fix list for v8, the new rules, and every report and study. It is written for Luthier's maintainer, the AI that keeps the spec. Words and music: Jason Bryant.

**For the maintainer:** read Parts 1, 2 and 17 in full. They say what to change. Parts 18 to 29 are the research for the new writers; Parts 3 to 16 are the evidence; go to them when a fix or rule needs checking. ## What we learned, in brief

1. **Time signatures.** Pitch habits carry across metres; rhythm habits do not, so the engine needs rhythm tables for each metre. Kept tables now exist for every common metre, and English stress falls on strong beats in each (findings 749.4 to 749.7, 749.15).
2. **Hymns and organ.** Hymn tunes are plainer than folk tunes and need their own mode. Organ accompaniment should be written by the role of each section (749.11 to 749.14, 749.16).
3. **Song shape.** Songs share one shape, a build to a late high point. There are no shape types, and shape says almost nothing about success. Use it as a craft check (749.17).
4. **What audiences reward.** Across 10,847 songs, more predictable melody, chords and rhythm go with more YouTube views, even within one artist's songs (749.18, 749.20).
5. **The moments people love.** Listeners mark moments where the melody thins for a beat or two and then re-enters on a fresh note, a little past the middle of the song. Luthier already has this rule; this is listener evidence for it (749.19).
6. **How it sounds.** Checks for roughness, low crowded chords and the sung tune staying on top. They restate Luthier Module 9.14 and port it to organ and choir (749.21).
7. **What musicians reward.** Songs that musicians re-record most have harmony that moves within sections and melodies with room in them. This is a separate channel from views, found in two sources (749.22).
8. **Chromatic harmony.** Our songs use chromatic chords as single colours, mostly ♭VII. Audiences watch songs with more chromatic harmony less, and musicians do not re-record them more. So it should be a style the engine can choose, not a new default. Hymns and art songs show what each style expects: in hymns, one or two applied dominants a verse are ordinary; art songs live in chromatic harmony at about three times the popular share (749.23).
9. **Back on the engine (song 54).** A country story song on Luthier v7 found the engine assumes a bridge, still loses two-thirds of its builds on long lines with borrowed rhythms, and lets most survivors sing two syllables on one note; the judge picked one, so the pick of record is the judge's own rule without that fault. Two of the new rules need reading by texture and by song type (749.24 to 749.29).
10. **Song 55, a second country song.** The same two-syllables fault decided the pick again (42% of clean survivors), so it is general. Most builds died at the chorus's one-bar lines drawn fresh, a new place for placement deaths, and short lines in two-bar slots can open long rests between their words (749.30, 749.31).

11. **Who writes what (the audit).** In every song since v7 the engine wrote the sung melody and its rhythm, and the bass routine wrote the bass; an AI wrote each song's chord plan and every other band part by hand, and songs 52 and 53 entirely. The goal is an engine that writes everything and uses AI only lightly, so v8 needs two new writers, harmony and arrangement, drawn from measured tables and tested, and a test that fails any song with a hand-typed chord or band note (Part 17).
12. **Harmony (Part 18).** On 739 Billboard songs each section kind has a sharp job (verses open on I three times in four, bridges end on V nearly half the time). A simple model of what follows what, trained on 592 songs, already passes as real on major-key verses and choruses; minor keys, bridges and pre-choruses need more data, and the writer needs a loop step, since real sections repeat a two- or four-bar cell.
13. **The band (Part 19).** Real intros carry about 70% of the fullest band; the chorus lifts by playing more notes (busier than the verse in 88% of 909 songs), not by moving higher; the bass plays about once a beat at any tempo; every genre's commonest snare bar is 2 and 4, and genre shows in kick patterns, bass syncopation and piano voicing. The best published systems draw real phrases and fit them to new chords, which is how v8's band writer should work.

14. **Tension (Part 20).** Harmonic tension and energy are two curves. In 214 Billboard hits the chords are most tense in the pre-chorus and bridge, with V just before each chorus and I on its first bar, while listeners' energy peaks in the chorus; both build late (about 0.68 and 0.74 of the song). v7's 1-to-3 chord value predicts nothing; models that fit listeners read trends and give harmony a memory of 20 to 30 seconds. v8 should plan both curves before writing and gate its writers by them.

15. **Metre above the bar (Part 21).** All four placement failures of songs 54 to 56 come from fixing a line's length in bars before the words are known. In 9,313 lines of 198 Billboard songs, lines are timed in seconds: slow songs give a line fewer bars (6 to 8 syllables in one bar 36% of the time, against 6% in fast songs). v8 should set slots in seconds, choose the felt tempo at intake, let phrases expand, overlap or shrink at kept rates, and place words by weighted rules (Hayes) instead of failing. This closes 749.25, 749.30 and 749.31.

16. **Melody by genre (Part 22).** v7 writes every melody from the 7-note scale and forbids the flat third against a major chord (fix 200) and the flat fifth (tri()). In 165 major-key Billboard songs the typical melody puts 85% of its notes on one five-note scale, notes fix 200 forbids appear in 63% of songs (69% of soul songs), and tri() mostly blocks plain diatonic notes. Songs 54 and 55 have no flat 3 or flat 7, and song 54 sings the leading tone at three and a half times the country rate. v8 should take its pitch table from the style, place flat 3, flat 7 and flat 5 where soul and blues put them, narrow tri(), and treat the leading tone as a moving note.

17. **Setting words (Part 23).** v7 measures stress on the beat, line endings and vowels on held notes, but its songs put weak syllables on the downbeat and it has no rule for a word's pitch shape or a man's vowels. In 199 Billboard and 80 Rolling Stone 200 songs, weak syllables take beat 1 only 4% to 6% of the time and a word's stressed syllable is lower than the rest of the word in 18% to 20% of words; songs 54 and 55 do both about twice as often, mostly through line openings like "And" on the bar line. Height alone barely changes the vowel; held high notes do (men's held top notes 61% open, 7% reduced). v8 should grade its stress cost, give weak openings a pickup, add a word-shape rule for pitch and a vowel rule for held high notes by voice, all as weights in Part 21's placement. A second round of reading adds two measured rules: stress and metre move together between neighbouring syllables, and words ending in a voiced consonant take longer notes. Across the century (1900 to 1999), weak syllables on beat 1 stay near 5% in every period while syncopation more than doubles: the downbeat band is fixed, the contour targets depend on era and style. After 2000 melodies carry more notes a bar and more sixteenths, with fewer anticipations; songs 54 and 55 already have that rhythm, and what they lack is the word placement inside it. In hits from 2000 to 2022 the word rules hold as before. Hymns, folk songs and standards with words attached (PDMX and OpenEWLD, 4,535 English songs) give hymn mode its own bands: weak syllables on beat 1 up to about 10% in 4/4 and 17% in 3/4, and a looser word pitch shape.

18. **Harmony voices (Part 24).** v7 has a duet harmony rule (a third or sixth below, chord tones, no crossing, no parallel fifths or octaves) and labels for other ensembles, but only the duet rule writes notes, and every second voice in songs 53 to 56 was written by hand; three of the four broke the rule (a harmony above the lead, a two-note answer, a descant and choir). Singing traditions define the harmony as the next chord tone, above the lead as often as below. In 10,529 scores with two or more vocal parts (PDMX), v7's allowed intervals cover about a fifth of real harmony; the second voice is above the lead 28% of the time, in unison or octaves about a quarter of the time, and holds still in about half its moves; parallel fifths are rare (about 1 per 100 moves, 3 in pop); second voices sing more as the song goes on. v8 should write six kinds of second voice (double, harmony line, pad, answer, descant, parts), a harmony line by chord tone on the side the style calls for, and entries that grow with the song.

19. **Song edges (Part 25).** v7 checks intros and endings (a 4-bar median intro, a chorus-arrival limit, four ending kinds) but writes neither, and every intro in songs 52 to 56 was 4 bars. In 739 Billboard songs intros doubled from about 9 seconds (4 bars) in 1958 to 1964 to 17 (8 bars) in 1985 to 1991, and later studies find the voice arriving within about 5 seconds by 2015; length does not separate hits from misses, so it is an era setting. A quarter of streaming plays end in the first five seconds, so something identifiable should sound there. 61% of the Billboard songs fade; cold endings land on the tonic 81% of the time, by V–I, IV–I (Temperley's grand plagal cadence) or ♭VII–I. A fade keeps the listener's pulse running past the end; a final slowing adds no pleasure. v8 should write six intro kinds with era bands, ending kinds that name the cadence and add the fade, bookend and coda, and render fades and held last chords.

20. **Vocal ornament (Part 26).** v7 counts melisma (two-note turns at corpus rates, none on a cadence note) and renders a delayed MIDI vibrato, and nothing else of delivery; no score or songbook for songs 52 to 56 named a scoop, fall, cry, run or vibrato style. In 6,716 songs, chart pop sets 8% to 11% of syllables on more than one note, arrangements half that, hymns and folk about 5% to 8%, standards almost none; melismas gather at phrase ends at 2.5 to 3 times their share, are two notes three times in four, and grow through the song, which runs against v7's cadence-note ban. Scoops are the commonest pop inflection, the country breaking voice falls on grief words, and gospel runs are pentatonic, 4 to 7 notes, at the ends of long notes. v8 should write melisma by style bands and placement, treat runs as designed moments, and name and render the performed inflections and vibrato by style.

21. **Key choice (Part 27).** v7 picks a key from the hook and the voice only (27.14); songs 52 to 56 got instrument-friendly keys because they were chosen by hand. In 739 Billboard songs, guitar-led songs sit in open-string keys 65% of the time and in E, A or B major twice as often as piano-led songs; piano, strings and synthesizer songs use flat keys about twice as often as guitar songs. Minor rose from 11% of hits before 1970 to about half after 1990, and open-string keys fell from about half to under a third; country and rock stay in open major keys, R&B, soul and hip-hop in flat keys and minor. A hit's sung range is about an octave with its floor on the tonic or the dominant below, so the key follows from the voice's floor and the frame; key colour is a myth on equal-tempered instruments. v8 should choose the frame from the voice, filter by the lead instrument (with a capo when needed), set the mode default by genre and era, then rank by the hook.

22. **Tempo, feel and length (Part 28).** v7 has one tempo figure (104 bpm, all eras), channel design specs, a fixed 2:1 shuffle and no length band. Top-five hits ran 116 bpm in the 1960s, 91 in the 1990s (a third under 80) and 108 since 2010; R&B is the slow genre, minor-key hits are not slower, and a fifth of hits sit in the 80 to 95 zone v7 says to avoid. 12/8 was a third of 1950s hits and has vanished; tempo has been steady since click tracks. Swing ratio depends on tempo (about 3.5:1 slow, 2:1 medium, near straight fast). Hit length went from 2:31 (early 1960s) to 4:12 (late 1980s) and back to about 3:15. v8 should read tempo, feel and length bands from the chosen era and style and render swing by tempo.

23. **Solos and breaks (Part 29).** v7 writes the instrument around the voice but has no solo. In 739 Billboard songs the share with a solo grew from 22% (1958 to 1964) to 46% (1985 to 1991), and declined after with hip-hop's rise; a solo is about 8 bars and 20 seconds, starts just past the middle (0.45 to 0.65), usually follows the second chorus and leads into the last verse or chorus, and plays its own chords half the time. Short links of 4 to 8 bars are nearly universal. v8 should write two kinds of instrumental section, link and solo, with bands for length, place and material and a solo default by era and style.

The main new idea: **judge every song on two channels**, what audiences reward and what musicians reward, and prefer songs that do well on both.

## Data and cautions

YouTube data is kept only as results: no comment text, usernames or audio. The Session's tune data is not used, since its licence bans processing by AI. Every effect found is a few percent of the variance at most, so the new rules are targets and tie-breakers, not laws.
