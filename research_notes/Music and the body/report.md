# Music and the body: which effects depend on the notes?

Research pass for Luthier (ZenEng), 27 November 2026, at the writer's request ("it could provide missing pieces of our Rosetta Stone"). Method: WebSearch and WebFetch only. Primary papers and meta-analyses were read in full text where the host allowed it; several PubMed Central pages returned a browser check, so Europe PMC, publisher pages or author copies were used instead. Where only an abstract or a secondary account was seen, the entry says so. Numbers are as reported by the source, or worked out from its tables where marked "computed". Sources with links are in `sources.json` beside this file and listed at the end.

## In short

1. Your body does respond to the notes, but mostly through a few broad things: how fast the beat is, how clear and steady it is, and how much the rhythm plays against it.
2. A little off-beat play makes people want to move more, too much makes them want to move less, and the one big new study suggests that in less common time signatures people prefer the plainer rhythm.
3. Music that groups beats in threes made people walk with shorter, gentler steps than music in a strict two-feel, which fits the waltz's softer character but means 3/4 needs a firm beat if it is meant to drive.
4. Words are understood better when their stressed syllables land on strong beats and when each syllable gets its own note, and a melody helps memory only when it is simple and repeats.
5. Many health claims shrink or vanish under scrutiny: the Mozart effect, the heart "locking" to the tempo, and special "relaxing" tempos all fail, and personal taste often matters more than the notes.

## Findings by area

Each entry gives what was shown, how big the effect was and how it was measured, the study design, how firm it is, and what it depends on: N = the notes; L = the listener (preference, familiarity, training); or both.

### 1. Rhythmic auditory stimulation and beat-based cueing

**Stroke gait (Cochrane, Magee et al. 2017) [12].**
- What was shown: rhythmic auditory stimulation (RAS) raised walking speed by 11.34 m/min (95% CI 8.40 to 14.28; 9 trials, 268 people) and affected-side stride length by 0.12 m. Both are moderate certainty.
- Cadence rose 10.77 steps/min (low certainty). Gait symmetry showed no clear effect.
- Firmness: most trials had a high risk of bias.
- Depends on: N (a beat at a set rate). The review does not test which beat properties matter.

**Parkinson's gait, large meta-analysis (Ghai et al. 2018) [10].**
- Design: 50 studies, mostly non-randomised, mean PEDro score 5.4/10.
- Pooled Hedges' g: walking speed 0.23, stride length 0.42, cadence about 0. Heterogeneity was very high (I² 85 to 94%).
- Tempo subgroups:
  - Cueing up to 10% faster than the person's own cadence gave g = 0.70 for speed and 1.0 for cadence.
  - Cueing at baseline cadence gave about 0 for speed.
- No pooled music-versus-metronome comparison.
- Firmness: moderate for "cue slightly faster than the walker", low for the exact numbers.
- Depends on: N (tempo relative to the body).

**Parkinson's gait, RCTs only (Frontiers in Neurology 2022) [11].**
- Design: 18 RCTs, 774 people.
- Results: stride length +4.64 (the text says about 5 cm), speed +0.06 m/s, motor score (UPDRS-III) down 4.74 points, freezing questionnaire down 2.06.
- No subgroups by cue type or tempo.

**High- versus low-groove music in healthy walkers (Leow, Parrott & Grahn 2014) [8].**
- Design: 42 students. For each person, music was their own three highest- and three lowest-groove unfamiliar clips, plus a metronome. Each was played at preferred tempo and 22.5% faster.
- Low-groove music slowed walking (computed: stride velocity about 7% slower at preferred tempo) and shortened steps.
- At the faster tempo, high-groove music sped walking by about 13% and the metronome by about 9%.
- Weak beat-perceivers were slower overall (ηp² = 0.11).
- Metre of the clips was not reported.
- Firmness: moderate.
- Depends on: N (groove) and L (beat-perception skill).

**Groove and familiarity in Parkinson's (Ready, Holmes, Lonnee & Grahn 2025) [9].**
- Design: 21 people with Parkinson's, cues set 10% faster than baseline cadence.
- Groove had large effects: velocity ηp² = 0.37, cadence 0.38, step length 0.22.
- High-groove music worked like a metronome. Low-groove music shortened steps and lengthened double-support time.
- Familiarity had no significant effect on any gait measure.
- Firmness: moderate (small sample, one session, one tempo).
- Depends on: N, not familiarity. This is the cleanest clinical sign that a property of the music, rather than liking it, changes movement.

**Duple versus triple grouping in walking (Leman et al. 2013) [13].**
- Design: 18 adults walked in step to 52 excerpts, all at 130 bpm in 4/4.
- With tempo held fixed, "activating" excerpts lengthened strides (107% of metronome speed) and "relaxing" ones shortened them (89%).
- Four audio features explained about 60% of the variance.
- The strongest single feature, loudness repeating every three beats, alone explained about 44%. Features at three- and six-beat periods lowered walking speed.
- The authors read this as relaxing music softening the binary metre with ternary traces.
- Firmness: low to moderate (one tempo, genre confound).
- Depends on: N. This is the most direct body evidence on triple grouping: it calms walking rather than driving it.

**Metre is felt through the body (Phillips-Silver & Trainor 2005) [14].**
- Infants bounced on every second or every third beat of an unaccented rhythm later preferred the version accented to match their bouncing (t(15) = 4.00).
- Watching someone else bounce had no effect. Adults showed the same pattern.
- Meaning for the engine: duple versus triple is partly decided by how the body moves, so the notes must make the intended grouping clear.

No study was found that compares walking or cueing in 3/4 or 6/8 with duple metre directly.

### 2. Groove and the urge to move

**Witek et al. 2014 [1].**
- Design: 66 online listeners rated 50 synthesised funk drum breaks in 4/4 at 120 bpm, with a steady eighth-note hi-hat.
- Measure: a syncopation index adapted from Longuet-Higgins and Lee, with weights for each drum. Scores ran from 0 to 81.
- Result: an inverted U. Syncopation alone explained R² = .347 of wanting to move and .427 of pleasure. Medium syncopation was rated highest.
- The index value at the peak is only in Supplementary Table S1, which was not read, so no peak number is reported here.
- Dance experience raised ratings. Familiarity with groove music did not.
- Firmness: moderate.
- Depends on: N.

**Matthews et al. 2019 [2].**
- Design: 201 listeners, 54 loops in 4/4 at 96 bpm. Three rhythm levels (no syncopation; clave patterns; only the first onset on a strong beat) were crossed with three harmony levels (triads; extended chords; chords with a flat ninth).
- Rhythm gave a strong inverted U for both wanting to move and pleasure.
- Harmony had a weaker effect: mostly a drop at the flat-ninth level, working through pleasure.
- Musicians showed a sharper rhythmic inverted U.
- Measure: Fitch and Rosenfeld index, with values shown only in a figure.
- Firmness: moderate.
- Depends on: N, sharpened by L (training).

**Sioros et al. 2014 [4].**
- Design: simple melodies and children's songs on synthesised piano, 4/4 at 120 bpm, syncopated by shifting notes.
- All syncopating changes raised groove over the plain version (effects of 0.20 to 0.35).
- Experiment 2:
  - Sparse anticipation or delay of strong-beat notes, especially the phrase-final note, gave about 0.60.
  - Syncopating every note gave 0.44.
  - Simply doubling note density gave about 0.2.
- Where the syncopations go mattered more than how many there are.
- Firmness: moderate (28 and 22 listeners).
- Depends on: N. This is the most usable groove study for a melody engine.

**Janata, Tomic & Haberman 2012 [3].**
- Design: 148 excerpts.
- Fast excerpts (mean about 116 bpm) were rated groovier than slow ones (about 91 bpm).
- Groove correlated with enjoyment at r = .82 and with familiarity at r = .57. Familiarity dropped out once enjoyment was included.
- High-groove music made tapping easier and drew spontaneous foot movement (about 31% of trial time versus about 20%).
- Depends on: N and L.

**Senn et al. 2018 (against the hope) [5].**
- Design: 665 listeners, 248 drum patterns transcribed from real tracks.
- Syncopation explained R² = 0.010 and event density R² = 0.011.
- Beat salience, microtiming and rhythmic variability had no effect.
- Liking the pattern's apparent style explained R² = 0.123. Style liking plus familiarity explained 0.152.
- Firmness: moderate to high.
- Depends on: mainly L. In real music, taste dwarfs the inverted U.

**Groove in other metres (Spiech, Câmara, Fuhrer & Penhune 2025) [6].**
- Design: 383 listeners, 63 commercial clips at 110 to 130 bpm, 34 in 4/4 and 29 in other metres.
- Measure: complexity as pulse entropy (how predictable the beat is, from onset autocorrelation).
- In 4/4: the usual inverted U.
- Outside 4/4: the curve reversed. The simplest rhythms drew the most urge to move, and pleasure was unrelated to complexity. This replicated.
- Firmness: moderate to high for the pattern. Uncertain for 3/4 and 6/8: the clip list was not seen, the paper's examples are 7/8, 5/4 and 9/8, and it names 3/4 and 6/8 only as examples of other metre types.
- Depends on: N interacting with L (learned metric expectations).

**Groove versus catchiness (Bechtold, Curry & Witek 2025) [7].**
- 120 bpm gave slightly more urge to move than 90 bpm. Tempo did not affect catchiness.
- Groove and catchiness correlated at r = 0.49.

**Pulse clarity and movement (Burger et al. 2013) [16]; metrical levels in the body (Toiviainen & Carlson 2022) [15].**
- Pulse clarity correlated with amount of movement (r = 0.62).
- 11 of 12 movement patterns locked to a metrical level: bounce at the beat, limb and torso movement at two beats, sway at four beats.
- Preferred pulse was about 500 to 600 ms.
- Neither study identified triple-metre stimuli.

No groove study was found that measures 3/4 or 6/8 separately, and no syncopation peak for triple or compound metre exists in what was read.

### 3. Heart rate, breathing, blood pressure

**Bernardi, Porta & Sleight 2006 [17].**
- Design: 24 adults, six styles of music.
- Faster tempo raised breathing rate, blood pressure and heart rate across styles. Preference did not explain the responses.
- A two-minute pause brought these measures below baseline.
- Firmness: low to moderate.
- Depends on: N (tempo).

**Bernardi et al. 2009 [18].**
- Crescendos were followed by skin vasoconstriction (r about -0.5 to -0.7 with the loudness envelope) and rises in systolic pressure (r about +0.4 to +0.7), lagging by 6 to 11 seconds.
- Mean changes were small.
- Phrases near 10 seconds (0.1 Hz) brought blood pressure into step with the phrasing.
- The correlations were computed on group averages.
- Firmness: low to moderate.
- Depends on: N and performance (dynamics).

**Koelsch & Jäncke 2015 review [19].**
- Heart rate changes about 1 to 2 bpm with music.
- When arousal was matched, 90 versus 120 bpm made no heart-rate difference.
- No evidence that heart rate entrains to the beat.
- Clinical trials carry high risk of bias.

**Bretherton, Deuchars & Windsor 2019 [21].**
- Design: 52 adults, tempo stepped from 60 to 180 bpm.
- Faster tempo did not raise sympathetic nerve activity.
- Slow ends of the ramps raised some parasympathetic markers (ηp² up to 0.18).
- Melody versus rhythm alone made no difference.

**Juslin et al. (online 2024) [20].**
- Design: 36 listeners heard music with tempo set 3% or 30% above or below their own heart rate.
- Heart rate did not move toward the tempo. Heart rate and arousal rose by a similar small amount in every condition.
- Firmness: moderate. A direct test against entrainment.

No 3/4 versus 4/4 cardiac study was found.

### 4. Infant-directed song and lullabies

**Mehr et al. 2018 [23].**
- Design: 750 listeners in 60 countries heard 118 songs from 86 societies.
- Listeners identified lullabies well (cross-cohort R² = 0.974).
- Listeners rated lullabies as:
  - slower (z -1.04)
  - less melodically complex (-1.12)
  - less rhythmically complex (-1.06)
  - less steady in beat (-0.63)
- Musical features added variance beyond context cues (R² from 0.586 to 0.683).
- Depends on: N, though the features are listener ratings, not counts.

**Mehr et al. 2019 [24].**
- Transcribed features, dance versus lullaby: lullabies had fewer accents (-0.64), slower tempo (-0.65) and less consistent bar-level metre (-0.44).
- Lullabies had a smaller pitch range than love songs.
- Triple metre signs were small and mixed: healing versus lullaby gave -0.23 for triple subdivision and +0.14 for triple bar grouping. The table layout was hard to read, so treat these with caution.
- Song function was identified at 42.4% against 25% chance.

**Hilton et al. 2022 [25].**
- Design: 1,615 recordings from 21 societies.
- Infant-directed song was softer and less rough than adult-directed song, with similar pitch.
- Infant-directedness was harder to classify for song than for speech (AUC 82% versus 91%).
- Listeners took clear pulse as a sign of infant-directed song, but pulse clarity did not reliably mark it across cultures.
- No tempo effect for song was reported.

**Bainbridge et al. 2021 [26], abstract and captions only.**
- 144 US infants heard unfamiliar foreign lullabies. Their heart rate, pupil size and electrodermal arousal fell compared with matched non-lullabies.
- Effect sizes were not readable.

**Hannon et al. 2016 [27].**
- In 269 English and French children's and folk songs, 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 dominated. 6/8 was uncommon (6 English, 20 French).
- Children's songs had less variable rhythm (nPVI 37 versus 42).

**Unyk et al. 1992 [28], abstract only.**
- Lullabies were judged simpler even with the words obscured.

**On "lullabies favour triple or compound metre":** no data read supports it. What does hold across studies: slower, fewer accents, simpler rhythm and melody, narrower range, softer. Falling contour was not confirmed in anything read.

### 5. Pain, anxiety, sleep

**Kühlmann et al. 2018 [29].**
- Design: 92 RCTs in surgery.
- Anxiety fell by about 21 mm and pain by about 10 mm on 100 mm scales.
- Heterogeneity was high, risk of bias moderate to high, and placebo could not be excluded.
- No music type stood out. Patient-owned music did worst.

**Panteleeva et al. 2018 [30].**
- Design: 21 studies, 1,144 non-clinical participants.
- Self-reported anxiety fell (d = -0.30). Physiological anxiety did not (d = -0.07, n.s.).
- Slow music gave d = -0.28, but there was no direct comparison with fast music.

**Husain, Thompson & Schellenberg 2002 [22].**
- Design: the same Mozart sonata at 165 or 60 bpm, in major or minor.
- Tempo moved arousal (F = 91.8) but not mood. Mode moved mood (F = 17.2) but not arousal.
- This is the cleanest isolation of tempo found.
- Depends on: N.

**Knox et al. 2011 [34].**
- Music people chose that relieved their cold pain was brighter, more major, softer and slower than a reference set.
- No rhythm feature correlated with relief. Less harmonic change correlated weakly (r about 0.3).

**Mitchell & MacDonald 2006 [33], abstract.**
- Preferred music beat designed relaxation music for pain tolerance and felt control.

**Cochrane insomnia review 2022 [31], abstract.**
- Self-rated sleep quality improved (PSQI -2.79, moderate certainty). Objective sleep did not clearly improve.

**Scarratt et al. 2023 [32], abstract.**
- Among 225,626 sleep-playlist tracks, most were softer and slower than average, but three of six subgroups were faster and more energetic.

**The 60 to 80 bpm claim.** No trial isolates this band. Tempo stopped mattering for heart rate once arousal was matched [19], and heart rate does not lock to tempo [20]. What survives is weaker: slower and softer music lowers arousal a little on average.

### 6. Negative and skeptical evidence

- **Mozart effect** [35] (abstract and secondary accounts only): about 39 to 40 studies, more than 3,000 people, no Mozart-specific gain. Effects from the original labs were more than three times larger than others', and publication bias was strong. What is left is arousal and mood [22].
- **Music training and intelligence** [36]: across 54 studies and 6,984 children, g = 0.056 with active controls and 0.064 when randomised.
- **Heart rate entrainment to tempo:** not supported [19, 20].
- **Groove from notes versus taste:** taste explains about 12 times as much as syncopation [5].
- **Physiological anxiety:** null [30].
- **Pain:** preference beats "relaxing" design [33], and rhythm features do not predict relief [34].
- **Song as a memory aid:** the advantage over speech disappears when presentation rate is matched (Kilgour et al. 2000), and speech beat song with complex folk melodies (Racette & Peretz 2007). Both are secondary, via [40].

### 7. Lyrics: stress, beat and setting

**Johnson, Huron & Collister 2014 [37].**
- Stress matched to the music: about 85% of words understood, against about 75% when mismatched (phi = .11).
- Syllabic settings beat melismatic ones (84% versus 77%).
- Immediate repetition helped. Repetition after one intervening word did not.
- Common words beat archaic ones (83% versus 48%), the largest effect in the study.
- Firmness: low to moderate (exploratory, alpha .10).

**Gordon, Magne & Large 2011 [38].**
- Stressed syllables on strong beats drew stronger beta-band brain activity and changed the follow-up word-recognition pattern.
- The authors caution this does not prove better comprehension.

**Palmer & Kelly 1992 [39].**
- In the songs studied, the first syllable of compound nouns got the stronger beat about 90% of the time (computed).
- Singers lengthened syllables for musical and linguistic accent independently.

**Ludke, Ferreira & Overy 2014 [40].**
- Singing beat speaking for recall of Hungarian phrases (d = 0.75 and 0.95), with rate matched.
- Rhythmic speech alone did not help.
- Their review: the same simple melody across verses helps verbatim recall, while a new melody per verse is worse than speech (Wallace 1994, secondary).

## For the engine

These are candidates to test rule first, not settled rules. Firmness: firm = replicated or meta-analytic; moderate = one or two decent studies; weak = small or indirect. "Inferred" means the step from the source to the rule is the research pass's own.

- **C1. Stressed syllables on strong beats in every metre** [37, 38, 39].
  - Firmness: moderate (about 10 points of intelligibility).
  - The literature backs alignment in general, not the beat-one share of 0.65 (25th percentile 0.583). The 3/4 and 6/8 application is inferred.
- **C2. Clear, regular beat one for movement songs in 3/4** [6, 13, 16, 24].
  - Moderate in direction, inferred for 3/4 and 6/8.
  - Target to test: share of bars with an onset on beat one at or above the kept-song 25th percentile, with movement songs above the median.
- **C3. Lullabies and tender songs may soften beat one** [23, 24, 25].
  - Use fewer accents, slower tempo, simpler rhythm and a narrower range, without breaking C1.
  - Moderate; the relaxation of C2 is inferred.
- **C4. Syncopation in 3/4 and 6/8 at or below the 4/4 level, and placed** [1, 2, 4, 6].
  - Cap the share of off-beat onsets near the kept 3/4 pop figure (1 - 0.68 ≈ 0.32).
  - Prefer one or two anticipations per phrase that land on beat one or the phrase-final strong beat over constant off-beat stress.
  - Firmness: moderate in 4/4, weak and inferred for 3/4 and 6/8.
- **C5. 3/4 will not drive like 4/4** [13, 24].
  - For energetic 3/4, compensate with a firm beat and low syncopation.
  - 6/8 may move bodies like duple at the dotted-quarter level while keeping a triple subdivision. Weak and inferred; untested.
- **C6. Tempo sets arousal, not heart rate** [17, 19, 20, 22].
  - For movement songs, the felt step or sway beat should sit at about 100 to 130 per minute [3, 6, 7, 13, 15].
  - In 3/4 and 6/8 the engine should record which metrical level it intends as the felt beat. Inferred.
  - Never tune tempo to the heartbeat.
- **C7. For movement songs, groove matters more than familiarity** [8, 9].
  - Measurable stand-ins: pulse clarity, moderate placed syncopation, medium harmony [2, 6, 16]. Inferred.
- **C8. Medium harmonic complexity; avoid harsh dissonance under a groove** [2].
  - Moderate, 4/4 only.
- **C9. Memorable lyrics** [37, 40].
  - One simple melody repeated across verses; one note per syllable on hook words; common words; bar-rhythm repetition at or above the kept 0.55.
  - Moderate.
- **C10. Phrase and dynamic arcs** [18].
  - Phrases of about 8 to 12 seconds with clear build and release.
  - Weak and inferred.
- **Pickups:** no body or health evidence found. Take them from the kept-song data plus C1 (a pickup carries unstressed syllables into a stressed beat one).

## For the judging research

Note-level measures to test against which songs last and get covered:

1. Stress-to-strong-beat alignment rate, including the beat-one stress share in 3/4 [37, 38, 39].
2. Syncopation index (Longuet-Higgins and Lee, or Fitch and Rosenfeld) per song, tested as an inverted U within each metre [1, 2, 6].
3. Syncopation placement: the share of syncopations that anticipate strong-beat or phrase-final notes [4].
4. Pulse clarity or pulse entropy from symbolic onsets, using autocorrelation [6, 16].
5. Bar-level metric consistency: how regularly beat one carries an onset or accent [24].
6. Strength of three- and six-beat periodicity [13].
7. Accent density and tempo [23, 24].
8. Melody reuse across verses, and bar-rhythm repetition [40].
9. Melisma rate on hook words [37].
10. Word frequency of the lyric [37].
11. Harmonic complexity level [2].
12. Event density [5].

Caution: in real tracks, taste outweighed note features about tenfold [5]. A note-only judge should test these within a genre and expect small effects.

## What does not help

- The Mozart effect [35, 22].
- Music lessons for IQ [36].
- Heart rate locking to tempo [19, 20].
- A magic 60 to 80 bpm relaxing band [19, 22, 30].
- Physiological anxiety relief in healthy people [30].
- Designed "relaxation music" over personal favourites for pain [33, 34].
- Song as a general memory booster: it helps only with simple, repeated melodies, and the effect disappears when rate is matched [40].
- "Lullabies are in triple or compound time" [24, 27].
- Groove as a pure note property: real but small next to taste, and maybe not present outside 4/4 [5, 6].
- Objective sleep gains from music [31].
