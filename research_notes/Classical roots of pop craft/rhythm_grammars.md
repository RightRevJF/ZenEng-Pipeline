## 0. What models exist, and what is their exact form? (catalogue)

### Takeaway
There are four families: (a) rule systems with no probabilities (GTTM, Longuet-Higgins & Lee, Hayes, Toussaint), (b) grid/position models that give each metrical position an onset probability (Palmer & Krumhansl profiles, Temperley 2007/2010), (c) sequence models over metrically-indexed symbols (Temperley's first-order metrical duration model, IDyOM/PPM with metrical-position viewpoints, Nakamura's metrical Markov and bar-pattern Markov models), and (d) tree grammars over time-spans (Rohrmeier 2020, Harasim et al. 2019, Foscarin/Jacquemard). Only families (b) and (c) come with held-out cross-entropy numbers; the tree grammars have so far been evaluated on parsing accuracy or transcription, not on rhythm likelihood.

### Cited Findings

**GTTM (Lerdahl & Jackendoff 1983) - metrical well-formedness and preference rules (verbatim as quoted on the source page)**
- MWFR 1: "Every attack point must be associated with a beat at the smallest metrical level present at that point in the piece." MWFR 2: "Every beat at a given level must also be a beat at all smaller levels present at that point in that piece." MWFR 3: "At each metrical level, strong beats are spaced either two or three beats apart." MWFR 4: "The tactus and immediately larger metrical levels must consist of beats equally spaced throughout the piece. At subtactus metrical levels, weak beats must be equally spaced between the surrounding strong beats." — [Wikipedia, Generative theory of tonal music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_theory_of_tonal_music)
- MPR 1 (Parallelism): "Where two or more groups or parts of groups can be construed as parallel, they preferably receive parallel metrical structure." MPR 2 (Strong beat early): "Weakly prefer a metrical structure in which the strongest beat in a group appears relatively early in the group." MPR 3 (Event): "Prefer a metrical structure in which beats of level Li that coincide with the inception of pitch-events are strong beats of Li." MPR 4 (Stress): stressed beats are strong. MPR 5 (Length): prefer a relatively strong beat at the inception of a relatively long pitch-event, dynamic, slur, articulation pattern, pitch duration in time-span reduction, or harmony. MPR 6 (Bass): "Prefer a metrically stable bass." MPR 7 (Cadence): "Strongly prefer a metrical structure in which cadences are metrically stable." MPR 8 (Suspension): suspension on a stronger beat than its resolution. MPR 9: minimise conflict in time-span reduction. MPR 10 (Binary regularity): "Prefer metrical structures in which at each level every other beat is strong." — [Wikipedia, GTTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_theory_of_tonal_music)
- Grouping preference rules also include Symmetry ("Prefer grouping analyses that most closely approach the ideal subdivision of groups into two parts of equal length") and Parallelism ("Where two or more segments of music can be construed as parallel, they preferably form parallel parts of groups"). — [Wikipedia, GTTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_theory_of_tonal_music)
- GTTM's rules are analytic (they choose a metre for a given surface); they are unweighted and give no probability to a rhythm. Read generatively, MPR 3 and MPR 5 are exactly the two regularities that Temperley's position models and duration model later quantify ("notes more likely on strong beats"; "long notes on strong beats"). — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)

**Longuet-Higgins & Lee (1984) syncopation measure (as stated by Fitch & Rosenfeld 2007)**
- Metrical weights in 4/4: bar start = 0; beat 3 = -1; beats 2 and 4 = -2; eighth-note off-beats = -3; sixteenth positions = -4; each finer level subtracts one more. — [Fitch & Rosenfeld 2007](https://web.uvic.ca/~aschloss/course_mat/MUS%20511/ARTICLES%20AND%20REFS%20FOR%20320/FitchRosenfeld20071.pdf)
- Rule: "a syncopation occurs when a rest (or tied note) is preceded by a sounded note of lesser weight." For each such pair with rest/tie weight R and preceding sounded-note weight N, N < R, strength S = R - N; the syncopation index of the rhythm is the sum of all S. For looped patterns the bar wraps round. — [Fitch & Rosenfeld 2007](https://web.uvic.ca/~aschloss/course_mat/MUS%20511/ARTICLES%20AND%20REFS%20FOR%20320/FitchRosenfeld20071.pdf)
- Worked example: one note on beat 2 of a looped 4/4 bar scores 3 (rest on beat 3: -1 - (-2) = 1; wrapped rest on beat 1: 0 - (-2) = 2). — [Fitch & Rosenfeld 2007](https://web.uvic.ca/~aschloss/course_mat/MUS%20511/ARTICLES%20AND%20REFS%20FOR%20320/FitchRosenfeld20071.pdf)
- Behavioural validity: for 30 rhythms with index 0-15 and 16 listeners, pulse-tracking error correlated with the index at r(29) = .82; tendency to "reset" the beat r = .78; reproduction error r = .51. — [Fitch & Rosenfeld 2007](https://web.uvic.ca/~aschloss/course_mat/MUS%20511/ARTICLES%20AND%20REFS%20FOR%20320/FitchRosenfeld20071.pdf)

**Palmer & Krumhansl (1990) metrical profiles (as summarised by van der Weij et al. 2017)**
- In a Western classical corpus the distribution of note onsets over metrical positions reflected the hierarchical metrical theory of Lerdahl & Jackendoff; listeners' goodness-of-fit ratings for probe positions showed the same hierarchy, more differentiated in musicians. — [van der Weij, Pearce & Honing 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)

**Temperley 2007 (Music and Probability) rhythm model**
- A generative metrical grid with three levels (upper, tactus, lower); each level divides the one above in 2 or 3; onset probability at a grid point depends only on that point's metrical level. Onset probabilities by salience 0-3 (below-lower, lower, tactus, upper): .01, .38, .74, .95. Parameters estimated from an Essen subset, some set by intuition. — [van der Weij PhD thesis, UvA](https://eprints.illc.uva.nl/id/document/12123)
- Tested on Temperley's own performances of 65 Essen melodies; in an error-detection test (10 distorted versions of each melody) the undistorted version got the higher probability in 82% of cases. — [Pearce, Müllensiefen, Lewis & Rhodes review, EMR 2007](https://core.ac.uk/download/159560081.pdf)

**Temperley 2010 "Modeling common-practice rhythm" - six models (all over a fixed grid, only onsets modelled)**
- Grid levels: L1 eighth, L2 quarter, L3 half, L4 whole (bar) for Essen; a sixteenth level is added for the quartets. — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- (1) Uniform Position: at every eighth-note beat, note with P = .51. 1 parameter. — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- (2) Zeroth-Order Duration: each inter-onset interval drawn independently from one distribution. 15 parameters. — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- (3) Metrical Position: Bernoulli per beat, P(note) depends on the beat's level (e.g. L1 = .21, L4 = .988 on Essen). 4 parameters (Essen), 5 (quartets). — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- (4) Fine-Grained Position: as (3) but one probability per position in the bar. 8 parameters (Essen), 16 (quartets). — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- (5) Hierarchical Position: generate top-down. L4 (downbeat) notes first (P = .998 Essen, .891 quartets). Then each lower-level beat gets a note with a probability that depends on its level and on whether the neighbouring stronger beats on either side carry notes: four contexts - both-anchored, post-anchored (note on following stronger beat only), pre-anchored (note on preceding stronger beat only), unanchored. Essen L2 values: both .713, post .889, pre .265, unanchored .062. 13 parameters (Essen), 17 (quartets). — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- (6) First-Order Metrical Duration: each note's bar position is drawn conditional on the previous note's bar position, P(C|A) = count(A then C)/count(A); an 8x8 table for Essen (e.g. P(position 0 -> position 3) = .163). 56 parameters (Essen), 240 (quartets). — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)

**IDyOM with metrical viewpoints (van der Weij, Pearce & Honing 2017)**
- IDyOM is a variable-order Markov model (PPM* with escape method C and interpolated smoothing); a long-term model (LTM) is trained on a corpus, a short-term model (STM) is learned incrementally within the current piece and emptied before each new piece; predictions from different viewpoints are combined with entropy-based weights; pitch and onset predictions multiply to give note probability. Timing viewpoints include inter-onset interval (ioi) and ioi-ratio. — [Pearce 2018, Ann. NY Acad. Sci.](https://www.marcus-pearce.com/assets/papers/Pearce2018.pdf)
- Metre-aware version: metrical position mp = (onset - phase) mod T_m (T_m = bar period in sixteenths); bar number bn = int((onset - phase)/T_m); bar distance bd = bn(current) - bn(previous); the linked viewpoint mp x bd is one-to-one with onset time. Prediction marginalises over metre and phase: p(next | past) = sum over m, phase of p(next | m, phase, past) p(m, phase | past). Prior p(m) = N_m/N; p(m, phase) = p(m) / sum_m' T_m' p(m'). — [van der Weij et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)
- 320 metre-phase interpretations in total; 48 when limited to 4/4, 2/4, 3/4 and 6/8. Onsets stored in 96th-note units so triplets and 32nds fit. — [van der Weij et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)

**Nakamura & Yoshii score models (rhythm transcription)**
- Three generic "score models": note-value Markov model (NoteMM; Markov chain on note lengths), metrical Markov model (MetMM; Markov chain on bar position of each onset - the same idea as Temperley's model 6), and note-pattern Markov model (PatMM; Markov chain on whole-bar binary onset patterns, i.e. a bar-level "rhythm word" n-gram, with bars here being half-note segments). — [Nakamura & Yoshii, arXiv:1908.06969](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.06969)
- Piece-specific Bayesian extension: each piece's transition tables are drawn from a Dirichlet process whose base is the generic model, pi_piece ~ DP(alpha, pi_generic); small alpha makes each piece reuse few patterns. Near-repeats are covered by two "modification" operations: note division (split one note in two) and onset shift, with "no modification" probability set to 0.9. — [Nakamura & Yoshii, arXiv:1908.06969](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.06969)

**Tree grammars of rhythm**
- Rohrmeier 2020 "Towards a Formalization of Musical Rhythm" (ISMIR): abstract context-free grammar whose categories are time-spans [a : b : c] = upbeat a, body b, coda c, on a metrical grid; constraints -b < a, c < b and a + c <= b; start symbols have c = 0. Rules: Split (body into 2 or 3 parts; "time stealing" encoded as negative offsets); Prepare / U-split (carve a new event of length d, 0 < d <= a, out of the upbeat); C-split (coda counterpart, possibly unnecessary for tonal rhythm); unary Shifts - anticipate (move a category earlier by its upbeat length) and delay (move later by coda length); surface rules turn a resolved category into a note of length l <= b plus rest. Metre is a recursive grid: each level's period is 2 or 3 times the level below, weight of a position = number of levels whose grid contains it. — [Rohrmeier 2020](https://program.ismir2020.net/static/final_papers/368.pdf)
- That paper has no probabilities, dataset or evaluation; two worked analyses of "Blue Bossa" only. — [Rohrmeier 2020](https://program.ismir2020.net/static/final_papers/368.pdf)
- Harasim, O'Donnell & Rohrmeier 2019 "Harmonic Syntax in Time" (ISMIR): rhythmic categories a (+) b (upbeat a, downbeat b), start category = whole piece length 1; rules usplit (separate/split the upbeat), dsplit (split the downbeat, optionally introducing a new upbeat), a unary rule; simplified variant split_s(a) -> s*a and a - s*a with split ratio 0 < s < 1. Rule probabilities depend only on whether the upbeat is zero. Combined with a harmony PCFG as a product grammar, p((r, r') | (A, A')) = p(r | A) p(r' | A'). Fitted on 75 hand-annotated trees of jazz chord sequences (iRealPro); most common split ratio 1/2. Evaluated by tree accuracy under leave-one-out, not by cross-entropy: adding rhythm significantly improved harmony parsing (p < 0.01), numbers only in a figure. — [Harasim et al. 2019](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/273863/files/ismir2019_paper_000039.pdf)

**Toussaint - Euclidean rhythms**
- E(k, n): k onsets among n pulses "distributed as evenly as possible", generated by Bjorklund's algorithm (repeatedly pair off the remainder sequences, as in Euclid's gcd algorithm). E(3,8) = [x . . x . . x .] (tresillo); E(5,8) = [x . x x . x x .] (cinquillo); E(2,3) = [x . x] (6/8 swing tumbao conga); E(4,12) = [x . . x . . x . . x . .] (12/8 fandango clap); E(7,12) = [x . x x . x . x x . x .] (West African bell); E(5,16) bossa-nova necklace; E(7,16) samba necklace. Rotations of one pattern form a "necklace"; cultures differ in start point. — [Toussaint, "The Euclidean algorithm generates traditional musical rhythms"](https://cgm.cs.mcgill.ca/~godfried/publications/banff.pdf)
- This is a deterministic generator of maximally even ostinati (timelines), not a probability model, and the paper's examples are overwhelmingly accompaniment/percussion timelines, not melodies. — [Toussaint](https://cgm.cs.mcgill.ca/~godfried/publications/banff.pdf)

**Hayes - text-setting on a metrical grid**
- Hayes's "Textsetting as constraint conflict" models how syllables are placed on a fixed grid by ranked, violable constraints (Optimality Theory): (a) match stress to strong positions, (b) avoid long lapses (stretches where no syllable begins), (c) avoid extreme syllable compression, (d) align phonological-phrase boundaries with line boundaries; "a good textsetting often must sacrifice perfect realization of one of these goals in order to satisfy another goal that takes higher priority." Data: 346 lines of English folk verse, each with the setting most often chosen by nine native speakers; a 640-line set (Hayes & Kaun 1996) adds syllable weight and phrasing. Data file is downloadable. — [Hayes, Textsetting page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)

### Inferences
- A songwriting engine needs a model from family (b)/(c) as its probability core, because only those assign a normalised probability to any onset pattern; the rule systems (GTTM, LHL, Hayes) are best used as features or as soft constraints/penalties on top.
- Temperley's model 5 is effectively a probabilistic version of a rhythm tree: "generate strong beats, then fill weaker beats conditional on their stronger neighbours" equals recursive Split with anchoring context. It is the cheapest bridge between the grid models and the Rohrmeier/Harasim grammars.
- LHL weights and Temperley's "unanchored / pre-anchored" contexts describe the same thing: a note on a weak position with nothing on the following stronger position. LHL counts it; Temperley gives it a low probability (.062 unanchored and .265 pre-anchored vs .889 post-anchored at quarter level in Essen).

## 1. In Temperley 2010 (and follow-ups), which model best predicted held-out rhythms, by how much, and what are its parameters? Does it handle compound and triple metre?

### Takeaway
The First-Order Metrical Duration model (each note's bar position conditional on the previous note's bar position) had the lowest held-out cross-entropy on both corpora, but only about 3.6% (Essen) and 4.3% (quartets) better than the Hierarchical Position model, which has 13-17 parameters instead of 56-240; Temperley judged the Hierarchical Position model the most plausible "on balance". The study tested 4/4 only; triple and compound metre were handled later by van der Weij et al.'s PPM-over-metrical-position model, which reached 1.34 bits per onset on German folk songs across all metres.

### Cited Findings
- Corpora. Essen: >6,000 melodies reduced to 1,585 in 4/4; 350 with notes shorter than an eighth removed, leaving 1,235 melodies / 13,786 bars; 988 train, 247 test (five-fold cross-validation changed figures by <1% and kept the ranking). Haydn-Mozart string quartets: 309 movements reduced to 63 in 4/4 (6,900 bars), first-violin part only, empty bars deleted, sixteenth level added; 32 train / 31 test movements. — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Table 1, held-out cross-entropy (reported per song for Essen and per movement for the quartets, not per note), with parameter counts:

| Model | Essen (per song) | params | Quartets (per movement) | params |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Uniform Position | 62.37 | 1 | 1150.14 | 1 |
| 2 Zeroth-Order Duration | 54.45 | 15 | 943.89 | 15 |
| 3 Metrical Position | 42.47 | 4 | 965.94 | 5 |
| 4 Fine-Grained Position | 40.21 | 8 | 959.42 | 16 |
| 5 Hierarchical Position | 38.76 | 13 | 819.37 | 17 |
| 6 First-Order Metrical Duration | 37.36 | 56 | 783.79 | 240 |

  — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Parameters of the winner (model 6): a transition table over bar positions, P(C|A) = count(A,C)/count(A); 8 positions on Essen, 16 on the quartets. Runner-up (model 5): downbeat note probability, plus for each lower level a note probability in each of four anchoring contexts (both / post / pre / unanchored). — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Conclusion: model 6 fits best but with many more parameters; model 5 is a close second and much simpler; Temperley calls model 5 "on balance" the most plausible while saying this is "really only an opinion". — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Perceptual check: correlation with Povel & Essens (1985) rhythm-complexity data was r = .76 for model 5 and r = .71 for model 6. — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Metre coverage: only 4/4 was tested. An extension to 3/4 is discussed only for models 5 and 6, with 19 and 132 parameters respectively. — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Follow-up that covers all metres: van der Weij, Pearce & Honing 2017, on 4,966 German Essen songs (4/4, 2/4, 3/4, 6/8 most frequent, plus 3/8, 6/4, 3/2, 9/8, 5/4 etc.), ten-fold cross-validation. Mean information content per onset: order-0 model 2.19 bits with metrical inference vs 2.29 without; order-bound 4: 1.34 bits with metrical inference vs 1.54 bits for plain IDyOM without metre. The largest gain is from order 0 to order 1. — [van der Weij et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)
- The same model inferred the notated time signature and phase for 38% (order 0), 67% (order 1) and 71% (order 4) of songs. — [van der Weij et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)
- 3/4 and 6/8 in that model: each time signature is its own category with its own learned statistics; nothing encodes that both are 12-sixteenth bars; 3/4 vs 6/8 was the most frequent confusion. — [van der Weij et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)
- In Temperley's 2007-style grid model, compound metre is expressed by two parameters: U = tactus beats per bar (2 or 3) and L = subdivisions of the tactus (2 or 3); 6/8 is a duple bar with triple subdivision; onset probability depends only on salience level (bar 3, tactus 2, lower 1, other 0). — [van der Weij PhD thesis](https://eprints.illc.uva.nl/id/document/12123)
- Pop melody data, same model types: on 401 training / 30 test pop melodies (RWC, Beatles, J-pop; 4/4 only; 138,583 training notes) held-out cross-entropy in bits per note: note-value Markov order 0/1/2 = 2.29 / 2.07 / 1.93; metrical Markov order 0/1/2 = 2.63 / 2.01 / 1.87; bar-pattern Markov order 0/1 = 1.97 / 1.89. — [Nakamura & Yoshii, arXiv:1908.06969](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.06969)

### Inferences
- Units (my derivation, not stated in what I read): Temperley's figures appear to be in nats per song. The Uniform model with P = .51 costs 0.693 nats (1.00 bit) per eighth-note beat; 62.37 / 0.693 = 90.0 beats, which matches 13,786 bars / 1,235 songs x 8 = 89.3 beats per song. On that reading, with about 0.51 x 90 = 46 notes per song, the Essen results convert to roughly: Uniform 1.96, Zeroth-Order Duration 1.71, Metrical Position 1.33, Fine-Grained 1.26, Hierarchical 1.22, First-Order Metrical Duration 1.17 bits per note. So the winner beats the runner-up by roughly 0.05 bits per note and beats the naive metrical-position model by roughly 0.16 bits per note. Treat these as estimates; the report writer should not quote them as Temperley's own numbers.
- These converted values (about 1.2 bits/note for simple 4/4 folk song with no sixteenths) are consistent in scale with van der Weij's 1.34 bits/onset (all metres, all note values) and Nakamura's 1.87-2.01 bits/note for pop melodies on a sixteenth grid. Pop melody rhythm is thus about 0.6-0.8 bits per note less predictable than Essen folk rhythm under the same first-order metrical model, though grid resolution differs.
- Across three independent studies the same ordering holds: conditioning on metrical position plus one previous onset captures most of the gain; going from first to second order adds about 0.14 bits (Nakamura: 2.01 to 1.87) and orders 2-4 little more (van der Weij).
- For the engine: a first- or second-order Markov chain over (bar position, bars skipped) per metre, backed off (PPM-style) to a Hierarchical Position model shared across metres through the U/L parametrisation, is the best-evidenced design. The backoff matters for 3/4, 6/8 and rarer metres where a full table is poorly estimated.
- The number of notes n in b bars can be imposed by conditioning: sample or score under the Markov model restricted to paths with exactly n onsets in b bars (a forward dynamic programme over position and count gives the exact normalised probability).

### Gaps
- Temperley 2010 does not report bits per note or note counts in the text I extracted; the per-note values above are my conversion.
- The quartet figures cannot be converted to per-note values without the quartet note count.
- I found no published held-out comparison of Temperley's six models on 3/4 or 6/8 material.
- van der Weij's thesis chapter 7 (classical Temperley-style model vs PPM "enculturation" model, on Dutch/German folk and Turkish makam) contains a direct comparison, but the fetch was truncated before those numbers.

## 2. Which models treat the bar or beat as a repeated/varied unit, and how much does modelling repetition help?

### Takeaway
Four mechanisms exist: GTTM's parallelism rule (qualitative), IDyOM's short-term model (an n-gram learned on the fly from the current piece), Nakamura & Yoshii's piece-specific Dirichlet-process models with "division" and "shift" edits for near-repeats, and bar-pattern ("rhythm word") Markov chains. Temperley's 2010 models contain no repetition at all, and he names it as the omission most likely to give "a substantial improvement"; the only numbers found show repetition modelling helped more than raising the Markov order in transcription, and that bar-pattern unigrams (1.97 bits/note) already beat note-level unigrams (2.29-2.63).

### Cited Findings
- Temperley 2010 states the models omit conventional multi-bar patterns, phrase structure (four- and eight-bar phrases), text rhythm, and rhythmic repetition within songs, and that a model including repetition "could achieve a substantial improvement". — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- GTTM MPR 1 and the grouping parallelism rule state that parallel passages preferably receive parallel metrical structure and grouping; no mechanism for detecting parallelism is given in the rule statements. — [Wikipedia, GTTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_theory_of_tonal_music)
- IDyOM: the short-term model "learns the current piece incrementally and is emptied before each new piece"; LTM and STM distributions are combined with entropy-based weights. — [Pearce 2018](https://www.marcus-pearce.com/assets/papers/Pearce2018.pdf)
- The metre-aware IDyOM rhythm study used the long-term model only ("In our simulations, we use only a long-term model") and notes that melodic repetition affects metre perception but was not modelled. — [van der Weij PhD thesis](https://eprints.illc.uva.nl/id/document/12123)
- Nakamura & Yoshii: repetition makes each piece's distribution over bar patterns and transitions sparse, with much lower entropy than the generic model; data sampled from the generic model do not show this, so it is a real property of songs. Piece-specific (Bayesian DP) models had significantly lower transcription error than their generic counterparts, and "the Bayesian extension often helped more than raising the model order" (the zeroth-order piece-specific metrical model beat the generic first-order one). Best model: second-order piece-specific metrical Markov, about 2.08% error on real performances. The division/shift edit operations gave a modest further gain; division helped all model types, shift only the metrical models. — [Nakamura & Yoshii, arXiv:1908.06969](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.06969)
- Bar-as-word models on the same pop data: bar-pattern Markov order 0 = 1.97 bits/note, order 1 = 1.89 bits/note, against 2.63 (metrical order 0) and 2.01 (metrical order 1). — [Nakamura & Yoshii, arXiv:1908.06969](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.06969)
- Corpus evidence that reuse is strong in pop: normalised within-song entropy of rhythmic 4-grams has median .91 in 80 Rolling Stone songs (1955-1997) and .87 in 24 Billboard top-5 songs (2015-2019), i.e. recent hits reuse rhythmic cells more; "Truth Hurts" scores .66. — [White, Pater & Breen 2021 preprint](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/545183b3e4b0f4d5bea12a07/t/6095435cf00ef61c437de243/1620394846707/PostMillenialAnalysis.pdf)
- Grammar side: Harasim et al. 2019 and Rohrmeier 2020 have split/prepare/shift rules but no copy or repetition rule; Rohrmeier 2020 "does not discuss repetition". — [Rohrmeier 2020](https://program.ismir2020.net/static/final_papers/368.pdf); [Harasim et al. 2019](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/273863/files/ismir2019_paper_000039.pdf)

### Inferences
- The best-evidenced repetition design for the engine is a two-level one: (i) a generic per-metre model, (ii) a per-song cache drawn from it (Dirichlet process / Chinese-restaurant: with probability proportional to count, reuse a bar rhythm already used in this song; with probability proportional to alpha, draw a new bar from the generic model), plus cheap edit operations (split one note, shift one onset) for varied repeats. This is Nakamura & Yoshii's model read generatively, and is the same idea as IDyOM's STM.
- A bar-rhythm vocabulary on its own cannot write unseen bars; the generic note-level model as the DP base distribution is what gives non-zero probability to rhythms never seen.
- Position-dependent reuse (bar 5 copies bar 1, bar 3 copies bar 1 with a changed ending) is not captured by any model found; it would have to be added as a prior over which earlier bar is copied.

### Gaps
- No source gave a bits-per-note gain from adding a short-term/repetition component to a rhythm model specifically. Pearce's thesis reports LTM/STM/BOTH comparisons for pitch; I did not retrieve rhythm figures.
- No published rhythm grammar with an explicit copy operation was found in this session. From memory, unverified: Finkensiep/Rohrmeier-lab and Harasim's thesis discuss repetition in grammars generally, and Temperley's "Music and Probability" discusses parallelism as a factor in metre finding without quantifying it.
- No counts found for how often bar rhythms repeat in classical themes or folk song (e.g. share of bars identical to an earlier bar).

## 3. How do the models handle the pickup (anacrusis) and phrase-final lengthening?

### Takeaway
Pickup is handled in three ways: ignored (Temperley 2010 drops everything before the first downbeat), as a phase variable (Temperley 2007-style and van der Weij: where in the bar the first note falls), or structurally as an upbeat slot attached to the following strong event (Rohrmeier, Harasim). Phrase-final lengthening is not modelled by any of the probabilistic rhythm models found; Temperley 2010 also drops the final bar after its downbeat, and only GTTM's cadence rule and Hayes's phrase-to-line alignment touch phrase endings.

### Cited Findings
- Temperley 2010: material before the first downbeat is excluded; the first note is given probability 1 in the duration models; material is included only through the last downbeat, so most of the final bar is omitted; phrase structure is listed among omitted factors. — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Temperley 2007-style model as formalised by van der Weij: the pickup is the distance from the bar line to the first onset, Pickup = Tph + Uph x T (tactus phase plus whole tactus beats); the tactus phase has one parameter, the relative frequency of rhythms that start on a tactus beat. — [van der Weij PhD thesis](https://eprints.illc.uva.nl/id/document/12123)
- van der Weij et al. 2017: phase is a hidden variable at sixteenth resolution (a 3/4 bar has 12 phases); initial phase uniform within a metre, with the prior over (metre, phase) p(m, phase) = p(m) / sum_m' T_m' p(m') so that long bars are not penalised; training corpora carry time signature and pickup annotations. — [van der Weij et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009); [van der Weij PhD thesis](https://eprints.illc.uva.nl/id/document/12123)
- Rohrmeier 2020: every time-span carries an upbeat part a; the Prepare (U-split) rule carves upbeat events from it, and the anticipate shift moves an event earlier by its upbeat; this is argued to keep upbeats context-free, where upbeats crossing metrical levels would otherwise need context-sensitive rules. — [Rohrmeier 2020](https://program.ismir2020.net/static/final_papers/368.pdf)
- Harasim et al. 2019: categories are upbeat (+) downbeat pairs; usplit separates an upbeat from its downbeat and can split the upbeat again; dsplit splits the downbeat and may introduce a new upbeat; rule probabilities depend only on whether the upbeat is zero. — [Harasim et al. 2019](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/273863/files/ismir2019_paper_000039.pdf)
- GTTM: MPR 2 "weakly prefer a metrical structure in which the strongest beat in a group appears relatively early in the group" (i.e. short upbeats), and MPR 7 "strongly prefer a metrical structure in which cadences are metrically stable". — [Wikipedia, GTTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_theory_of_tonal_music)
- Hayes: text-setting constraints include aligning phonological phrase boundaries with line boundaries and avoiding long lapses and extreme compression; these interact by ranking. — [Hayes, Textsetting page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)
- Evidence that phrase position matters in pop: the pre-2000 rock corpus shows a spike of onsets on the last eighth of the bar tied to the following downbeat (anticipation), i.e. bar-end behaviour differs from bar-interior behaviour. — [White, Pater & Breen 2021](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/545183b3e4b0f4d5bea12a07/t/6095435cf00ef61c437de243/1620394846707/PostMillenialAnalysis.pdf)

### Inferences
- For generation, pickup is best a separate first decision: P(pickup length | metre), then the bar model runs from the first downbeat. That matches both Temperley's phase variable and the grammar view (the pickup belongs to the first downbeat event, not to a "bar zero").
- Phrase-final lengthening has to be added by the engine; the literature surveyed offers no fitted model. The natural place is to make the bar model depend on the bar's place in the phrase (opening / interior / cadence bar), which the Essen corpus supports because it carries phrase marks.
- Temperley's choice to cut the last bar at its downbeat is a tacit admission that final bars do not follow the same statistics (long final note, then silence).

### Gaps
- No figures found for the frequency or length distribution of pickups by metre, or for the size of phrase-final lengthening in notated rhythm (ratio of final-note length to mean note length), in Essen, classical themes or pop.
- Hayes & Kaun 1996 (which treats line-final lengthening and cadences in sung verse, [link from search, not read](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)) was not fetched; its constraint definitions and counts are not in these notes.

## 4. How does rhythm differ between classical themes, folk song and pop/rock (syncopation, repetition, note density)?

### Takeaway
The clearest counted difference is syncopation: in 80 rock songs a third of stressed syllables are syncopated (and three quarters of those are anticipations of a strong beat), against zero in a sample of 19th-century English song; folk and classical rhythm obey "anchoring" so strongly that an unanchored quarter-level note has probability .06 in Essen. Pop note density has also risen sharply: 4.12 onsets per bar in 1955-1997 hits vs 7.19 in 2015-2019 hits.

### Cited Findings
- Rock corpus: 80 songs (top 20 per decade of the Rolling Stone list, 1950s-1990s, after excluding 17 non-4/4-or-2/4 songs and 3 rap songs), 23,129 notes, 23,032 in 4/4 bars; tempo 58-234 BPM, mean 117.2. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019, Music Perception 36(4)](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Definition: a syncopation at the n-th-note level is a stressed syllable on a weak n-th-note beat not followed by another syllable on or before the next n-th-note beat. Syncopation quotient SQ = syncopations / all stressed syllables. Anticipatory quotient ASQ = share of those syncopations that fall immediately before a strong beat (eighth positions 4 and 8; sixteenth positions 4, 8, 12, 16). — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Rock results: eighth-level syncopations 2,382 of 10,433 stressed syllables, SQ = .228; sixteenth-level SQ = .105; combined SQ = .333. ASQ = .772 (eighth), .740 (sixteenth), .762 combined. 66 of 76 songs have eighth-level ASQ > .5. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Comparison: ten 19th-century English songs, 1,397 notes, 602 stressed syllables, 18 stressed syllables on weak eighth beats, 0 syncopations (difference in weak-beat stress chi-square(1) = 240.1, p < .0001). — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Where rock anticipates (eighth level; "standard" = note on the strong beat with no note on the preceding weak eighth; "syncopation" = the note sits on that preceding eighth instead): beat 1 anticipated on eighth 8 of the previous bar: 1,046 syncopated vs 1,028 standard (tendency .504); beat 3 anticipated on eighth 4: 794 vs 639 (.554); beat 2 on eighth 2: 305 vs 389 (.439); beat 4 on eighth 6: 237 vs 438 (.351). — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Eighth- and sixteenth-level syncopation are alternatives, not additive: across songs r = -.52; no song is high on both; sixteenth-level syncopation is rare above about 120 BPM. Eighth-level SQ rises with year (logistic coefficient 0.0337, p < .001). Eighth-level ASQ does not change significantly with tempo or year. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Text: 43.5% of syllables carry primary stress; 31% of word tokens are monosyllabic function words; share of occurrences on quarter-note beats: "in" .562, "you" .335, "I" .313, "the" .250, "it" .122. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Note density in pop melody: 1955-1997 (80 Rolling Stone songs) mean 4.12 onsets per bar, within-song SD 1.87; 2015-2019 (24 Billboard top-5 songs) mean 7.19, SD 2.91. — [White, Pater & Breen 2021](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/545183b3e4b0f4d5bea12a07/t/6095435cf00ef61c437de243/1620394846707/PostMillenialAnalysis.pdf)
- Spread over the bar: normalised entropy of onsets over metrical positions, median .51 (1955-1997) vs .94 (2015-2019) - recent hits use sixteenth positions almost evenly; the older corpus has a spike on the last eighth of the bar (anticipation of the downbeat) that the newer one lacks. — [White, Pater & Breen 2021](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/545183b3e4b0f4d5bea12a07/t/6095435cf00ef61c437de243/1620394846707/PostMillenialAnalysis.pdf)
- Folk song (Essen, 4/4, no sub-eighth notes): P(note) on an eighth-note grid = .51 overall; downbeat note probability .998; weak eighth level .21 in the plain position model; quarter-level note probability .889 when only the following stronger beat has a note, .713 when both neighbours do, .265 when only the preceding one does, .062 when neither does. — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Classical instrumental (Haydn-Mozart first violin): downbeat note probability .891 (vs .998 in Essen), and a sixteenth level is needed. — [Temperley 2010](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Metre mix in folk song: in 4,966 German Essen songs the most frequent time signatures are 4/4, 2/4, 3/4 and 6/8, followed by 3/8, 4/2, 6/4, 3/2 and others. In a 1,136-song German sample: 2/4 339, 4/4 427, 3/4 296, 3/8 74; in the matched Chinese sample: 2/4 1,009, 4/4 90, 3/4 24, 3/8 13. — [van der Weij PhD thesis](https://eprints.illc.uva.nl/id/document/12123)
- Cross-style predictability: a German-trained rhythm model scores 1.34 bits/onset on German songs but 1.72 on Chinese songs; a Chinese-trained model scores 1.49 on Chinese and 1.70 on German (empirical-prior condition). — [van der Weij et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)

### Inferences
- From the Essen numbers, about 4.1 onsets per 4/4 bar (0.51 x 8) for simple folk song - essentially the same density as 1955-1997 rock melody (4.12). So the difference between folk and classic rock melody is placement (anticipation), not density; the density jump is a post-2000 phenomenon.
- Rock anticipation can be modelled as a transformation on an unsyncopated "deep" rhythm: generate with a common-practice model, then shift a stressed note from a strong beat to the preceding weak eighth (or sixteenth at slow tempo) with a position-specific probability of about .35-.55 (the table above), choosing eighth or sixteenth level per song by tempo. This is what Temperley's "anticipatory syncopation" account implies, and it matches Rohrmeier's anticipate shift rule.
- A model trained on one style mis-predicts another by roughly 0.2-0.4 bits per onset (the German/Chinese figures), which bounds what to expect when a folk- or classical-trained rhythm model is asked to score pop.

### Gaps
- No like-for-like table of syncopation rate, bar-repetition rate and note density across classical themes, folk song and pop was found; the figures above come from different studies with different definitions.
- Huron & Ommen 2006 (syncopation in American popular music 1890-1939) is cited by Tan et al. as showing rising syncopation, but its figures were not retrieved ([EMR article link from search, not read](https://www.osu.tests.sfulib4.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/EMR/article/view/6986/5453)).
- No counts for classical themes specifically (e.g. Barlow & Morgenstern dictionary): none retrieved.
- White, Pater & Breen figures are from the 2021 preprint; the published version (Journal of Mathematics and Music 16(2), 2022, [link](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17459737.2022.2075946)) was not checked for changes. The preprint itself is inconsistent on whether the newer corpus is 24 or 25 songs and whether the older one starts in 1955 or 1957.
- Tan et al. 2019 has two internal inconsistencies noted on reading: the text says sixteenth-level SQ "decreases with tempo and year" while the table shows positive coefficients, and the sixteenth-level ASQ test repeats the eighth-level chi-square value.

## 5. Ready-to-use implementations and datasets

### Takeaway
Data for fitting exist for all three repertoires (Essen folk song in Humdrum, the Rolling Stone rock melody transcriptions, Dutch folk in the Meertens collections), and two usable model code bases were confirmed (IDyOM for PPM/viewpoint models; Nakamura & Yoshii's Bayesian score-model code). No ready-made fitted rhythm grammar (tree-based) for melodies was found.

### Cited Findings
- Essen Folksong Collection in Humdrum format, from kernscores.stanford.edu; carries time signatures (and was filtered on them) and pickup information used for training. — [van der Weij et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)
- Rolling Stone rock corpus: melodic transcriptions (with rhythm, and the stress-annotated lyrics used in Tan et al.) at the corpus site. — [Rock Corpus, melodic transcriptions](https://rockcorpus.midside.com/melodic_transcriptions.html)
- Meertens Tune Collections (Dutch folk song). — [Meertens Tune Collections](https://www.liederenbank.nl/mtc/)
- IDyOM software and documentation (Lisp; variable-order PPM, LTM/STM, viewpoints including onset/IOI). — [IDyOM project wiki, "Running IDyOM"](https://code.soundsoftware.ac.uk/projects/idyom-project/wiki/Idyom)
- A Python re-implementation of IDyOM appeared in 2026. — [GraphIDyOM, arXiv:2607.25787](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25787)
- Nakamura & Yoshii: data and source code for the note-value, metrical and bar-pattern Markov models and their piece-specific Bayesian versions. — [bayesianscoremodel.github.io, as stated in arXiv:1908.06969](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.06969)
- Syncopation model implementations and a rated rhythm dataset (Queen Mary, SMC 2015 project covering Longuet-Higgins & Lee and other syncopation measures). — [syncopation-dataset project](https://code.soundsoftware.ac.uk/projects/syncopation-dataset)
- Hayes text-setting data: 346 lines with stress transcription and preferred settings, tab-delimited text. — [Hayes, Textsetting page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)
- Harasim's GitHub hosts material for his grammar work. — [dharasim/MCR wiki](https://github.com/dharasim/MCR/wiki)
- Temperley's papers (including the 2010 paper PDF) are on his site. — [davidtemperley.com](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)

### Inferences
- The fastest route to a fitted model for the engine is to re-implement Temperley's models 5 and 6 and a PPM back-off directly (they are counting models; a few dozen lines each) and fit them per metre on Essen (folk), the Rock Corpus melodies (pop/rock) and a classical theme set, then compare held-out bits per note against the published scale (about 1.2-1.3 Essen 4/4; 1.34 Essen all metres; 1.9-2.0 pop on a sixteenth grid).
- Essen is the only one of these corpora with substantial 3/4 and 6/8 material and phrase marks, so it is the natural training set for metre generality and phrase-ending behaviour.

### Gaps
- I did not open the repositories to check licences, formats or whether they run today.
- From memory, unverified: IDyOM is also on GitHub (mtpearce/idyom); Temperley's Melisma analyser and his probabilistic metre code are downloadable from his site; Foscarin & Jacquemard's "qparse" is on an Inria GitLab; "synpy" (Song, Pearce & Harte) is the Python syncopation toolkit behind the Queen Mary project; music21 ships the Essen collection. None of these URLs was confirmed this session.
- No public dataset of classical themes with clean rhythm encoding was confirmed in this session.
- Neural models (brief, from memory, unverified - no source read): LSTM/Transformer melody models (e.g. Folk-RNN, Music Transformer, and bar/position token schemes such as REMI) learn rhythm implicitly from bar-position and duration tokens, which is the same conditioning as the metrical Markov model with a long context; I retrieved no held-out rhythm-only bits-per-note figures for them.
