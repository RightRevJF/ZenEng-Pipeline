## 1. What are the sentence and the period, in exact bar proportions, and how common is each in classical themes?

### Takeaway
The sentence is basic idea + repeated basic idea + continuation-to-cadence in a 1:1:2 ratio (2+2+4 in the 8-bar model; scalable by halving or doubling); the period is antecedent (weak cadence, usually a half cadence) + consequent (same opening, stronger cadence, usually a perfect authentic cadence), normally 4+4. Caplin himself gives almost no counts; the only tallies found are a 214-sentence cross-style corpus ("simple" 1:1:2 sentences by far the most common) and a recount of Schoenberg's own 37 examples (fewer than half are strict 1:1:2).

### Cited Findings

Sentence: definition and proportions
- A sentence has two subphrases, presentation and continuation. The presentation is "a basic idea (b.i.) and its repetition" and "is often four measures long". "Basic ideas are often two measures long, but one-measure or four-measure basic ideas also occur with some frequency." — [Open Music Theory, "Phrase Archetypes"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/phrase-archetypes-unique-forms/)
- "The continuation is often the same length as the presentation"; "it's not common for the continuation to be shorter than the presentation." — [Open Music Theory, "Phrase Archetypes"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/phrase-archetypes-unique-forms/)
- Continuation devices: fragmentation ("making unit sizes shorter than the previously established unit sizes") and increased harmonic rhythm ("chord changes occur more often than before"); the glossary adds faster surface rhythm and sequences. "Continuations always drive toward a cadence", which may be a PAC, HC or IAC. — [Open Music Theory, "Phrase Archetypes"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/phrase-archetypes-unique-forms/); [Open Music Theory, "AABA and Strophic Form" glossary](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/aaba-and-strophic-form/)
- "Presentations typically begin on the tonic harmony." Minimum requirement: "to be sentential, a phrase needs a presentation and a continuation." The archetype is 4 or 8 measures; many real sentences lack some traits. — [Open Music Theory, "Phrase Archetypes"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/phrase-archetypes-unique-forms/)
- Schoenberg's sentence is a basic idea, immediately repeated, then a continuation with developmental and then cadential function. The "practice form" ratio basic idea : repetition : continuation is 1:1:2, usually written (2 x 2) + 4, a formula the article attributes to Ratz (1973). Schoenberg held the proportions flexible ("a practice form is only an abstraction from art forms"); what counts is proportion, not notated bar count, so halved or doubled versions are still sentences. — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures", College Music Symposium vol. 54](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)
- Callahan's working definition (after Caplin 1998, BaileyShea 2004, Richards 2011): (1) a presentation of two basic ideas, "almost always exactly two"; (2) a continuation that goes beyond the n + n repetition and builds momentum; (3) a half or authentic cadence. Typical lengths: presentation 2n, continuation 2n, whole 4n. Continuation options: accelerate harmonic or surface rhythm, fragment into ½n + ½n + n, leave the tonic, or run as one unbroken 2n unit. — [Callahan, "Sentential Lyric-Types in the Great American Songbook", MTO 19.3 (2013)](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)
- Inside the "simple" sentence's 4-bar continuation, two layouts are given: 2 bars of development + 2-bar cadential idea, or 3 bars of development + 1-bar cadential idea. — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)
- The continuation "always begins with material motivically related to the basic idea" (the article's reading of Schoenberg). — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)

Sentence: how the basic idea is repeated
- Schoenberg and Caplin name three relations between basic idea and its repetition: exact, sequential, and complementary (Caplin's "statement-response"). BaileyShea splits the last: complementary = idea starts on tonic and the repetition returns to tonic (e.g. I-V / V-I); statement-response = idea on tonic, repetition ends on dominant (e.g. I-I / V-V). — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)

Sentence: counts
- Schoenberg's 37 sentence examples in *Fundamentals*, recounted: 6 have a basic idea that is not repeated; 8 have a continuation whose first segment is less than half the basic idea's length; fewer than half show strict 1:1:2 proportions. Schoenberg's usual basic idea is 7 to 15 notes long; 9 examples are shorter. — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)
- Corpus of 214 sentences (Baroque through popular music), five types: simple (strict 1:1 presentation:continuation), DF/NF (delayed or no fragmentation), expanded, truncated (keeps at least 75% of the model), imitative. Reported trends: simple sentences are "by far the most common"; DF/NF rare in Baroque, common elsewhere; expanded sentences uncommon in popular music; truncated sentences rare or absent in Romantic and popular music; imitative rare outside Baroque. The per-type numbers are in the article's Figure 13 and were not readable as text. — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)

Period: definition and proportions
- A period is "a phrase-level form consisting of an antecedent and a consequent." The antecedent "is often four measures long", typically starts on tonic, and "ends with a weaker cadence, most often a half cadence (HC)." — [Open Music Theory, "Phrase Archetypes"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/phrase-archetypes-unique-forms/)
- The consequent "ends with a stronger cadence than the antecedent, most often a perfect authentic cadence (PAC)"; "it may be the same length as or longer than the antecedent; it's rare for it to be shorter." A parallel period is one "in which the consequent begins the same way as the antecedent." "The consequent's contrasting idea is almost always different from the antecedent's." If the period modulates, it usually does so in the consequent. — [Open Music Theory, "Phrase Archetypes"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/phrase-archetypes-unique-forms/)
- A "repeated phrase" is not a period: both statements must end with the same cadence. — [Open Music Theory, "Phrase Archetypes"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/phrase-archetypes-unique-forms/)

Hybrids and compounds (Caplin's four hybrids, 8 bars each in the textbook examples)
- Hybrid 1 = antecedent + continuation. Hybrid 2 = antecedent + four-measure cadential progression. Hybrid 3 = compound basic idea (CBI) + continuation. Hybrid 4 = CBI + consequent ("resembles the period, with the exception that the first phrase is comprised of a 'compound basic idea'"). The page does not rank them by frequency. — [Open Music Theory 1e (Wharton and Shaffer), "Classical Theme Types - Hybrid Themes"](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Music/Music_Theory/Open_Music_Theory_1e_(Wharton_and_Shaffer_Eds)/04%3A_Form_(I)_-_Thematic_Structure_in_the_Classical_Style/4.03%3A_Classical_Theme_Types_-_Hybrid_Themes)
- Compound forms: "it's possible for a period to be made of two sentences." — [Open Music Theory, "Phrase Archetypes"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/phrase-archetypes-unique-forms/)

Caplin's corpus and his attitude to counting
- Caplin's repertory is instrumental music of Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven, c. 1780-1810. He states that "relatively few instances in the repertory correspond identically to the complete definition of a given category" (p. 4). The reviewer notes Caplin uses adverbs ("frequently", "sometimes") instead of numbers; the one figure quoted is that about 10 percent of classical minuets resemble small binary (p. 220). — [Grave, review of Caplin's *Classical Form*, MTO 4.6 (1998)](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.98.4.6/mto.98.4.6.grave.php)
- "Tight-knit" organization (Caplin p. 257): "conventional theme-types, harmonic-tonal stability ... a symmetrical grouping structure, form-functional efficiency, and a unity of melodic-motivic material." "Loose" (p. 255): non-conventional structures, instability, "asymmetrical grouping structure, phrase-structural extension and expansion, form-functional redundancy." — [Grave, MTO 4.6](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.98.4.6/mto.98.4.6.grave.php)

### Inferences
- Rule set for a sentence generator, each element sourced above: pick n (1, 2 or 4 bars; 2 is the default). Bars 1..n = basic idea over tonic. Bars n+1..2n = repetition, one of three kinds: exact, sequential (transposed), or statement-response (re-harmonized toward dominant, or returning to tonic). Bars 2n+1..4n = continuation, opening with material derived from the basic idea, cut to ½n + ½n units, with faster chord change, ending in a cadence that occupies the last ¼ to ½ of the continuation (1 or 2 bars of 4).
- Rule set for a period generator: phrase 1 = basic idea + contrasting idea, ending on a weak cadence (default HC). Phrase 2 = same basic idea + a different contrasting idea, ending on a stronger cadence (default PAC). Phrase 2 length >= phrase 1 length. Never end both halves with the same cadence, or the result is a repeated phrase, not a period.
- The one-line difference between the two models is where the repeat falls: the sentence repeats its 2-bar idea immediately (bars 3-4), the period repeats it after a contrasting idea (bars 5-6). A melody engine can treat this as one switch.
- Because fewer than half of Schoenberg's own examples are strict 1:1:2, and Caplin says exact matches to a definition are few, the engine should treat 2+2+4 as a centre of gravity with controlled deviations (expansion of the continuation is the commonest one), not as a hard gate.

### Gaps

- Caplin's own wording could not be checked against the book. Recalled, not verified here: Caplin's model sentence is 8 real measures (2-bar basic idea, 2-bar repetition, 4-bar continuation phrase with fragmentation, harmonic acceleration, increased surface rhythm and sequential harmony, closing with a cadential idea); his period is 4+4 with basic idea + contrasting idea in each half; "liquidation" (from Schoenberg) is the systematic removal of a motive's characteristic features until only uncharacteristic, conventional material is left, which is what makes the cadence sound like an ending; his compound themes are 16 measures.

## 2. Which scholars have shown these forms in pop and rock? What is SRDC and how does it map to the classical sentence?

### Takeaway
SRDC (statement, restatement or response, departure, conclusion) is Walter Everett's label for the usual inside of a pop strophe or verse; Summach, Callahan, Nobile and the Open Music Theory authors all treat it as the pop sentence: S + R = presentation, D = continuation (fragmentation, faster chords, often subdominant), C = cadence. The best counts are Summach's (more than a quarter of 700 Top-20 hits 1955-89 contain a sentential module; strophes are sentential almost half the time) and Callahan's (111 of 207 Songbook songs contain a sentence).

### Cited Findings

SRDC: origin, definition, proportions
- SRDC is a four-part pattern: s (statement), r (restatement or response), d (departure), c (conclusion). The labels come from Walter Everett, *The Beatles as Musicians* (1999, p. 16); Everett later (2009, pp. 146-47) tied the pattern to verse-prechorus-chorus form. — [Summach, "The Structure, Function, and Genesis of the Prechorus", MTO 17.3 (2011)](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- The essential feature is "short-short-long proportionality": dc together must be longer than either s or r. Standard case: s = 4 bars, r = 4 bars, dc = 8 bars, 16 bars total ("Dream Lover", "Everybody Wants to Rule the World"). Modules typically run about 8 to 24 bars. — [Summach, MTO 17.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- Stretched cases: "Call Me" has s, r, d at 4 bars each and c at 8; "Runaway" has s, r, d at 8 bars each and c at 16 (40 bars). — [Summach, MTO 17.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- Momentum in dc comes from faster harmonic change, fragmentation, and optionally subdominant emphasis in d. In "Dream Lover" chords last two bars in s and r, one bar in d, and half a bar in c. — [Summach, MTO 17.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- r is usually a restatement of s but sometimes a response (e.g. "Wild One"). Summach builds the pop sentence on BaileyShea's (2004) list of essential and optional sentence features: aa'b extended either by return (aa'ba') or by new material (aa'bc). — [Summach, MTO 17.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- "An srdc structure shares many features with the Classical sentence." In both AABA and strophic songs srdc is the most common internal pattern of the strophe; for three-part strophes the 12-bar blues is most common. — [Open Music Theory, "AABA and Strophic Form"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/aaba-and-strophic-form/)

Summach's counts (700 songs, Billboard annual Top 20s, 1955-1989)
- Of the 700 songs, 93 were strophic and 202 were AABA. "More than a quarter" of the 700 include a sentential module. Strophes are sentential "almost half of the time." — [Summach, MTO 17.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- Share of AABA and strophic songs with sentential strophes: under a third in 1959; 60% in 1964; an average of 43% in 1965-69. — [Summach, MTO 17.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- The prechorus grows out of d: as sentences expand, "d, with its momentum-building characteristics, emerges as a prechorus," s and r become the verse, and c becomes the chorus. "The origins of the prechorus lie at an intersection between verse-chorus form and strophic form." — [Summach, MTO 17.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- In 1965-69 verse-chorus songs averaged 8 of the 20 annual Top-20 slots and about a third had a prechorus; by the late 1980s 60% did. Prechorus use dipped around 1970 and around 1980 (only 1 of 12 disco songs in 1979 had one). Median song length rose from about 150 seconds (early 1960s) to about 250 seconds (late 1980s). — [Summach, MTO 17.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- Summach's threshold opinion, as reported by de Clercq: a 4-bar passage is not long enough to count as a prechorus. (Opinion.) — [de Clercq, "Pitfalls and Windfalls in Corpus Studies of Pop/Rock Music", EuroMAC 9 (2017)](https://creaa.unistra.fr/websites/gream/Activites/Euromac_2017_-_Postprint_-_Article_-_DeCLERCQ_Trevor.pdf)

Callahan's counts (Great American Songbook: 207 songs, 1905-1973, five composers)
- "Eight- and sixteen-measure phrases are the overwhelming norms," with AABA and ABAC song forms and two- and four-measure sub-phrases. — [Callahan, MTO 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)
- "111 of the 207 songs contain at least one phrase" meeting the sentence criteria (54%). By composer: Rodgers 67%, Porter 63%, Gershwin 59%, Loewe 42%, Kern 37%. — [Callahan, MTO 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)
- How the lyric lines up with the sentence, as share of sentential phrases: List + Reveal 35% (list items in the presentation and fragments, a "reveal" at the cadence); 2n + 2n 20%; n + n + 2n 18%; Bookended 16% (title at start and end, contrast between). These four total 89%. — [Callahan, MTO 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)
- Worked proportions: Rodgers, "You Took Advantage of Me": presentation 2n, then ½n + ½n + n in bars 5-8. Porter, "All of You": nested ¼n + ¼n + ½n. Loewe, "With a Little Bit of Luck": 16-bar sentence expanded to 20. Loewe, "I Could Have Danced All Night": one 64-bar sentence across a whole AABA (the two A sections are the presentation; bridge + last A fuse as continuation and cadence). — [Callahan, MTO 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)
- Callahan adopts Everett's (2009, pp. 140-43) Statement-Restatement-Departure terms but replaces "Conclusion" with "Return" for the bookended type. — [Callahan, MTO 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)

Nobile, *Form as Harmony in Rock Music* (OUP 2020), via review
- Repertoire: rock, 1960-1991. A "functional circuit" is "a harmonic trajectory spanning a complete formal unity": tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, tonic (p. 5). A cadence must coincide with the end of a complete circuit (p. 34), an idea drawn from Caplin. — [Barna, review of Nobile, MTO 28.1 (2022)](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.1/mto.22.28.1.barna.html)
- Sectional verses complete a circuit and are usually cast in a small form: "aaba, srdc (statement, restatement, departure, conclusion), or a blues progression." Initiating verses prolong tonic, do not cadence, and must lead to a chorus. Chorus types: sectional (autonomous), continuation (no full circuit, often starts off tonic, cadences at the end), telos (arrival, usually over tonic prolongation). — [Barna, MTO 28.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.1/mto.22.28.1.barna.html)
- Reviewer's criticisms (opinion): the method leans on Schenkerian training, and its harmony-first view sidelines rhythm, text and timbre. — [Barna, MTO 28.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.1/mto.22.28.1.barna.html)

Sentences in popular music within a cross-style corpus
- The 214-sentence taxonomy corpus includes popular songs; its example "Here Comes the Sun" has a cadential idea compressed by a meter change. Trend statements: expanded sentences are uncommon and truncated ones rare or absent in popular music, i.e. pop sentences cluster on the plain 1:1:2 model. — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)

### Inferences
- Mapping table the engine can use (assembled from Summach, Callahan, Nobile via Barna, and Open Music Theory):
  - S = basic idea; tonic; 2 or 4 bars; lyric line 1.
  - R = repetition of the idea (exact, transposed, or over different chords); 2 or 4 bars; lyric line 2, often a parallel or list item.
  - D = continuation; new or cut-down material in half-length units; chord rate doubles; pre-dominant (often IV) emphasis; lyric fragments.
  - C = cadence; chord rate doubles again or holds; completes dominant to tonic; carries the title or tail refrain (Callahan's "reveal").
- The same shape scales: 8 bars (2+2+2+2, Songbook A section), 16 bars (4+4+4+4, rock strophe), and song level (verse = S+R, prechorus = D, chorus = C). One generator with a scale parameter covers all three.
- Nobile's circuit gives the harmonic rule under the melodic one: S and R sit on tonic, D moves to pre-dominant, C delivers dominant and tonic. This is the same alignment as the classical sentence, where the presentation prolongs tonic and the continuation leads to the cadence.
- The period is less documented in pop by the sources reached here. Callahan's "2n + 2n" class (20% of sentential phrases) and the ABAC song form are the nearest counted items. The evidence base for "pop uses the period" is thinner than for "pop uses the sentence".

## 3. What is known, with numbers, about melodic sequence, repetition at pitch vs transposed repetition, and where the melodic peak sits?

### Takeaway
The arch (rise then fall) is the most common phrase contour in large folk-song corpora and is confirmed against random baselines, but I could not retrieve Huron's exact percentages, and no source found states a numeric position for the peak. Repetition is well counted at the phrase level in pop (repeated phrases cover 50-90% of a song; over 90% of songs use only two or three distinct phrases); counts of transposed vs at-pitch repetition were not found.

### Cited Findings

Contour and the arch
- Huron (1996, "The Melodic Arch in Western Folksongs", *Computing in Musicology* 10) studied 6,251 melodies from the European part of the Essen collection and sorted phrases into nine contour classes. — [Baker, "MeloSol Corpus", Empirical Musicology Review](https://www.osu.tests.sfulib4.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/EMR/article/view/7645/6267)
- Of Huron's nine types, four are highly frequent and the other five are each 1.2% of the data or less. — [Cornelissen et al., "Melodic contour does not cluster" (arXiv 2604.13119)](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13119v1)
- Huron's second method: average the pitch at each note position across all phrases of the same length (shown for 7-, 8- and 9-note phrases, "almost twenty thousand musical phrases"); the averaged curve rises and then falls. Exceptions exist (e.g. "Joy to the World" starts high). — [Shanahan, course notes on empirical methods](https://shanahdt.github.io/mus-345-445/emp_methods_workshop/quantitative.html)
- Replication in sight-singing melodies: in the 783-melody MeloSol corpus, contours show "a predominance of convex contours, followed by ascending, then descending contours." (Whole melodies, not phrases.) — [Baker, EMR](https://www.osu.tests.sfulib4.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/EMR/article/view/7645/6267)
- Tierney, Russo and Patel (2011): 9,467 folk songs, 52,899 phrases, 32 regions. Arch contours (middle third of the phrase higher than both outer thirds) were significantly more common than V-shapes (P < 0.0001). Phrase-final notes are longer than non-final notes: mean 1.58 vs 0.93 (relative duration units; t = 134.3, P < 0.0001). Human song is biased toward small intervals (median interval compression ratio 1.46). — [Tierney, Russo and Patel, "The motor origins of human and avian song structure", PNAS 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3174665)
- Cornelissen, Zuidema and Burgoyne (ISMIR 2021) define the melodic arch hypothesis as "the claim that melodic phrases tend to be arch-shaped or descending" and find real phrases significantly more descending and more arched than random segments of the same melodies (Essen subsets of 9,782 and 7,601 contours, plus chant). 95% of contour variance needs 1 cosine component for motifs, 9 for phrases, 61 for songs. — [Cornelissen et al., "Cosine Contours", ISMIR 2021](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2021/paper/000016.pdf)
- Caution: a 2026 preprint by the same group argues contour shapes form a continuum and do not fall into discrete clusters, so "nine types" is a labelling convenience. The most common phrases are 5 to 15 notes long. — [Cornelissen et al., arXiv 2604.13119](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13119v1)
- Phrase joints: 32.41% of adjacent phrase pairs in 1,268 German folk songs (5,289 pairs) have a pitch discontinuity at the join; duration discontinuity at 4.51%. Phrases run 2 to 21 notes. — [Rodríguez-López and Volk, "Symbolic Segmentation: A Corpus-Based Analysis of Melodic Phrases"](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/289643/MRodriguez-CMMR13-corrected.pdf?sequence=1)

Peaks and words
- In 679 pop lead sheets (4/4 only), local melodic peaks (a note higher than both neighbours) coincide with stressed syllables significantly more than chance (chi-square, p < 0.0001); 55% of notes on primary-stress syllables are long and 55% of notes on unstressed syllables are short. — [Nichols, Morris, Basu and Raphael, "Relationships Between Lyrics and Melody in Popular Music", ISMIR 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)

Repetition in pop, counted
- POP909 (909 Chinese pop songs, human-transcribed MIDI): repeated melodic phrases cover "50% to 90% of the whole song" in most songs; most phrases are 4 or 8 measures; over 90% of songs have only two or three distinct melodic phrases; sections hold 1 to 6 phrases and 1 to 3 distinct phrases. — [Dai, Zhang and Dannenberg, "Automatic Analysis and Influence of Hierarchical Structure on Melody, Rhythm and Harmony in Popular Music" (arXiv 2010.07518)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.07518)
- Between successive sections: 20% repeat the previous section's phrases exactly, 29% repeat its ending (suffix), 18% repeat its beginning (prefix). — [Dai et al., arXiv 2010.07518](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.07518)
- Cadence placement in the same corpus: probability of V-I is 0.89 at phrase ends (0.84 at phrase ends inside a section, 0.94 at section ends) vs 0.47 elsewhere; 58% of progressions at section ends are V-I. 72% of notes a whole note or longer fall at section-final phrase ends; only 6.4% at mid-section phrase ends. Melody tends to scale degree 1 at section ends and degree 3 at phrase starts (no figure given). — [Dai et al., arXiv 2010.07518](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.07518)
- Rhythm-cell repetition: Billboard top-five melodies of 2015-2019 reuse their most frequent 4-note rhythm patterns more heavily than 1955-1997 melodies (rank-frequency slope -6.93 vs -4.22). — [White, Pater and Breen, "A Comparative Analysis of Melodic Rhythm in Two Corpora of American Popular Music", J. Mathematics and Music 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10356498)

Sequence
- Sequence is named as one of the three standard ways to repeat a basic idea (exact, sequential, complementary / statement-response) and as one of the continuation devices, in both the classical and pop-textbook accounts. — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures); [Open Music Theory, "AABA and Strophic Form" glossary](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/aaba-and-strophic-form/)

### Inferences
- Defaults a rule engine can take from the counted evidence: phrase contour = arch or descent unless there is a reason otherwise; the highest region belongs in the middle third of the phrase (the Tierney definition is the only numeric placement located); final note of a phrase about 1.7 times the average length of the others (1.58 / 0.93); longest notes saved for the section-final cadence; start phrases on degree 3 (or 1 or 5) and end sections on degree 1; put local high notes on stressed syllables.
- Repetition budget: 2 or 3 distinct phrase ideas per song, with repeats filling more than half the running length. Varying the ending while keeping the beginning (prefix repeat) and varying the beginning while keeping the ending (suffix repeat) are each about as common as exact repeat between sections.
- Since 9 components are needed for 95% of phrase-contour variance, a pure "arch" template explains only part of real phrases. The arch should be a bias on the note-chooser, not a fixed curve.

### Gaps
- Huron's exact contour percentages and phrase count were not on any page fetched. Recalled, not verified here: about 36,000 phrases, with convex near 40%, then descending, ascending and concave in that order, the four together covering roughly 95% or more. Note the order "descending second" conflicts with MeloSol's whole-melody order (ascending second); the difference may be phrase vs whole melody. Verify against the original paper before quoting.
- No source found gives the position of the single highest note as a fraction of the phrase or song (the often-repeated "about two thirds through" claim is unsupported by anything retrieved here; treat as lore).
- No corpus count was found for how often a repeated idea in pop or classical themes returns at pitch vs transposed (sequence), nor for the usual transposition interval. Only the category lists exist. This is a real hole for the engine and may need the user's own counting on an annotated set.
- No count found for motivic liquidation in pop.

## 4. Are there corpus studies comparing classical themes and pop melodies on the same measures?

### Takeaway
Direct like-for-like comparisons are scarce. What exists: one cross-style sentence corpus (214 sentences, Baroque to pop) with qualitative trends only; Temperley and de Clercq's rock corpus compared with common-practice scale-degree profiles (flat 7 beats natural 7 in rock melody, the reverse of classical); and pop-vs-pop rhythm comparisons across eras.

### Cited Findings
- 214-sentence corpus across styles: simple 1:1:2 sentences dominate everywhere; expanded sentences are uncommon in popular music; truncated sentences are rare or absent in Romantic and popular music; imitative ones are rare outside Baroque. — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)
- Rolling Stone corpus: 200 rock songs, 194 with transcribed melody. In common-practice music, rock harmony and rock melody alike, the most frequent scale degree is 1, then 5. But "b7 is more common than 7 in rock melodies," the reverse of common practice. The major scale is the most frequent pitch set in 46 of 194 melodies; the 8-note "pentatonic union" in 37. In 144 of 194 songs melody and harmony agree on major or minor; most of the rest have minor-type melody over major-type harmony. — [Temperley and de Clercq, "Statistical Analysis of Harmony and Melody in Rock Music", J. New Music Research 2013](https://www.midside.com/publications/temperley_declercq_2013.pdf)
- The common-practice side of that comparison is only 46 excerpts, shown as graphs without printed values. — [Temperley and de Clercq 2013](https://www.midside.com/publications/temperley_declercq_2013.pdf)
- Pop vs pop over time (melodic rhythm): notes per bar 4.29 (1955-1997) vs 6.75 (2015-2019), tempo-standardized; notes per second 1.87 vs 2.71; variance of notes per bar 1.84 vs 2.91; metric-position entropy 0.81 vs 0.89. — [White, Pater and Breen 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10356498)
- Within pop over time: similarity between phrases falls and phrase complexity rises with release date (P < .01 and P < .002) in POP909. — [Dai et al., arXiv 2010.07518](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.07518)
- Phrase length norms match across the two repertories as described by their analysts: classical model themes are 4 or 8 bars; Songbook phrases are "overwhelming[ly]" 8 and 16 bars; POP909 phrases are mostly 4 or 8 bars. — [Open Music Theory, "Phrase Archetypes"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/phrase-archetypes-unique-forms/); [Callahan, MTO 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php); [Dai et al., arXiv 2010.07518](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.07518)
- Corpus-method caution from de Clercq: results depend heavily on encoding choices; even two expert annotators agreed on Roman-numeral function only 92% of the time. — [de Clercq, "Pitfalls and Windfalls", EuroMAC 9](https://creaa.unistra.fr/websites/gream/Activites/Euromac_2017_-_Postprint_-_Article_-_DeCLERCQ_Trevor.pdf)

### Inferences
- The claim "pop uses the classical models at shorter length" is supported mainly by analysts' case-based and count-based work inside pop (Summach, Callahan), not by a single study that measured both repertories with one ruler. The report should say so.
- "Shorter length" needs care: the pop SRDC strophe is typically 16 bars against the classical 8-bar model, so in bar count pop is often longer. The models are shorter in pop only in the sense that the idea (n) is short and plain, and that expansion and truncation are rarer.
- Where pop and classical melody differ measurably is pitch vocabulary (flat 7, pentatonic sets, melody that ignores the chord's mode) and rhythm density, not the phrase plan.

### Gaps
- No study was found that compares classical themes with pop melodies on repetition rate, interval-size distribution, or rhythmic variety using the same method. Temperley and de Clercq 2013 reports no interval distribution at all.
- Recalled, not verified here: Temperley's work on syncopation and "melodic-harmonic divorce" (Temperley 2007) and Tan, Lustig and Temperley (2019, *Music Perception*) on anticipatory syncopation in rock give rock-vs-classical rhythm numbers; not retrieved.
- The per-style table in the 214-sentence corpus (Figure 13) should be read from the article directly; it would give the only cross-style counts of sentence types.

## 5. How do these forms work in 3/4 and 6/8 specifically?

### Takeaway
No source reached gives triple- or compound-meter rules for the sentence or period; the theory is stated in proportions (1:1:2) and in "real" measures that may be half or double the notated bar, so the forms carry over by counting felt bars, not written ones.

### Cited Findings
- Proportion, not notated bar count, defines the sentence: a version with doubled or halved bar counts is still a sentence. — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)
- Caplin raises the question "whether each real measure equals a notated measure or one-half a notated measure" (p. 272, note 53); the reviewer explains that some late-18th-century 4/4 movements behave like cut time with every other bar line removed. — [Grave, MTO 4.6](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.98.4.6/mto.98.4.6.grave.php)
- Triple-meter pieces (minuets, sarabandes, waltzes; Chopin's Waltz in B minor as a delayed-fragmentation sentence) sit in the 214-sentence corpus without special treatment; meter is not analysed as a variable. — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)
- Meter can be reinterpreted to restore the model: in "Here Comes the Sun" the cadential idea is squeezed by a meter change, and hearing the last 4/4 bars as 6/8 + 2/4 gives a more typical bar count. — ["A Taxonomy of Sentence Structures"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-54/articles-1752877057/a-taxonomy-of-sentence-structures)
- A 64-bar single sentence exists in a fast waltz-time show tune ("I Could Have Danced All Night", presentation = the two A sections). — [Callahan, MTO 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)
- Evidence limits: the pop lyric-melody corpus excluded everything not in 4/4, so its peak and stress findings are untested in 3/4 and 6/8. — [Nichols et al., ISMIR 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)

### Inferences
- Working rule for the engine: build the form in felt bars. In moderate 3/4, one written bar = one real bar, so 2+2+4 bars holds as written. In fast one-in-a-bar waltz time, two (or four) written bars = one real bar, so the sentence is written as 4+4+8 or 8+8+16 (the Loewe case is 16+16+32). In 6/8 felt in two, one written bar holds two beats and may act as one real bar (2+2+4 written) or as two (1+1+2 written, a 4-bar sentence), depending on tempo.
- Fragmentation in triple meter cannot halve a 3-beat bar cleanly. The safe reading of "½n" is in bars (2-bar idea cut to 1-bar units); below the bar the engine needs its own rule (e.g. hemiola or 1-beat cells), which no source here supplies.

### Gaps
- No source states how sentences or periods behave differently in 3/4 or 6/8, and no corpus count by meter was found. Caplin's real-measure discussion (R = N, R = 2N, R = ½N) was seen only through one quoted footnote; the full passage in *Classical Form* (and in his 2013 textbook *Analyzing Classical Form*) should be read directly.
- No pop-specific study of phrase models in waltz-time or 6/8 ballads was located.
