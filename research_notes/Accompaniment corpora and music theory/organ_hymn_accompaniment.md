# Organ (and Keyboard) Accompaniment of Hymns, Carols and Anthems: Moving Beyond Doubling the Four Voice Parts

Scope note: research done 2026-11-15 with 16 web searches and fetches. Sources were AGO (*The American Organist*), RSCM Australia, PCUSA, *Reformed Worship* (Calvin Institute), *The Diapason*, IMSLP, Hymnary.org, Boston University's organ library catalogue, Wikipedia, a University of York thesis, and an organ-training course. The RCO's hymn-playing video (Daniel Moult) is paywalled, so its content could not be read. "Consensus" below means a point stated independently by two or more of these bodies or authors. Single-author points are labelled as opinion.

## 1. What do pedagogy texts and professional bodies (AGO, RSCM, RCO) teach about hymn playing and free accompaniment, verse by verse?

### Takeaway
The sources agree on a plan. Play a short introduction. Keep the inner stanzas close to the hymnal harmony, with varied registration, manual and texture. Save the richest harmony for introductions, interludes and the final stanza, where the congregation (and choir) sing the tune in unison over a free, fuller and often louder organ part. That stanza is sometimes prepared by an interlude or modulation and often has a descant. Throughout, the congregation must stay the main sound.

### Cited Findings

**Overall principle: the text drives the treatment (consensus)**
- RSCM Australia (Dr Steven Nisbet OAM): "The meaning of the text dictates the mood of the hymn's accompaniment and level of harmonic complexity." Strong tunes such as HYFRYDOL get different treatment from reflective ones such as FINLANDIA. — [RSCM Australia, Nisbet, "Reharmonising Last Verses of Hymns" script, 2021](https://rscmaustralia.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Reharmonisation-of-last-verses-of-hymns-video-script-by-SN.pdf)
- PCUSA (Enoch Smith Jr.): "Reharmonization changes the harmonic language while preserving the melody." "The goal is not to surprise the congregation but to illuminate the text in fresh ways." Test each change with three questions: does it support the text, does it invite singing, does it help the congregation worship? — [PCUSA, "Improvising & Reharmonizing in Worship" Quicksheet, 2026](https://pcusa.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/Improvising%20%26%20Reharmonizing%20in%20Worship%20Quicksheet.pdf)
- Emily R. Brink: organists should not "play every stanza exactly the same way". They "preach" the text through their playing, and "the primary sound must come from the congregation." She also criticises pressing one preset for every stanza. — [Reformed Worship, Brink, "The Organist's Use of Alternative Hymn Arrangements"](https://reformedworship.org/resource/i-sing-bass-organists-use-alternative-hymn-arrangements)

**Where the richness goes (consensus)**
- PCUSA: "Reserve richer harmonies for introductions, interludes, or final verses." Keep the stanzas the congregation sings simpler. Also: "Simplicity is often more effective than complexity." — [PCUSA Quicksheet](https://pcusa.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/Improvising%20%26%20Reharmonizing%20in%20Worship%20Quicksheet.pdf)
- Brink: alternative arrangements appear "most often on the last stanza, and sometimes preceded by a transition". A first attempt should use "the final stanza of a very familiar melody" that is climactic or doxological. — [Reformed Worship, Brink](https://reformedworship.org/resource/i-sing-bass-organists-use-alternative-hymn-arrangements)
- "Last verse harmonisation" is described as keeping the melody (sometimes embellished) while the harmony changes. "Organists recommend it as a technique which encourages the congregation to sing." — [Wikipedia, "Last verse harmonisation"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_verse_harmonisation)

**Unison singing pairs with free accompaniment (consensus)**
- On free-harmony stanzas, "everyone sings the tune together in a strong unison". Harmony singing "should certainly be possible much of the time". Inner stanzas are therefore usually left in hymnal harmony so the basses can sing. — [Reformed Worship, Brink](https://reformedworship.org/resource/i-sing-bass-organists-use-alternative-hymn-arrangements); see also [Reformed Worship, "Why we shouldn't always sing in 4-part harmony"](https://reformedworship.org/resource/i-sing-bass-why-we-shouldnt-always-sing-4-part-harmony)
- When a choir leads, it usually sings the last verse in unison rather than SATB. Trebles or sopranos (occasionally tenors) may add a descant. — [Wikipedia, "Last verse harmonisation"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_verse_harmonisation)
- The historical titles show the same pairing: C. H. Lloyd, *Free Accompaniment of Unison Hymn Singing* (1919); Bairstow, *Organ Accompaniments to the Unison Verses of 24 Hymn-Tunes from The English Hymnal* (OUP 1941); Gerald H. Knight, *Accompaniments for Unison Hymn-Singing* (RSCM 1971). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_verse_harmonisation)

**Introductions**
- A hymn introduction "should help prepare the congregation to sing confidently." Simpler options: add one voice per phrase, alternate unison and harmony phrases, or solo the melody over simplified harmony. More complex options: soprano solo with alto and tenor on a second manual, a fanfare-like change, or added passing tones and suspensions. (Opinion: Landon Finch, independent organ-training course) — [Organ Training Course, Lesson 10](https://sites.google.com/view/organtrainingcourse/lessons/lesson-10-creative-hymn-playing)
- For a familiar tune, Brink recommends a partial introduction (first and last phrase, or the refrain) and criticises playing the whole hymn just to cue standing. If people stand at the start of the introduction, its final phrase may differ from the hymnal version. — [Reformed Worship, Brink](https://reformedworship.org/resource/i-sing-bass-organists-use-alternative-hymn-arrangements)

**Varied textures for inner stanzas (single-author pedagogy, Finch)**
- **Soprano (melody) solo:** melody alone on one manual (solo stop), alto and tenor on another manual, bass in the pedals. It can be played an octave lower for a softer sound, for a whole verse or part of one.
- **Tenor solo:** suits inner verses or a congregation that already knows the tune.
- **Alto up an octave:** suits a final verse or a build toward a climax. "Raising the alto creates a descant-like effect."
- **Soprano/tenor switch:** the tenor goes above the melody, which drops an octave. It "can sound like a free accompaniment". Finch calls it the hardest technique.
- **Manuals only:** pedals dropped (or manual-to-pedal coupling), giving "a lighter and more reflective effect".
- **Non-chord tones:** passing tones, neighbour tones and suspensions add motion and tension.
- **Pedal points:** usually on tonic or dominant. They work "before final cadences", in interludes, or to build anticipation in the last verse.
- Source for all of the above: [Organ Training Course, Lesson 10](https://sites.google.com/view/organtrainingcourse/lessons/lesson-10-creative-hymn-playing)
- BYU's Organ Tutor has hymn-registration and hymn-project pages, but their content was not read. — [BYU Organ Tutor, hymn registration](https://www.organ.byu.edu/organtutor/hymn_playing/registration/index.htm)

**Registration across stanzas (consensus at a general level)**
- Changing registration and volume, or soloing the melody, helps focus attention on the text. Do not use one combination for every stanza. — [Reformed Worship, Brink](https://reformedworship.org/resource/i-sing-bass-organists-use-alternative-hymn-arrangements)
- Last-verse registration usually favours stops that emphasise the bass, or that are loud, rich or "harsh". A tuba is often used with the pedals, and reeds are useful. Published collections may suggest stops, but the choice is the organist's. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_verse_harmonisation)
- Do not drown unison singing. Brink calls over-loud organ a main reason congregations resist alternative arrangements. — [Reformed Worship, Brink](https://reformedworship.org/resource/i-sing-bass-organists-use-alternative-hymn-arrangements)

**Interludes**
- Use interludes to build anticipation, prepare a final verse, create contrast, or cover a change of registration or key. Begin by interrupting the last chord of the verse, draw on motifs from the melody, and end the way the hymn ends. (Finch) — [Organ Training Course, Lesson 10](https://sites.google.com/view/organtrainingcourse/lessons/lesson-10-creative-hymn-playing)
- A modulating interlude should be "no longer than the hymn itself". Interludes give singers "a short 'breathing space'" and "must always be planned and used thoughtfully." (Jan Overduin) — [Reformed Worship, Overduin, "Giving a Hymn a Lift"](https://reformedworship.org/resource/giving-hymn-lift-when-should-organist-change-keys)

**Improvisation and printed settings**
- Experienced organists usually improvise the last verse. Beginners use hymnal or published settings. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_verse_harmonisation)
- AGO certification tests these skills. The Colleague (CAGO) exam includes task C8c, an improvised modulating bridge between two hymns in different keys. — [AGO / The American Organist, Gehring, "The Modulating Bridge," Oct 2013](https://wp.agohq.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/Modulating-Bridge.pdf)

**Placement in non-hymn liturgy (PCUSA)**
- At Communion, "extend the final refrain rather than repeating the entire hymn." At the offering, "use a simple vamp until the ushers return" (I–IV, I–vi or ii–V). Support spoken prayer with quiet textures. — [PCUSA Quicksheet](https://pcusa.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/Improvising%20%26%20Reharmonizing%20in%20Worship%20Quicksheet.pdf)

### Inferences
- A default stanza plan for an engine, synthesised from the sources above rather than prescribed by any one of them:
  - **Intro:** first and last phrase (or the refrain), melody soloed or in plain harmony, ending on a clear tonic or dominant.
  - **Stanza 1:** hymnal harmony, full registration.
  - **Inner stanzas:** hymnal harmony, varied by registration, a manual change, soprano or tenor solo, or manuals only for a reflective stanza, chosen to fit the text.
  - **Penultimate:** optionally lighter, to make room for the climax.
  - **Optional interlude or modulation**, no longer than one stanza.
  - **Final stanza:** unison melody, free harmony, pedal reeds, optional descant.
  - **Ending:** extended final cadence over a pedal point.
- Free harmony should come at most once or twice per hymn. Every source frames it as a special event, mostly for the last stanza.
- "Organists recommend it" (Wikipedia) and Brink's guidance are practitioner consensus, not empirical findings. I found no study measuring whether free accompaniments improve congregational singing.

### Gaps
- I could not read the RCO's Daniel Moult hymn-playing video (paywalled for non-members) — [RCO](https://i.rco.org.uk/?p=4648). Its stanza-by-stanza teaching is unknown.
- I found no open-access RSCM UK text on hymn playing. Only RSCM Australia's script was reachable.
- The requested books (Gerre Hancock, *Improvising: How to Master the Art*; Noble Cain; Wayne Leupold editions; Alan Wicks; Charles Callahan, *The Art of Hymn Playing*) were not available online. None of their content is cited here.
- No source gave a published rule on how often a stanza's registration should change, or on endings and codas beyond "pedal points before final cadences".

## 2. What are the conventions of carol and anthem organ parts (independence from the choir, figuration, building to the last verse)?

### Takeaway
The *Carols for Choirs* (Willcocks, Jacques, Rutter) tradition follows the hymn model at larger scale. Early verses are in choral harmony. Some verses are for choir alone or sections of it. The final verse has unison voices, an independent organ part with a chromatic bass, and a high treble descant. Willcocks' last verse of "O come, all ye faithful" opens with the melody in octaves across three registers, the lowest on the pedals. Published detail on how the organ parts are structured verse by verse is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Willcocks' setting of verse 7 of "Adeste Fideles" begins with the melody in unison at three octaves, the lowest on the pedals. After that the harmony departs from the standard setting. *Carols for Choirs* is named as a source of last-verse settings. — [Wikipedia, "Last verse harmonisation"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_verse_harmonisation)
- The *Carols for Choirs* volumes credit David Willcocks, John Rutter and Reginald Jacques most often. Entries marked "desc." include "It Came Upon the Midnight Clear" (vol. 2 no. 21) and "Hark! the Herald Angels Sing (2 verses)" (vol. 4 no. 17). The page does not define "desc.", though it presumably means a descant arrangement. — [Wikiwand, "Carols for Choirs"](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Carols_for_Choirs)
- The series began in 1961 under Willcocks and Jacques. Willcocks said the arrangements were "well within the range of the average parish church choir". The series brought the King's College arrangements "within the reach of ordinary parish choirs". — [Williams, *'What sweeter music'*, MA thesis, University of York, 2019, pp. 15, 30–31](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/24691/2/What%20sweeter%20music%20revised%20final.pdf)
- Descants entered the King's service gradually. Recordings of "O come, all ye faithful" show none in 1954, a transitional descant in 1958, and the complete descant by 1963. A 1987 listener complained of a "perfect rainstorm of descants!" June Boyce-Tillman called the descants "elitist", which the thesis author disputes. — [Williams thesis, pp. 7, 31, 39](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/24691/2/What%20sweeter%20music%20revised%20final.pdf)
- A descant needs harmony written to fit it. You can keep the original harmony or use an alternative written for the descant. Otherwise "the descant melody may clash with the harmony." — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_verse_harmonisation)
- Willcocks also edited *Hymns for Choirs* (OUP), which Brink lists as a source of alternative arrangements. Geoffrey Shaw's *The Descant Hymn-Tune Book* (Novello) has "optional descants". — [Reformed Worship, Brink](https://reformedworship.org/resource/i-sing-bass-organists-use-alternative-hymn-arrangements)
- Solo trumpet or tuba lines played by the left hand on a separate manual are a recognised last-verse device, for example EASTER HYMN, HYFRYDOL (last two lines) and ST DENIO. This is a hymn source, but the device is the one used in carol last verses. — [RSCM Australia, Nisbet](https://rscmaustralia.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Reharmonisation-of-last-verses-of-hymns-video-script-by-SN.pdf)

### Inferences
- A carol-arrangement template for the engine, inferred from the above:
  - **Intro:** from the last phrase, or a fanfare-like octave statement.
  - **Early verses:** SATB with organ supporting or doubling.
  - **Middle verse:** a choir-only or upper-voices verse with lighter or no organ.
  - **Last verse:** unison congregation and choir, treble descant above the tune, independent organ with a chromatic or stepwise bass, pedal reeds, and a broadened ending.
  - These steps are reconstructed from the hymn sources plus the one documented Adeste v7 detail, not from an analysis of the scores.
- Do not hard-code "descant on every carol". The King's history shows descants spreading over time and also drawing criticism.

### Gaps
- I found no source describing organ figuration in *Carols for Choirs* (for example, Rutter's ostinati or the Willcocks introductions), how independent the accompaniment is from the voices, or the OUP prefaces. The volumes are in copyright (OUP) and their prefaces are not online. The thesis's analytical chapters (pp. 67–119) were cut off in the fetched text.
- Anthem organ-part conventions (as opposed to hymns and carols) were not covered by any source found.

## 3. What harmonic vocabulary and voice-leading rules define a free (reharmonized) accompaniment, and how far does it depart from the hymnal harmony?

### Takeaway
The melody is fixed and everything below it may change. The bass line is planned first: a pedal point, a stepwise descending or ascending line, or a walking bass. The added vocabulary is secondary dominants (often over a chromatic bass), passing diminished chords, 7ths and 9ths, suspensions, relative-minor and major/minor swaps, inversions and the occasional distant excursion. A key rule: return "safely" to the home harmony, especially at the final cadence. Texture thickens to five or more notes, with pedal reeds.

### Cited Findings

**Process / voice leading**
- Nisbet's working order: put the melody on the middle staff, write the pedal (bass) next, then fill the inner parts to suit the new harmony. Build a continuous chord progression, not isolated "spiky" chords. Add passing notes and passing chords in later drafts to make chords "crunchy". Judge each draft at the organ by ear. — [RSCM Australia, Nisbet](https://rscmaustralia.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Reharmonisation-of-last-verses-of-hymns-video-script-by-SN.pdf)
- "Remember that when you go off into new harmonic territory you have to come back safely." — [RSCM Australia, Nisbet](https://rscmaustralia.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Reharmonisation-of-last-verses-of-hymns-video-script-by-SN.pdf)
- Plan the bass first ("sketch out a fundamental progression, working upward from the bass voice") and move the voices by step where possible. Double chord roots. In inversions, double tonal degrees (1, 4, 5), and never leading tones or dissonances. Strengthen cadences with 7–6 or 4–3 suspensions. (Thomas L. Read, written about modulation, but these are general voice-leading rules.) — [The Diapason, Read, "The modulating organist," 2022](https://www.thediapason.com/content/modulating-organist)

**Bass-line archetypes, with tune examples (Nisbet's catalogue)**
- **Sustained tonic pedal** with changing harmony above: SONG 34.
- **Sustained dominant pedal:** CAMBERWELL, SLANE, DOWN AMPNEY, NICAEA, AUSTRIA, LASST UNS ERFREUEN, MICHAEL, PADERBORN.
- **Descending pedal line:** NUN DANKET, ISTE CONFESSOR, Servant Song, DIADEMATA, ABERYSTWYTH, LOVE UNKNOWN, CRIMOND, WOODLANDS.
- **Ascending pedal line:** WARRINGTON, ANTIOCH, MCKEE, GERONTIUS, ELLACOMBE, DOMINUS REGIT ME, LAUDES DOMINI.
- **Walking (jazz) bass:** BATTLE HYMN.
- Source for all five: [RSCM Australia, Nisbet](https://rscmaustralia.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Reharmonisation-of-last-verses-of-hymns-video-script-by-SN.pdf)

**Melody treatments (Nisbet)**
- Opening melody notes in octaves, then "crunchy" chords (diminished, 9ths): ODE TO JOY, CHRISTE SANCTORUM, ABBOT'S LEIGH, HYFRYDOL, CHURCH TRIUMPHANT, MONMOUTH.
- Canon: WAREHAM.
- Left-hand trumpet or tuba solo: EASTER HYMN, HYFRYDOL, ST DENIO.
- Blues (flattened thirds, swing): CONVERSE.
- Source: [RSCM Australia, Nisbet](https://rscmaustralia.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Reharmonisation-of-last-verses-of-hymns-video-script-by-SN.pdf)

**Chromatic and borrowed devices (Nisbet)**
- Diminished chords and 9ths used for "delayed reharmonisation", for example a G diminished chord in line 2 of MACCABAEUS.
- Major/minor swaps: DUNEDIN.
- Starting in or moving to the relative minor: ST MAGNUS opens in B minor, ST COLUMBA in C minor, CARLISLE moves to F# minor.
- Tension released at the final cadence: WOODLANDS.
- Exercise: harmonise one melody note many ways. With G on top: G, Gm, G7, C, E♭, F9.
- Source: [RSCM Australia, Nisbet](https://rscmaustralia.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Reharmonisation-of-last-verses-of-hymns-video-script-by-SN.pdf)

**Overall character of a last verse**
- Fuller organ part, with lower bass notes on the pedalboard, "more secondary dominants (especially over chromatic bass lines)", and often five or more notes per chord. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_verse_harmonisation)
- PCUSA vocabulary list: secondary dominants, passing diminished chords, suspended chords, modal interchange, gospel harmony, ii–V. Use tritone substitution "sparingly". — [PCUSA Quicksheet](https://pcusa.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/Improvising%20%26%20Reharmonizing%20in%20Worship%20Quicksheet.pdf)
- Brink reports congregations complaining about "strange harmonies" that lose the bass singers, which is a reason to keep the departure bounded. — [Reformed Worship, Brink](https://reformedworship.org/resource/i-sing-bass-organists-use-alternative-hymn-arrangements)

### Inferences
- Constraints an engine could encode:
  1. The melody is invariant (octave doubling or light embellishment allowed).
  2. Choose one bass archetype per phrase: tonic pedal, dominant pedal, stepwise descent, stepwise ascent or walking.
  3. Harmonise each melody note from an expanded set: diatonic chords in any inversion, V/x (secondary dominants), vii°7/x, ii7, 9ths, sus4→3, iv or ♭VI borrowed from the parallel minor, and the relative minor at a phrase start.
  4. Cadences return to the hymnal's own cadence (or a decorated one: 4–3, 7–6, pedal point).
  5. The final cadence is diatonic and strong.
  6. Density is 4 voices on inner verses, 5–6 voices plus pedal on the last verse.
  7. Avoid isolated jarring chords. The progression should be smooth, mostly stepwise and continuous.
- How far it departs: in practitioner terms, mid-phrase harmony may differ "entirely" (Wikipedia) while cadence points and the melody hold. That makes a phrase-level metric possible, for example the share of beats re-harmonised versus cadential beats preserved. No source measures this.

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative study, for example the percentage of chords changed, the frequency of secondary dominants, or statistics on chord density in published free accompaniments. A corpus analysis (see Q4) would be needed.
- Eric Thiman's *The Varied Harmonisation of Hymn Tunes* (OUP 1934) is the classic treatise, but I could not read its text. Its rules are not cited here.

## 4. Are there public-domain collections of free accompaniments or descants that could serve as a corpus, with formats and licences?

### Takeaway
Some free-accompaniment collections exist as scanned PDFs on IMSLP. Most of the classic 20th-century ones (Thiman 1937, Coleman 1953, Bairstow 1941) are still in copyright in the US. Only Bairstow is PD in the EU (life+70). Hymnary.org has PD tunes in their standard harmonisations as PDF and MusicXML, and indexes many descant and free-harmonisation collections that are mostly commercial and in copyright. No ready-made, machine-readable, PD corpus of free accompaniments was found. Building one would need OMR (optical music recognition) of the PD scans.

### Cited Findings
- **Eric Thiman, *Varied Accompaniments to 34 Well-known Hymn Tunes*** (OUP 1937). IMSLP PDF, 18 pp. Tags: Non-PD Canada, Non-PD US, Non-PD EU, hosted on PML-Asia. — [IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Varied_Accompaniments_to_34_Well-known_Hymn_Tunes_(Thiman,_Eric))
- **Henry Coleman, *Varied Hymn Accompaniments*** (OUP 1953). Sixteen tunes with three settings each: HYFRYDOL, LOBE DEN HERREN, QUAM DILECTA, ST PETER, ABRIDGE, OLD HUNDREDTH, ST ANNE, EASTER ALLELUIA (LASST UNS ERFREUEN), ROCKINGHAM, RICHMOND, WILTSHIRE, MARTYRDOM, BRISTOL, GWALCHMAI, RHOSYMEDRE, SURREY. Manuals with optional pedal. PD in Canada and other life+50 countries, Non-PD US and EU. PDF, 32 pp. — [IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Varied_Hymn_Accompaniments_(Coleman,_Henry))
- **Edward C. Bairstow, *Organ Accompaniments to 24 Unison Hymn-Tune Verses*** (OUP 1941). Tunes include HELMSLEY, ADESTE FIDELES, STUTTGART, EBENEZER, LLANFAIR, ST GEORGE, HYFRYDOL, PICARDY, MILES LANE, OLD HUNDREDTH, RICHMOND, RHUDDLAN, ST ANNE, HANOVER, AURELIA, MONKLAND, LEONI and others. Marked "Public Domain" except Non-PD US. PDF, 44 pp. — [IMSLP](https://cn.imslp.org/wiki/Organ_Accompaniments_to_24_Unison_Hymn-Tune_Verses_(Bairstow,_Edward_Cuthbert))

- **T. Tertius Noble, *Free Organ Accompaniments to One Hundred Well-Known Hymn Tunes*** (J. Fischer & Bro.). Catalogued with 102 entries, for example ADESTE FIDELES, ALBANY, AURELIA and AUSTRIA. The publication year is not shown in the catalogue, and it is still sold commercially. — [BU Organ Library](https://www.bu.edu/phpbin/organ-library/collection.php?id=4598); [Sheet Music Plus](https://www.sheetmusicplus.com/look_inside?R=1557675)

  - Source: [Hymnary.org, OLD 100TH](https://hymnary.org/tune/old_100th)

  - David N. Johnson, Paul Ensrud and Dale Wood (Augsburg); Karen Keene (Flammer) — [Reformed Worship, Brink](https://reformedworship.org/resource/i-sing-bass-organists-use-alternative-hymn-arrangements)
- **A free-harmonisation volume from *The Diapason*** ("FMP Free Harmonizations Vol. 1, Voice & Organ", 2024) appears as a PDF on thediapason.com. Its licence and contents were not checked. — [The Diapason PDF](https://thediapason.com/sites/diapason/files/2024-09/FMPFreeHarmonizationsVol1Voice%26Organ2024s.pdf)

### Inferences
- These copyright inferences are my own and were not stated by any source.
  - **C. H. Lloyd (1919):** probably PD in the US, since works published before 1931 are PD there as of 2026. Lloyd died in 1919, so it is probably PD worldwide. It would be the cleanest PD free-accompaniment source if a scan can be found. It was not checked on IMSLP.
  - **Bairstow (died 1946):** PD in life+70 jurisdictions, but not the US.
  - **Thiman, Coleman, Knight and Noble:** treat as copyrighted for US use.
- Realistic corpus route:
  1. Use Hymnary or Cyber Hymnal MusicXML of PD hymnal harmonisations as the baseline ("hymnal harmony").
  2. OMR the IMSLP scans (Bairstow, and Coleman and Thiman only where the jurisdiction permits) to get paired settings of the same tunes. The tune overlap is high: OLD HUNDREDTH, ST ANNE, HYFRYDOL and RICHMOND appear in several collections.
  3. Compute how far each free setting departs from the hymnal setting.

## 5. How is a modulation up a step before the last verse usually managed (length, pivot chords)?

### Takeaway
A modulation is placed in a short interlude after the penultimate stanza. That interlude interrupts or extends the final chord, uses a motif from the tune, and is in the hymn's meter. It weakens the old key, establishes the new dominant (preferably preceded by its own applied dominant or diminished 7th), and stops cleanly so the congregation can breathe and enter. For a half step, which has no common chords in major, a German augmented-sixth chord is the standard quick route. For a whole step, use a circle-of-fifths or ii–V–I approach to the new key. The interlude is typically a few bars. The only explicit limit found is "no longer than the hymn itself".

### Cited Findings
- **When to modulate (Overduin):** suits long or very familiar hymns, since "Transposing breaks the monotony brought about by the feeling of being locked into one key". It especially suits the last stanza: "The last stanza especially can often be given appropriate emphasis when introduced by an interlude." Skip it if the singing is already robust. Ascension texts invite it. — [Reformed Worship, Overduin](https://reformedworship.org/resource/giving-hymn-lift-when-should-organist-change-keys)
- **How far:** "The effect of modulating up one-half step is more liberating than that of moving up a whole tone". Bach's Credo example moves by half steps (D minor → E♭ minor → E minor). For a short hymn, choose a "not too distant" key (Gounod: C → D). This is one author's preference (Overduin). — [Reformed Worship, Overduin](https://reformedworship.org/resource/giving-hymn-lift-when-should-organist-change-keys)
- **Length:** the interlude should be "no longer than the hymn itself". — [Reformed Worship, Overduin](https://reformedworship.org/resource/giving-hymn-lift-when-should-organist-change-keys)
- **Harmony (Thomas L. Read):**
  - A step up "can allow the congregation to 'catch its breath'".
  - A half step in major "is challenging as there are no chords common to both keys". "Using a German augmented-sixth chord is one of the quickest and simplest ways of moving to a key a half-step above."
  - "Dissolve or weaken the energy of the initial key before beginning to establish the new key."
  - Precede the new V with its applied dominant or a diminished 7th, so the arrival does not sound like a half cadence in some other key.
  - Common-tone and circle-of-fifths routes also work. Decorate the goal cadence with 7–6 or 4–3.
  - The models are by step, up or down, to suit hymn accompaniment.
  - Source: [The Diapason, Read, "The modulating organist," 27 Sept 2022](https://www.thediapason.com/content/modulating-organist)
- **AGO method for a modulating bridge (Philip Gehring, FAGO):**
  1. Chart the keys (circle of fifths).
  2. Think in the meter of the target hymn.
  3. Take a motif from the target hymn, usually its opening phrase.
  4. State it in the old key, restate it a 4th or 5th away, and arrive at the new key.
  5. Add a short coda (3 bars in his example).
  - Three voices are easier than four. Make a complete break before and after the bridge.
  - For distant keys, pivot on the doubly diminished 7th on #4 of the new key, or use an augmented 6th.
  - This is for bridges between two hymns, but it is the AGO-examined skill.
  - Source: [AGO / The American Organist, Gehring, Oct 2013](https://wp.agohq.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/Modulating-Bridge.pdf)
- A dominant 7th used to move to the next key appears among RSCM Australia's basic exercises. — [RSCM Australia, Nisbet](https://rscmaustralia.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Reharmonisation-of-last-verses-of-hymns-video-script-by-SN.pdf)
- Organists' interludes should interrupt the last chord of the verse, use the melody's motifs, and end like the hymn. Interludes can also cover a change of key. (Finch) — [Organ Training Course, Lesson 10](https://sites.google.com/view/organtrainingcourse/lessons/lesson-10-creative-hymn-playing)

### Inferences
- An engine rule set synthesised from Read, Gehring, Overduin and Finch:
  1. Extend or interrupt the final tonic of the penultimate stanza.
  2. Over 2–4 bars in the hymn's meter, quote the tune's first phrase or the last phrase of the intro.
  3. Weaken the old key: move to IV or ii, or use the tonic as a pivot.
  4. Pivot:
     - **Half step up (C → D♭):** in C, play the ♭VI chord A♭–C–E♭ and add G♭ (spelled F♯, that is the German sixth of C). Respell it as A♭7, which is V7 of D♭, and resolve it to D♭, optionally through the D♭ cadential 6/4. Another route treats C major as V of F minor (iii of D♭), then goes through B♭m (vi) or G♭ (IV) to A♭7, then D♭.
     - **Whole step up (C → D):** C acts as ♭VII of D. Go to E7 (V/V of D) or Em (ii of D), then A7, then D. ii–V or V/V–V both work, since the keys share chords (Em, G, Bm/B° area).
  5. Precede the new V with its applied dominant or vii°7, decorated with a 4–3 suspension.
  6. Break (rest or held V7) so singers can breathe.
  7. The final stanza enters in the new key.
  - These voicings are textbook applications of the cited principles. The specific chord spellings are mine and were not given by the sources.
- Overduin's preference for a half step conflicts with the common modern practice of moving up a whole step. I found no survey resolving which is more usual. Read's note that a half step "has no common chords" explains why whole-step lifts are simpler to improvise.

### Gaps
- No source gave a standard bar count for a pre-last-verse modulation beyond "no longer than the hymn". Read's notated examples (*Diapason* p. 14) were not available in the text.
- Published collections such as "Hymn Introductions and Modulations" ([Sheet Music Plus](https://www.sheetmusicplus.com/en/product/hymn-introductions-and-modulations-book-xii-20735435.html)) and The Salvation Army NZ's "3 easy ways to move between keys" ([salvationarmy.org.nz](https://salvationarmy.org.nz/church-community/creative-ministries/article/3-easy-ways-move-between-keys)) were found but not read.
- Endings and codas: the only rules found were a pedal point before the final cadence (Finch) and tension released at the final cadence (Nisbet, WOODLANDS). No source gave codified coda patterns such as a plagal Amen tag, a broadened last line, or a Tierce de Picardie for minor tunes.
