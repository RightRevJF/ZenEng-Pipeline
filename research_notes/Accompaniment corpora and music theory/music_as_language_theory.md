# Music as Language: Grammars, Schemata, Expectation and Statistical Learning, and the Tools a Songwriting Engine Could Apply

Scope note: these notes cover (1) generative/grammatical theories, (2) schema theory, (3) expectation and statistical learning, (4) music-language parallels and their limits, (5) popular-song theory, and (6) predictors of liking/quality. They are organized under the three key questions, with sub-headings per theory area. Everything in "Cited Findings" was verified against a fetched page or a search-result title in this session. Material known from background but not verified this session is quarantined in "Gaps" and labeled as such.

## Q1. Which theories have working, downloadable implementations, and what inputs do they need?

### Takeaway
The expectation/statistical-learning family is the most usable today: IDyOM (Common Lisp, with a pip-installable Python wrapper) and Harrison's `ppm` R package give per-note information content and entropy from symbolic sequences. Grammar-based harmony parsing has a real open implementation (Harasim's Julia chart parser, trained on iRealPro jazz chord sequences), voice-leading schema detection has open code and data (DCMLab, Mozart sonatas), and Kirlin's probabilistic Schenkerian model ships its data. GTTM analyzers (Hamanaka) exist and perform well in recent deep-learning versions, but I could not confirm a current public download.

### Cited Findings

**(3) Expectation / statistical learning: IDyOM and relatives**
- IDyOM is "written in Common Lisp by Marcus Pearce" and implements a multiple-viewpoint statistical modelling framework for auditory expectation; documentation covers installation, database management, viewpoints, and the ppm-star and resampling packages; Pearce's 2005 thesis is the reference shipped with it — [IDyOM wiki v20](https://code.soundsoftware.ac.uk/projects/idyom-project/wiki/Wiki/20)
- IDyOM's entry point is `idyom:idyom`, taking a dataset ID, target viewpoints and source viewpoints. Default output is mean information content (IC) for the dataset, per-composition mean IC, and per-note IC; results can be written to files with `:output-path` — [IDyOM wiki, Idyom page v58](https://code.soundsoftware.ac.uk/projects/idyom-project/wiki/Idyom/58)
- Viewpoints include e.g. `cpitch` (chromatic pitch) and `cpintfref` (interval from tonic); `:select` runs hill-climbing viewpoint selection. Short-term model (STM) trains only on the current piece; long-term model (LTM) trains on a pretraining corpus; `:ltm+` adds incremental learning of the test piece; default `:both+` combines STM with LTM+ — [IDyOM wiki, Idyom page v58](https://code.soundsoftware.ac.uk/projects/idyom-project/wiki/Idyom/58)
- IDyOM "generates conditional probability distributions" for each event plus related information-theoretic measures — [IDyOM GitHub wiki Home](https://github.com/mtpearce/idyom/wiki/Home/f1e836c5a2f2ddef668bc6b690afda2fb36ba602)
- The canonical repository is https://github.com/mtpearce/idyom/ (as linked by the Python wrapper) — [py2lispIDyOM docs](https://xinyiguan.github.io/py2lispIDyOM)
- py2lispIDyOM: open-source Python (3.9+, pip) interface that runs IDyOM, preprocesses/export data, and visualizes outputs (surprisal, entropy, piano-roll predictions), writing CSV and MAT result folders; it requires a local IDyOM install — [py2lispIDyOM docs](https://xinyiguan.github.io/py2lispIDyOM); peer-reviewed in JOSS — [JOSS paper page](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/3f2e7b31cf9ee2f96ba631ff2617f8c5)
- Release history (v1.0 2014 through v1.5 2018) announced on the AUDITORY list — [IDyOM v1.0](https://auditory.org/postings/2014/360.html), [IDyOM v1.5](https://auditory.org/postings/2018/449.html)
- Peter Harrison's `ppm` R package implements Prediction by Partial Matching (the variable-order Markov method underlying IDyOM) — [pmcharrison/ppm](https://rdrr.io/github/pmcharrison/ppm)

**(1) Generative grammars of harmony: Rohrmeier, Harasim**
- Rohrmeier's grammar theory of jazz harmony: chords form nested hierarchical dependencies whose syntactic structure "tightly corresponds with the functional interpretation"; core dependency types are prolongation and preparation; the grammar covers diatonic harmony, embedded modulation, borrowing, substitution, phrase relations and forms of standards; the Blues is treated as a plagal derivation from tonic; specified in enough detail for computational implementation. Published as Rohrmeier (2020), "The Syntax of Jazz Harmony: Diatonic Tonality, Phrase Structure, and Form," *Music Theory and Analysis* 7(1), 1–62 — [EPFL DCML page](https://www.epfl.ch/labs/dcml/a-grammar-theory-of-jazz-harmony/); open copy on [Zenodo 4618222](https://zenodo.org/records/4618222)
- Harasim, Rohrmeier & O'Donnell (ISMIR 2018) introduce Probabilistic Abstract Context-Free Grammars (PACFGs): categories are (scale degree, key) pairs and rules are partial functions, so probabilities are shared key-independently; bottom-up parsing-as-deduction; Bayesian learning (Dirichlet priors, stochastic variational Bayes) — [ISMIR 2018 paper](https://ismir2018.ismir.net/doc/pdfs/258_Paper.pdf)
- Input/corpus: iRealPro jazz standards, 1,173 chord sequences; 394 pieces of ≤40 chords considered, 357 parseable used for training; evaluated against 13 hand-annotated trees. Tree accuracy: random 15.35%, right-branching <10%, uniform prior 36.30%, trained PCFG 39.43%, trained PACFG 45.95% (PCFG has ~12x the free parameters) — [ISMIR 2018 paper](https://ismir2018.ismir.net/doc/pdfs/258_Paper.pdf)
- Code: Julia package https://github.com/dharasim/GeneralizedChartParsing.jl — [ISMIR 2018 paper](https://ismir2018.ismir.net/doc/pdfs/258_Paper.pdf)
- Related follow-ups exist (Harasim EPFL thesis; ISMIR 2020/2021 grammar papers; "Unsupervised Induction of Harmonic Syntax," BNAIC 2024) — [EPFL thesis record](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/entities/publication/e9989ecb-c65b-4caa-b230-1c8f40cbb8ed/thesisdetails), [ISMIR 2021 paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2021/paper/000071.pdf), [BNAIC 2024 PDF](https://bnaic2024.sites.uu.nl/wp-content/uploads/sites/986/2024/11/Unsupervised-Induction-of-Harmonic-Syntax.pdf) (titles/records only; contents not fetched)

**(1) GTTM computer implementations (Hamanaka, Hirata, Tojo)**
- ATTA: "Automatic Time-Span Tree Analyzer Based on Extended GTTM" (ISMIR 2005) — [ISMIR 2005 record](https://ir.webis.de/anthology/2005.ismir_conference-2005.50)
- A "Musical Structural Analysis Database Based on GTTM" was presented at ISMIR 2014 (pp. 325–330) — [ISMIR 2014 record](https://ir.webis.de/anthology/2014.ismir_conference-2014.53)
- deepGTTM-IV (CMMR 2023): Transformer-learned "stepwise reduction" extended so each removed note is linked to the note that absorbs it, with a matrix tree representation, producing full time-span trees; earlier analyzers "mostly performed very poorly" or needed manual parameter tuning; correct time-span trees for 29 of 30 pieces. Paper is CC BY 4.0 on Zenodo — [Zenodo 10114163](https://zenodo.org/records/10114163)

**(1) Schenkerian computational models**
- Kirlin's dissertation: a probabilistic model of Schenkerian analysis with parameters learned from a corpus and an algorithm for the most probable analysis; analyses encode prolongations as X (Y) Z; corpus of 41 common-practice major-key keyboard excerpts, each with MusicXML plus analysis file, described as "the first publicly available data set" of computer-readable Schenkerian analyses; human judges rated algorithmic analyses about one letter grade below a professional's. Data downloadable (dissdata.zip / .tar.gz); source code not listed on the page — [Kirlin dissertation page](https://www.cs.rhodes.edu/~kirlinp/diss.html)
- Follow-up: "Learning to Uncover Deep Musical Structure" (AAAI 2015) — [AAAI](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/9476)

**(1) Lerdahl's Tonal Pitch Space, implemented as a chord-sequence distance**
- De Haas, Veltkamp & Wiering's Tonal Pitch Step Distance (TPSD) is a chord-sequence similarity measure built on Lerdahl's Tonal Pitch Space chord distance — [de Haas et al. 2008 PDF](https://zenodo.org/records/1418069/files/HaasVW08.pdf); a third-party TPSD implementation is mirrored on GitHub (andreamust/TPSD) — [mirror](https://github.imc.re/andreamust/TPSD) (not inspected)

**(2) Schema detection**
- Finkensiep, Déguernel, Neuwirth & Rohrmeier (ISMIR 2020), "Voice-Leading Schema Recognition Using Rhythm and Pitch Features": generalized skipgrams enumerate key-independent note configurations matching a schema's interval pattern; logistic regression classifies true instances using regularity, complexity, salience and harmonic context features; two-voice variants only — [ISMIR 2020 paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2020/paper/000124.pdf)
- Corpus/data: 18 Mozart piano sonatas (54 movements, ~103,800 notes); 244 expert-annotated true instances vs ~191,000 false candidates, 10 schema types/20 subtypes. Data: https://github.com/DCMLab/schema_annotation_data; code: https://github.com/DCMLab/schemata_code/tree/ismir2020 — [ISMIR 2020 paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2020/paper/000124.pdf)
- Results: F = 0.894, MCC = 0.787 on balanced data, but precision ~0.01 on raw unbalanced data; schemata tend to have regular rhythm and low complexity, with metric regularity a strong positive factor; main limitation is judging each candidate in isolation, whereas experts reject matches because a better alternative analysis exists — [ISMIR 2020 paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2020/paper/000124.pdf)

**(5)/(6) Pop-song feature toolkits**
- `catchy` ("Corpus Analysis Tools for Computational Hook discovery," Van Balen) is on PyPI — [catchy-toolbox on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/catchy-toolbox); used in the Hooked! study alongside 58 symbolic FANTASTIC descriptors for melodies and bass lines — [Van Balen et al., ISMIR 2015](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/17202/1/Vanbalenetal_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- The de Clercq–Temperley Rock Corpus distribution includes the custom scripts used to extract data and aggregate statistics, under CC BY 4.0 — [Rock Corpus site](https://rockcorpus.midside.com/)
- The McGill Billboard dataset has a loader in `mirdata` — [mirdata billboard module](https://mirdata.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_modules/mirdata/datasets/billboard.html)

### Inferences
- For a songwriting engine the lowest-friction path is: export generated melodies/chord lines as symbolic sequences, score them with IDyOM (via py2lispIDyOM) or `ppm` (pure R, no Lisp), pretrained on a pop corpus (Rock Corpus melodies; Billboard chords). IDyOM's STM/LTM split maps naturally onto "within-song repetition" vs. "style familiarity."
- Rohrmeier/Harasim grammars are the best-developed route to a "syntactic well-formedness" check of chord progressions, but the trained model is jazz-specific (iRealPro). Applying it to pop would require retraining or rule edits (pop's plagal/bVII-heavy syntax differs; see Q2/Q3).
- The schema-detector precision problem (~0.01 on unbalanced data) means a naive detector would hallucinate schemata in generated music; for a judge, schema matching should be restricted to known slot positions (phrase starts/cadences) or done at the chord-symbol level rather than note level.
- GTTM analyzers are attractive for a "hierarchical coherence" score of melodies, but availability is the bottleneck.

## Q2. Which measures could serve as a judge for generated melodies, harmonies and accompaniments, and what evidence supports them?

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced judge is expectation-based: IC (surprise) and entropy (uncertainty) jointly predict pleasure, in an interaction rather than a single "sweet spot" of IC, and preference for predictability follows an inverted U. For pop specifically, conventionality and repetition of melody (plus vocal prominence) predict how catchy/recognizable a section is, chorus repetition predicts chart success, and choruses differ from verses mainly in timbre, register and compressed dynamics. Syntactic well-formedness and schema conformance are theoretically motivated but have little direct evidence linking them to listener liking; the neural evidence for shared music-language syntax is contested.

### Cited Findings

**(6) Information content and entropy as liking predictors**
- Cheung, Harrison, Meyer, Pearce, Haynes & Koelsch (2019, *Current Biology*): a statistical-learning model quantified the uncertainty and surprise of ~80,000 chords from US Billboard pop songs; pleasure depended nonlinearly on their interaction: high pleasure for low uncertainty + high surprise, and for high uncertainty + low surprise; amygdala, hippocampus and auditory cortex reflected the interaction; nucleus accumbens reflected uncertainty only — [Cambridge Faculty of Music summary](https://cms.mus.cam.ac.uk/publications/cheung-uncertainty-surprise/)
- Same study: 745 classic Billboard songs; chords were isolated from melody and lyrics so songs were unrecognizable; pleasure arises from "the dynamic interplay between two temporally dissociable aspects of expectations" (anticipation before, surprise after); researchers suggest applications to computer composition and hit prediction — [Max Planck CBS press page](https://www.cbs.mpg.de/music-pleasure-in-pop-songs)
- Mas-Herrero et al. (2025, appears to be *PNAS* per DOI link): "an inverted U-shaped relationship between predictability and preference"; "smaller surprises preferred in low-entropy melodies and larger surprises favored in high-entropy music"; computational models "accurately predicted the types of music people like" — [MindBlog summary](https://mindblog.dericbownds.net/2025/08/predictability-and-pleasure-of-music.html) (secondary source; primary not fetched)
- Gold, Pearce, Mas-Herrero, Dagher & Zatorre (2019, *J Neurosci*), "Predictability and uncertainty in the pleasure of music: a reward for learning?" — [PMC6867811](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867811) (page blocked by captcha; content not verified, see Gaps)

**(5)/(6) Repetition, conventionality, hooks**
- Hooked! study (Van Balen, Burgoyne, Bountouridis, Müllensiefen, Veltkamp; ISMIR 2015): 1,715 segments from 321 songs, 973 participants; recognizability (drift rate) predicted mostly by *conventionality* (sounding typical of the corpus); strongest audio component "Vocal Prominence" (β ≈ 0.14), then timbral conventionality (≈0.09), melodic conventionality (≈0.06); symbolic: melodic repetitivity (β ≈ 0.12) and melody/bass conventionality (≈0.07), i.e., recognizable melodies are more repetitive and use more typical motives — [Van Balen et al. 2015](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/17202/1/Vanbalenetal_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- Same study: marginal R² only 0.06 (audio), 0.07 (symbolic), 0.10 combined, while conditional R² ≈ 0.47, so most variance is between songs, not explained by the features — [Van Balen et al. 2015](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/17202/1/Vanbalenetal_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- Chorus features (Van Balen, Burgoyne et al., ISMIR 2013; McGill Billboard, 649 songs, 6,462 sections): choruses are sharper, rougher, higher and more salient in pitch, more varied in MFCC timbre, with smaller dynamic range (loudness IQR coefficient −0.33); section length, position and chroma (harmonic) variance had no direct relation to "chorusness"; 1-NN classifier F = 0.52 vs 0.36 baseline — [ISMIR 2013 paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000180.pdf)
- Nunes, Ordanini & Valsesia (*Journal of Consumer Psychology*): Billboard Hot 100, 1958–2012; 1,029 No. 1 songs vs 1,451 songs never above #90; each additional chorus repetition raised odds of reaching No. 1 by 14.5%; more word repetition raised Top-10 odds and speed of ascent, but excessive word repetition slowed ascent (ceiling); explanation offered: processing fluency — [Pacific Standard summary](https://psmag.com/?p=30843) (secondary)

**(5) Form theory usable as structural constraints**
- Nobile: verse–prechorus–chorus form follows a "telos principle": verse = initiation, prechorus = buildup, chorus = arrival, where "the goal is the *beginning* of the chorus" and then sustained as a plateau; 1960s–80s realized harmonically as T–PD–D–T (verse tonic, prechorus to PD then D, chorus arrives on tonic), prechoruses fragment verse phrases, telos choruses often begin with anacrusis to 1̂; 1991–2010 texture/timbre/voice take over and chord loops become the most common tonic-prolonging technique; 2010–2020 "anti-telos choruses" and EDM drops, which still express the telos principle — [Nobile, MTO, "Teleology in Verse–Prechorus–Chorus Form, 1965–2020"](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/864/206)
- Nobile's evidence is close analysis, not a systematic corpus; one quantitative point (via Summach): 1980s Billboard annual Top 20 had more VPC songs than verse–chorus without prechorus — [Nobile, MTO](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/864/206); book: *Form as Harmony in Rock Music* (OUP 2020) — [MTO review](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.1/mto.22.28.1.barna.html)

**(2)/(5) Pop schemata usable for schema-conformance scoring**
- Doll, *Hearing Harmony* (2017): schemas are recurring chord patterns written root-wise in angle brackets (e.g., <II–V–I>), including two-, three-, four-chord "slot" schemas (e.g., "La Bamba" C–F–G–F, "doo-wop" C–Am–F–G, "axis" Am–F–C–G), pentatonic schemas, meta-schemas (e.g., 5–♯5–6 harmonized as I–III–vi, I–♭VI–IV, I–I+–IV), and ten extended schemas (twelve-bar blues etc.); functions are positional (alpha tonic, beta pre-tonic, gamma pre-pretonic) — [de Clercq review, MTO 24.1](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.18.24.1/mto.18.24.1.declercq.php)
- De Clercq's critique: Doll's frequency claims for schemas rest on intuition, not a corpus study — [de Clercq review](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.18.24.1/mto.18.24.1.declercq.php)
- Richards (MTO 2017): the four rotations of Am–F–C–G ("Axis-a, -F, -C, -G"); the a-form is tonally ambiguous between vi–IV–I–V and i–♭VI–♭III–♭VII, listeners show a "major-mode bias," and tonality "depends primarily on the melodic content," via harmonic, temporal, rhetorical, contextual and poetic emphasis; in Billboard Year-End Hot 100 1990–2016 (2,715 songs) the C-form was usually most common, the a-form overtook it ~2009–2014, the G-form appeared in only 10 songs — [Richards, MTO](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/308/110)

**(5) Melody–harmony relations in pop (constraints for a melody-over-chords judge)**
- Temperley & de Clercq (2013, *JNMR* 42(3)): in rock, melody favors ♭7 over 7, whereas harmony favors 7; ♭2 and ♯4 are rarest (supporting a "supermode" of all degrees except ♭2/♯4); of 194 songs, 41 are minor melodically but major harmonically vs 9 the reverse (e.g., "Satisfaction," "Respect"); "pentatonic union" (1–2–♭3–3–4–5–6–♭7) is the second most common scale set; combined melodic+root key profiles found the key in 97/100 test songs; rock shows "temporal symmetry," lacking common-practice directional tendencies — [Temperley & de Clercq 2013](https://www.midside.com/publications/temperley_declercq_2013.pdf)

**(4) Music–language parallels and their limits**
- Patel's shared syntactic integration resource hypothesis (SSIRH, 2003) is cited as one of the sources for the view that "abstract syntactic structure building is supported by a shared brain network" in inferior frontal cortex — [Shain, Kean, ... Fedorenko 2024, J Cogn Neurosci](https://www.mit.edu/~hopekean/files/jocn_Shain_Kean.pdf)
- Counter-evidence (Fedorenko-lab bioRxiv preprint): individually defined language regions respond weakly to music (at or below fixation; below nonwords); no consistent sensitivity to music structure (intact vs scrambled melodies/drum patterns); songs not above spoken lyrics; three people with severe aphasia judged melodic well-formedness at or above control level; authors conclude "a critical role of the language system in music structure processing is unlikely" and that any shared resources more likely lie in domain-general or auditory systems — [bioRxiv 10.1101/2021.06.01.446439](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.06.01.446439.full)
- Behavioral interference studies continue to report language-harmony interactions "beyond attention" — [PMC4785990 title](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4785990) (content blocked by captcha)

### Inferences
- A defensible expectation judge would compute per-event IC and entropy for melody (pitch + onset viewpoints) and for chord sequence, then reward the *interaction* pattern reported by Cheung et al. (surprising events placed where context is predictable; conventional resolutions where context is uncertain), and penalize globally extreme mean IC (inverted U, Mas-Herrero). A single global IC "target range" is less supported than the interaction; any numeric target would have to be calibrated on the engine's own pop training corpus, since IC values depend on model, viewpoints and training set.
- Conventionality and repetition evidence (Hooked!, Nunes et al.) argues for a chorus judge that rewards: repetitive, typical melodic motives; within-song recurrence; multiple chorus repetitions; and (in production/arrangement) vocal prominence, higher register, and compressed dynamics in choruses. Harmony variance is not what distinguishes choruses.
- Nobile's telos principle gives a concrete form check: chorus onset should be the energy/arrival point (tonic arrival, register/texture lift), prechorus should build (phrase fragmentation, PD→D or textural ramp), unless intentionally anti-telos.
- Schema conformance for pop should be at the chord-root level against Doll/Richards loops, with the melody deciding mode for Axis-type loops (Richards). Rock syntax is fourth-symmetric and IV-heavy, so a common-practice "V→I required" grammar would wrongly penalize idiomatic rock (see Q3 data).
- Given the contested SSIRH, "music is a language" should be treated as a useful engineering metaphor (hierarchical grammars, statistical prediction) rather than a claim that linguistic well-formedness equals musical quality.
- Effect sizes are small (Hooked! marginal R² ≤ 0.10): these measures can filter bad output and nudge toward the stylistic center, but cannot reliably identify great songs.

- Gold et al. 2019 (J Neurosci) content could not be read (captcha); background knowledge says it found inverted-U liking over IC and entropy for artificial melodies, unverified here.
- Mas-Herrero et al. 2025 was read only via a blog summary; primary paper details (stimuli, N, model) unverified.
- No study found that directly validates grammar-parse probability (Rohrmeier/Harasim) or GTTM tree well-formedness as a predictor of listener liking.
- Lyric–melody relations (stress alignment, syllable setting) were not researched in depth; the Rock Corpus includes stress-aligned lyrics for a subset (see Q3), but no findings were retrieved.

These are the theoretical backbone but were not fetched; cite with care.

## Q3. What corpora underlie these theories, and are they available?

### Takeaway
Pop-relevant corpora are available and openly licensed: the de Clercq–Temperley Rock Corpus (200 RS-500 songs, harmony + melody + timing, CC BY 4.0) and the McGill Billboard dataset (890 chart slots/740 songs, chord + structure annotations, open access). Grammar and schema research relies on iRealPro jazz, Mozart sonata schema annotations (DCMLab GitHub), and Kirlin's 41-excerpt Schenkerian set. Billboard-based studies (Cheung et al., Nunes et al., Richards) show how these are used for liking and hit research.

### Cited Findings
- **Rock Corpus (de Clercq, Temperley, Lustig, Tan):** based on Rolling Stone's "500 Greatest Songs"; RS 200 (200 songs) grown from RS 5x20 (top 20 per decade 1950s–1990s); harmonic analyses by both authors, melodic transcriptions, timing data referenced to original audio, lyrics and syllabic stress aligned to melodies for a subset; versions 1.0–2.1 as zip files; CC BY 4.0 — [Rock Corpus site](https://rockcorpus.midside.com/)
- Encoding: key in brackets, Roman numerals in bar-delimited measures, recursive CFG-like notation with named sections ($Vr, $Br, `*n` repetition), expanded by a program into explicit chord lists with time points and roots — [de Clercq & Temperley 2011](https://rockcorpus.midside.com/2011_paper/declercq_temperley_2011.pdf)
- 2011 findings (99 songs): I 32.8%, IV 22.6%, V 16.3%, ♭VII 8.1%, VI 7.2%, II 3.6% (top five = 87%); IV→I 1,162 vs V→I 788; ascending and descending fourths nearly equal (2,266 vs 2,220); top tonic-ending trigrams IV–V–I 352, V–IV–I 292, ♭VII–IV–I 146, VI–IV–I 126, ♭VII–♭VI–I 103; 1950s I/IV/V >95%; ♭VI rises from 0.3% (1960s) to 6.2% (1990s); 75.8% major chords, 94.1% root position — [de Clercq & Temperley 2011](https://rockcorpus.midside.com/2011_paper/declercq_temperley_2011.pdf); published in *Popular Music* — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/popular-music/article/corpus-analysis-of-rock-harmony/C5210A8EC985DDF170B53124F4464DA4)
- Inter-analyst agreement: 92.4% chromatic relative root, 97.3% key (2011); 93.3% harmonic agreement and melodic transcription F = 0.893 (2013) — [2011 paper](https://rockcorpus.midside.com/2011_paper/declercq_temperley_2011.pdf), [2013 paper](https://www.midside.com/publications/temperley_declercq_2013.pdf)
- **McGill Billboard:** annotations and audio features for 890 chart slots / 740 distinct songs (first 1,000 of a random chart-slot sample from ISMIR 2011 plus 300 for MIREX 2012), CSV/TXT, open access; homepage https://ddmal.music.mcgill.ca/research/The_McGill_Billboard_Project_(Chord_Analysis_Dataset)/ — [MusoW entry](https://projects.dharc.unibo.it/musow/view-1645596007-813033); v1.2 (649 Hot 100 songs, 1958–1991, 7,762 sections) used for chorus analysis — [ISMIR 2013](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000180.pdf); loader in mirdata — [mirdata](https://mirdata.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_modules/mirdata/datasets/billboard.html)
- **Billboard chords for pleasure study:** ~80,000 chords from 745 songs — [MPI CBS](https://www.cbs.mpg.de/music-pleasure-in-pop-songs)
- **iRealPro jazz standards:** 1,173 chord sequences used to train PACFG harmony grammar — [Harasim et al. 2018](https://ismir2018.ismir.net/doc/pdfs/258_Paper.pdf)
- **DCMLab schema annotations (Mozart piano sonatas):** https://github.com/DCMLab/schema_annotation_data — [Finkensiep et al. 2020](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2020/paper/000124.pdf)
- **Kirlin Schenkerian corpus:** 41 excerpts, MusicXML + analyses, downloadable — [Kirlin](https://www.cs.rhodes.edu/~kirlinp/diss.html)
- **Hooked! data:** 1,715 segments, 321 songs, 973 participants (collection-level; availability not stated in the fetched text) — [Van Balen et al. 2015](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/17202/1/Vanbalenetal_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- **Billboard Year-End Hot 100 1990–2016** (2,715 songs) used for Axis counts (Richards' own tally, not a released dataset) — [Richards, MTO](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/308/110)

### Inferences
- For a pop songwriting engine, the Rock Corpus is the single best training/calibration set because it pairs melody with harmony and timing (melody–harmony judges, IDyOM pretraining) and is CC BY 4.0; McGill Billboard adds chord + section structure for form and chorus statistics.
- Rock Corpus chord statistics (IV as the main pre-tonic chord, symmetric fourth motion, ♭VII and VI common) should define the "syntax" prior instead of common-practice rules; jazz-trained grammars would need retraining on these chord strings, and the Rock Corpus encoding is already CFG-like, which eases grammar induction.
- Galant schema data (Mozart) is relevant mainly as method; pop loops (Doll, Richards) have no annotated corpus, so the engine would need its own loop lexicon validated against Billboard/Rock Corpus counts.
