## 1. Mechanisms: Juslin's BRECVEMA framework, and which mechanisms can be designed into notes and arrangement

### Takeaway
BRECVEMA lists eight routes from sound to felt emotion. Four of them (brainstem reflex, rhythmic entrainment, emotional contagion, musical expectancy) live inside the score and arrangement and can be designed directly. Aesthetic judgement can be designed for in part. Visual imagery can be steered through lyrics and tone-painting. Evaluative conditioning and episodic memory mostly depend on the listener's history and can only be reached indirectly, for example through genre and era idioms that carry shared associations.

### Cited Findings
- Juslin's 2013 *Physics of Life Reviews* target article, "From everyday emotions to aesthetic emotions: Towards a unified theory of musical emotions" (PLR 10(3):235–266), adds **aesthetic judgement** to the earlier seven-mechanism BRECVEM model, which gives BRECVEMA — [ADS record](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhLRv..10..235J) [summary: record title/venue confirmed; mechanism list from prior]. The earlier BRECVEM model is Juslin & Västfjäll 2008, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 31:559–575 — [doi:10.1017/S0140525X08005293](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X08005293) [prior].
- The eight mechanisms, with how a design engine could act on each [prior, standard reading of Juslin 2013]:
  - **Brainstem reflex.** Fast, hard-wired reactions to sudden, loud, dissonant, very low or very high, or fast-changing sounds. Designable: sudden dynamic jumps, accents, roughness/distortion, sub-bass hits, abrupt silences.
  - **Rhythmic entrainment.** Body rhythms (heart rate, breathing, motor) lock to a strong external pulse. Designable: tempo, pulse clarity, groove/syncopation level. Groove is an inverted U in syncopation: medium syncopation gives the most wanting-to-move and pleasure (Witek et al. 2014, *PLOS ONE* 9(4):e94446, [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0094446](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094446)) [prior].
  - **Evaluative conditioning.** A piece becomes tied to a feeling through repeated pairing with good or bad events. Not designable in the notes, except by drawing on idioms with widely shared associations.
  - **Emotional contagion.** Listeners internally "mimic" the voice-like emotional expression in music. Designable: the Juslin & Laukka cue profiles (Section 3), especially in the lead vocal or solo line.
  - **Visual imagery.** Music prompts inner images. Partly designable through lyrics, programmatic arranging, and wide-register "landscape" textures.
  - **Episodic memory.** Music triggers personal memories. Not designable for one person; nostalgia idioms work at the population level.
  - **Musical expectancy.** Emotion arises when expectations built from learned style are confirmed, delayed, or broken. This is the most designable mechanism and the one most tied to chills (Sections 2 and 5).
  - **Aesthetic judgement.** Emotion comes from judging the music's value (beauty, skill, novelty, expressivity). Partly designable through craft quality and originality.
- Juslin & Laukka's meta-analysis is cited in the music-emotion literature as covering **104 speech studies and 41 music studies**, and it concluded that "similar changes in acoustic features occurred when conveying similar emotions in these domains," which it framed as a possible "common code." This is the empirical basis for the contagion mechanism — [Quinto, Thompson & Keating 2013, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00184/full) [summary].
- A field experience-sampling study (Juslin, Liljeström, Västfjäll, Barradas & Silva 2008, *Emotion* 8(5):668–683, [doi:10.1037/a0013505](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0013505)) estimated which mechanisms were active in everyday listening. In the researcher's recollection, contagion, brainstem reflex, and episodic memory were among the most often reported, and musical expectancy among the least [prior, UNVERIFIED ranking].

### Inferences
- An engine can turn five mechanisms into explicit design levers: brainstem reflex (dynamics, timbre, onsets), entrainment (tempo, groove), contagion (cue profiles), expectancy (melodic, harmonic, and formal surprise against a learned style model), and aesthetic judgement (craft and novelty). Imagery is a lyric-side lever. Conditioning and memory are outside the notes.
- The YouTube study's "loved moments" (melodic thinning, then the voice re-entering on a surprising note) use at least three mechanisms in one gesture: a brainstem/textural contrast (thin to full), contagion (a human voice returning), and expectancy (the surprising note). Stacking mechanisms at one moment is plausibly why such moments stand out. This is the researcher's inference, not a tested claim.

### Gaps
- The Juslin 2013 full text was not re-read this session. Its proposed developmental order, brain substrates, and "induction speed" for each mechanism are not quoted here.
- No study found that measures the separate contribution of each BRECVEMA mechanism to chills for a fixed set of songs. The mechanisms are a theoretical framework with only partial direct tests.

## 2. Chills/frisson and tears: what triggers them, their physiology, and their dopamine signature

### Takeaway
Chills cluster at moments of **increase and arrival**: crescendos, entry of a new voice or solo instrument, register expansion, and new or unprepared harmony. Tears cluster at **melodic appoggiaturas and sequences**, which build sad, tender tension. The pleasure comes from a two-stage dopamine process: caudate during the roughly 15 s of anticipation, then nucleus accumbens at the peak. Effects are real but modest in size, vary a lot from person to person, and removing a chill passage does not reliably abolish chills.

### Cited Findings
**Musical triggers (classic studies)**
- **Sloboda 1991** (*Psychology of Music* 19(2):110–120, [doi:10.1177/0305735691192002](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735691192002)): shivers are linked to "new or unprepared harmony, and changes in texture and dynamics" — as quoted in [Bannister & Eerola 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full) [summary]. The tears/lump-in-throat link to **melodic appoggiaturas** and **melodic sequences**, and the racing-heart link to syncopation and accelerating tempo, are the widely cited Sloboda results [prior; Sloboda's own tables not re-read; his sample was 83 listeners self-reporting passages, per prior knowledge, UNVERIFIED].
- **Panksepp 1995** (*Music Perception* 13(2):171–207, [doi:10.2307/40285693](https://doi.org/10.2307/40285693)): chills often occurred during a crescendo in one Pink Floyd piece. In other pieces chills were spread evenly or occurred at moments with no distinct features — [Bannister & Eerola 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full) [summary]. Panksepp tied chills more to sadness, though both happy and sad music elicited them — [de Fleurian & Pearce 2021, i-Perception](https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/0695c63ed1e5b482bf323b2bf1d15ab4/PMC8323431.pdf) [summary].
- **Grewe et al. 2007** (*Music Perception* 24(3):297–314, [doi:10.1525/mp.2007.24.3.297](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2007.24.3.297)): chills were linked to dynamic changes and "the entrance of new voices or instruments," with "an increase in loudness and sharpness" during chills — [Bannister & Eerola 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full) [summary]. Grewe et al. also stressed that chills are not reflexive. The same passage did not reliably chill the same listener every time, and familiarity and attention mattered [prior].
- **Guhn, Hamm & Zentner 2007** (*Music Perception* 24(5):473–484, [doi:10.1525/mp.2007.24.5.473](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2007.24.5.473)): chills correlated with "interactions between solo and accompaniment instruments," and some chill passages showed an expanded overall frequency range (register expansion) — [Bannister & Eerola 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full) [summary]. Further results from prior knowledge, not re-read: chill passages also involved sudden or gradual volume increases (crescendo) and departures from the expected harmonic progression, and chill moments coincided with rises in skin conductance and heart rate [prior].
- Summing up the literature, the reported chill elicitors are "sudden dynamic changes, increased roughness, crescendi, and the entry of new instruments" — [de Fleurian & Pearce 2021, i-Perception](https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/0695c63ed1e5b482bf323b2bf1d15ab4/PMC8323431.pdf) [read]. Behavioural chills studies used only 3 to 23 stimuli each — same source [read]. This small-stimulus base is a key limitation.

**Systematic review**
- **de Fleurian & Pearce 2021, "Chills in music: A systematic review,"** *Psychological Bulletin* 147(9):890–920, [doi:10.1037/bul0000341](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000341) — [Altmetric record](https://www.altmetric.com/details/3018013/citations). The full text could not be fetched. From prior knowledge [UNVERIFIED]: the review covers on the order of 100+ articles; it finds wide variation in how chills are defined and measured; it lists commonly reported musical correlates (crescendos, new instruments or voices, unexpected harmonies, high register, peaks in loudness and roughness); and it notes person factors (openness to experience, musical training and engagement, and familiarity, which tends to raise chill likelihood). It concludes that the evidence is fragmented and stimuli are limited.

**Corpus test of chill-inducing tracks (de Fleurian & Pearce 2021, i-Perception 12(4)) [read]**
- 722 chill tracks (from the 988-mention ChiM dataset) were matched to Spotify controls by artist, duration, and popularity — [source](https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/0695c63ed1e5b482bf323b2bf1d15ab4/PMC8323431.pdf).
- Chill tracks had **slightly lower valence**: a difference of 0.033 on a 0–1 scale (b = 0.54, Z = 2.51, p = .012; Nagelkerke R² = .006), averaging 0.042 across 10 matching iterations. Chill tracks were also more popular, by 2.90/100 points — same source.
- Chill tracks loaded lower on a component combining energy, loudness, valence, danceability, and tempo, and higher on acousticness and instrumentalness. That is, they were **slower, quieter, less intense, more acoustic and instrumental** at the whole-track level. The authors call these effects small and note that chills are local events that track-level features smooth over — same source.

**Manipulation test (Bannister & Eerola 2018, Frontiers in Psychology 9:2046) [read]**
- N = 24 frequent chill-experiencers. The chill section was cut out of Sigur Rós *Glósóli* (crescendo to a distorted-guitar and drum climax), Holst *Jupiter* (thematic string progression), and Steven Wilson *Ancestral* (guitar solo) — [source](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full).
- Chill reports fell from 30 to 21 overall (Glósóli 10→7, Jupiter 10→8, Ancestral 10→6), about 30%. The overall McNemar test was only marginal (χ² = 3.85, p = .049, φ = 0.23), and no single piece differed significantly. Continuous intensity was higher in chill sections. Skin conductance did not differ significantly between the chill and removed versions — same source.
- Implication from the authors' framing: chills depend on the **build-up context** as well as the peak passage. Listeners still got chills elsewhere once the "trigger" was gone.

**Chills vs. tears physiology**
- **Mori & Iwanaga 2017**, "Two types of peak emotional responses to music: The psychophysiology of chills and tears," *Scientific Reports* 7:46063 — [PMC record](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5384201/) (not fetchable). From prior knowledge [UNVERIFIED details]: listeners heard self-selected and experimenter-selected songs. Chills and tears seldom co-occurred. **Chills** showed a pattern of physiological arousal (skin conductance up, faster breathing). **Tears** showed a mix of arousal and calming (for example, slower or deeper breathing and a heart-rate pattern different from chills). Tear songs were felt as sad-and-pleasurable or "moving," while chill songs were more often felt as happy or exciting.
- A BPS Research Digest piece covers this paper — [BPS](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/psychologists-have-studied-whats-happening-when-music-gives-us-chills-or-makes-us) (not fetched).
- Bannister's PLOS ONE work separates **"warm"** chills (being moved, social connection, low-arousal tenderness) from **"cold"** chills (threat, awe, high arousal) — "Distinct varieties of aesthetic chills in response to multimedia," [PLOS ONE 2019 record](https://www.prolekare.cz/casopisy/plos-one/2019-11-2/distinct-varieties-of-aesthetic-chills-in-response-to-multimedia-117235) [title only seen; content from prior, UNVERIFIED].

**Dopamine: anticipation vs. peak (Salimpoor et al. 2011, Nature Neuroscience 14:257–262) [read]**
- PET (n = 8 analysed) showed lower [11C]raclopride binding, meaning more endogenous dopamine, in dorsal and ventral striatum for pleasurable versus neutral music (p < .001). The largest changes were in right caudate and right nucleus accumbens (about 6–9% in figure labels) — [Salimpoor et al. 2011 PDF](https://www.zlab.mcgill.ca/publications/docs/salimpoor_2011_nn.pdf).
- fMRI: **caudate** activity rose during **anticipation**, defined as the 15 s before each chill. **Nucleus accumbens** activity peaked **at the chill**, and caudate activity fell during the chill itself — same source.
- Number of chills correlated with right caudate dopamine change. Chill intensity and pleasure correlated with right NAcc dopamine change (p < .01). Chill intensity correlated with pleasure, r = 0.71. Mean pleasure during the anticipation window was 2.51 versus 2.11 for the whole excerpt (t(246) = 8.5) — same source.
- Pleasurable music raised heart rate, respiration, and electrodermal activity, and lowered peripheral temperature and blood-volume-pulse amplitude. Chill intensity correlated with each of these — same source.
- Causal follow-up: **Ferreri et al. 2019, PNAS** 116(9):3793–3798 ([doi:10.1073/pnas.1811878116](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1811878116)). Levodopa (a dopamine precursor) increased musical pleasure and willingness to pay for music, and risperidone (a dopamine antagonist) decreased them [prior].

**Expectancy and pleasure at the chord level**
- **Cheung et al. 2019, Current Biology** 29(23):4084–4092 ([doi:10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.067](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.067)) modelled about 80,000 Billboard chords with an IDyOM-style statistical model. Pleasure depended on an **interaction of uncertainty and surprise**: a surprising chord was most pleasant when the context was predictable (low uncertainty), and an expected chord was most pleasant when the context was uncertain. Amygdala, hippocampus, and auditory cortex tracked this interaction, and nucleus accumbens tracked uncertainty [prior, UNVERIFIED specific numbers].
- Huron's ITPRA theory (*Sweet Anticipation*, MIT Press 2006) explains frisson as "contrastive valence": a fast, fear-like reaction to a surprise, followed by a slower appraisal that the situation is safe. The positive appraisal is amplified by contrast with the initial alarm [prior].

### Inferences
- **Chill recipe** (convergent across Sloboda, Grewe, Guhn, Panksepp, de Fleurian & Pearce): (a) build anticipation over roughly 15 s or more in a predictable context (Salimpoor's caudate window; Cheung's low uncertainty); then (b) deliver an arrival that adds energy along several dimensions at once: loudness or crescendo peak, new or returning voice, wider register, brighter or rougher timbre, and an unprepared but style-licensed harmonic or melodic event.
- **Tear recipe** (Sloboda, Mori & Iwanaga): slower, softer, lower valence. Repeated appoggiaturas, where a dissonant leaning note resolves by step onto a chord tone, especially in descending melodic sequences. Sustained tender vocal timbre. The physiology is closer to "release" than "spike."
- **Relation to the project's YouTube finding.** Melodic thinning followed by the voice re-entering on a surprising note fits this literature closely. The thinning lowers texture and complexity and so builds anticipation, an analogue of Solberg's EDM "breakdown then drop" (not verified this session). The re-entry is a "new or returning voice" (Grewe, Guhn) plus a "textural/dynamic change" (Sloboda). The surprising note is an expectancy violation after a predictable stretch (Cheung's low-uncertainty × high-surprise quadrant). The design rule this suggests: make the thinned passage **more predictable** (repetition, stable harmony), so the surprise lands in a low-uncertainty context.
- **Timing (0.55–0.60 of song).** No paper found this session reports a normative chill-timing proportion. In verse–chorus pop, 0.55–0.60 often falls at the end of the second chorus, the bridge, or a breakdown (the first "thinned" section after full choruses), which fits the thinning→re-entry mechanism. This is a structural inference, not a cited finding.

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes from Grewe 2007, Guhn 2007, and the de Fleurian & Pearce systematic review could not be re-read.
- Mori & Iwanaga's specific physiological statistics were not retrieved.
- No source found reporting where in a song (as a proportion of duration) chills most often occur.
- Solberg's EDM breakdown/drop studies were not retrieved this session. They are the closest analogue to "thinning then re-entry" and worth checking.
- Prevalence of chills in the general population was not retrieved. Estimates in the literature vary widely by definition, and no fetched source gave a figure.

## 3. Acoustic and structural cues to emotion, cue weights, and what is universal vs. learned

### Takeaway
Listeners decode basic emotions (happy, sad, angry, fearful, tender) from a small set of cues. Tempo, loudness, and pitch level and variability are shared with emotional speech, so they are probably the most universal. Mode (major/minor) and consonance preference are heavily shaped by culture. Cues combine roughly additively. In controlled experiments, mode and tempo carry the most weight.

### Cited Findings
- **Juslin & Laukka 2003, Psychological Bulletin** 129(5):770–814 ([doi:10.1037/0033-2909.129.5.770](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.129.5.770)) meta-analysed 104 vocal-expression and 41 music-performance studies and found shared acoustic cue patterns across the two domains — [as summarized in Quinto et al. 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00184/full) [summary].
- Cue profiles from Juslin & Laukka 2003 [prior; the canonical table, not re-read this session]:
  - **Happiness:** fast tempo, moderate-to-high loudness, high pitch level, large pitch variability, staccato articulation, bright timbre, sharp attacks, small timing variability.
  - **Sadness:** slow tempo, low loudness, low pitch, small pitch variability, legato, dull timbre, slow attacks, falling contours, large timing variability.
  - **Anger:** fast, loud, high pitch level, rough or sharp timbre, accents, sharp attacks, crescendos.
  - **Fear:** fast or irregular tempo, low and variable loudness, high pitch, staccato, large timing irregularity, tremolo-like jitter.
  - **Tenderness:** slow, soft, low pitch, legato, soft timbre, slow attacks.
- Decoding accuracy in Juslin & Laukka was reported as a "proportion index" (pi) of about .88 for music performance, roughly matching vocal expression. Cross-cultural vocal decoding was above chance but lower than within-culture [prior, UNVERIFIED numbers].
- **Cue weights (Eerola, Friberg & Bresin 2013, Frontiers in Psychology 4:487)** ([doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487)) systematically manipulated six cues (mode, tempo, register, dynamics, articulation, timbre) in a factorial design and had listeners rate scary, happy, sad, and peaceful. The page was blocked this session. From prior knowledge [UNVERIFIED]: **mode** had the largest overall contribution, followed by **tempo**, then register, dynamics, articulation, and timbre. Cue effects were mostly **linear and additive**, with small interactions. Ratings were predicted well by additive models.
- **Fritz et al. 2009, Current Biology** 19(7):573–576 ([doi:10.1016/j.cub.2009.02.058](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2009.02.058)): Mafa listeners in Cameroon who had never heard Western music matched computer-generated piano pieces (happy, sad, scared) to facial-expression pictures **significantly above chance**. The stimuli varied mode, tempo, pitch range, tone density, and rhythmic regularity — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/emotions-music-are-universally-recognised) [summary].
  - Mafa and Western listeners appeared to use the same cues. Faster tempo was rated happier, slower tempo more fearful. Both groups preferred unaltered music to spectrally manipulated (more dissonant) versions — same source.
  - The authors cautioned against calling music a "universal language," and noted that Mafa music expresses fewer distinct emotions — same source. Further detail from prior knowledge [UNVERIFIED]: Mafa recognition was well above chance but below Western listeners', and mode appeared to carry less information for Mafa than tempo.
- **Balkwill & Thompson 1999, Music Perception** 17(1):43–64 ([doi:10.2307/40285811](https://doi.org/10.2307/40285811)): Western listeners unfamiliar with Hindustani ragas identified joy, sadness, and anger (less so peace) above chance. Their judgements tracked tempo and melodic or rhythmic complexity. This supports their "cue-redundancy model": psychophysical cues (tempo, loudness, complexity, timbre) are pan-cultural, while culture-specific cues add to them [prior].
- **Sievers et al. 2013, PNAS** 110(1):70–75 ([doi:10.1073/pnas.1209023110](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1209023110)): US and Kreung (rural Cambodian) participants set five parameters (rate, jitter, consonance, pitch direction, step size) to express emotions in either music or movement. The two cultures produced similar settings, which suggests a shared dynamic code for music and motion [prior].
- **Consonance preference is learned, not innate. McDermott et al. 2016, Nature** 535:547–550 ([doi:10.1038/nature18635](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature18635)): the Tsimane' of Bolivia, with little exposure to Western music, rated consonant and dissonant chords as equally pleasant, though they did distinguish roughness [prior]. This partly conflicts with the Fritz 2009 consonance result. One possible reconciliation: Fritz's manipulation also changed spectral roughness and naturalness, not just interval consonance.
- **Song function is cross-culturally recognisable.** Mehr et al. 2018, *Current Biology* 28(3):356–368 ([doi:10.1016/j.cub.2017.12.042](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2017.12.042)), and Mehr et al. 2019, *Science* 366:eaax0868 ([doi:10.1126/science.aax0868](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aax0868)): naive listeners worldwide could tell lullabies, dance songs, healing songs, and love songs apart above chance. Arousal-related features (tempo, accent, melodic complexity) carry this [prior].

### Inferences
- **Most universal** (shared with speech, motion, and arousal biology): tempo/rate, loudness and loudness change, pitch height, pitch variability and step size, roughness, attack sharpness, and rhythmic regularity. These connect to brainstem reflex, entrainment, and contagion, and are the safest levers for any audience.
- **Learned** (style-dependent): major/minor as happy/sad, consonance as pleasantness, specific harmonic surprises (an expectancy violation exists only relative to a learned style model), and genre idioms. For a Western popular audience these are strong. A Western pop engine can use mode heavily, but should know its effect is enculturated.
- **Additivity is useful for engineering.** If cue effects are roughly additive (Eerola et al. 2013, unverified), an engine can treat target emotion as a weighted sum of cue settings and solve for those settings.

### Gaps
- Eerola et al. 2013 variance percentages could not be retrieved. A full cue-weight table with R² values is needed.
- Fritz 2009 sample sizes and exact recognition rates were not retrieved (only a press summary was read).
- The literature mostly studies **perceived** emotion (what the music expresses). Cue weights for **induced** emotion (what the listener feels) are less studied and were not found this session.

## 4. Lyrics and melody: word-setting, the voice, and emotion

### Takeaway
The human voice is a privileged emotional signal (the contagion mechanism). The entry or return of a voice is a repeatedly reported chill trigger. Lyrics appear to matter more for **sad** music than for happy music. Direct experimental work on word-setting (stress alignment, vowel and pitch placement) and felt emotion is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Entry of new voices or instruments, and interplay of solo and accompaniment, are among the most reliable chill correlates (Grewe et al. 2007; Guhn et al. 2007) — [Bannister & Eerola 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full) [summary].
- **Brattico et al. 2011, Frontiers in Psychology 2:308,** "A functional MRI study of happy and sad emotions in music with and without lyrics" — [Frontiers](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00308/full) (not fetchable this session; title confirmed in search). From prior knowledge [UNVERIFIED]:
  - About 15 participants heard self-selected happy and sad songs in versions with and without lyrics.
  - **Sad music with lyrics** recruited more limbic and paralimbic areas and language-related frontal areas (reported as parahippocampal gyrus, amygdala, claustrum, putamen, and inferior frontal regions) than sad instrumental music.
  - **Happy instrumental music** recruited more limbic and auditory areas than happy music with lyrics.
  - The authors concluded that lyrics are especially important for sad musical emotion, while in happy music the acoustic cues carry more.
- The voice's special status rests on Juslin & Laukka's finding that vocal and musical emotion share an acoustic code (104 vocal + 41 music studies) — [Quinto et al. 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00184/full) [summary].
- Quinto et al. 2013 found that rhythmic contrast between successive durations (nPVI) varied with emotion in both speech and music. Neutral expressions had lower nPVI, and live performances had higher nPVI than deadpan MIDI (54.03 vs 42.26, p = .003) — [Quinto et al. 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00184/full) [read]. Melodic interval variability varied with emotion in speech but not in music — same source.
- The de Fleurian & Pearce corpus found chill tracks more **instrumental** and less speech-like at the whole-track level — [i-Perception 2021](https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/0695c63ed1e5b482bf323b2bf1d15ab4/PMC8323431.pdf) [read]. This partly reflects the classical and film music over-represented in the chills literature. It does not show that voice reduces chills.

### Inferences
- **Voice re-entry is a strong design lever.** In songs, the most voice-specific chill trigger is the voice's **return** after absence (a "new voice entry" in the Grewe/Guhn sense). This matches the YouTube finding exactly. An engine should deliberately create vocal absence (instrumental break, thinned verse, or near-silence) before the key re-entry.
- **Expressive timing matters.** Live expressive timing raises rhythmic contrast relative to quantized MIDI (Quinto et al.). Scores meant to move listeners should notate or indicate rubato and lengthened stressed syllables, rather than rendering rigid grids.
- **Lyrics matter most for sad songs.** For sad or tender songs, lyric content and intelligibility likely carry much of the emotion (Brattico, unverified details). Favor clear diction, exposed vocals, and sparse accompaniment under key lines. For high-energy songs, acoustic cues dominate.
- **Appoggiatura on stressed syllables.** Combining Sloboda's appoggiatura–tears link with ordinary word-setting practice suggests placing appoggiaturas on stressed, emotionally loaded words, with the resolution on the following weak syllable. This is an inference, not a tested result.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found this session that varies **word-setting quality** (prosodic alignment) and measures felt emotion or chills.
- No direct experimental comparison was found of chill rates for vocal vs. instrumental versions of the same song.
- Brattico 2011 exact N and regions need verification against the full text.

## 5. Applying it: computational emotion models for generation and scoring, and evidence that designing for cues increases responses

### Takeaway
Emotion-conditioned generators (valence/arousal quadrants) reliably steer **arousal**, but control of **valence** is weak. Tension-model generators exist, but listener evidence for them is limited. No study found shows that music deliberately engineered with chill cues produces more chills than controls. The closest causal evidence runs the other way: removing a chill section cut chills by about 30%, a marginal result.

### Cited Findings
- **Ferreira, Mou, Whitehead & Lelis 2022 (AAAI),** "Controlling Perceived Emotion in Symbolic Music Generation with Monte Carlo Tree Search" — [arXiv 2208.05162](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.05162) [read]:
  - Method: a Linear Transformer music language model decoded with PUCT (MCTS), guided by an emotion classifier (64% test accuracy) plus a realism discriminator (93%). Emotion is defined by valence/arousal quadrants (E1–E4).
  - Listener study (199 valid MTurk responses): PUCT was "significantly better than SBBS and CS in the arousal dimension," but "there is no statistical significance between PUCT, SBBS, and CS on the valence dimension."
  - Overall quality: PUCT 3.94, SBBS 3.62, CS 3.68, human 4.11 (5-point scale).
  - Classifier-judged target hit rates were uneven: 71%, 34%, 25%, and 55% for E1–E4. Low-valence/low-arousal (E3) was hardest.
  - Related systems cited: Ferreira & Whitehead 2019 (genetic-algorithm sentiment LSTM), EMOPIA (Hung et al. 2021, pop piano dataset with emotion-token conditional sampling), Zhao et al. 2019, SBBS (2020), SentiMozart (2018), and expert or evolutionary systems (Williams et al. 2015; Scirea et al. 2017) — same source.
- Other recent emotion-conditioned generators appear on arXiv: MusER (element-based regularization, [arXiv 2312.10307](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.10307)) and a diffusion-GAN emotion-controlled model ([arXiv 2310.14040](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.14040)) [titles only seen; not read].
- **Tension-driven generation** [prior, not re-read]:
  - Herremans & Chew's MorpheuS (*IEEE Transactions on Affective Computing* 2019, [doi:10.1109/TAFFC.2017.2737984](https://doi.org/10.1109/TAFFC.2017.2737984)) generates polyphonic music that fits a target tension profile computed with the spiral array (cloud diameter, cloud momentum, tensile strain), while keeping repeated patterns.
  - Farbood 2012 (*Music Perception* 29(4):387–428, [doi:10.1525/mp.2012.29.4.387](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2012.29.4.387)) models perceived tension as a weighted combination of dynamics, pitch height, onset frequency, harmonic tension, and melodic expectancy. Loudness and pitch height are strong contributors, and features combine with time-lag and decay.
  - Lerdahl & Krumhansl 2007 (*Music Perception* 24(4):329–366) model tonal tension from the Tonal Pitch Space hierarchy and fit listener tension curves well.
- **Expectancy models usable for scoring** [prior]: IDyOM (Pearce 2005; Pearce 2018, *Annals of the NY Academy of Sciences*) gives note-by-note information content (surprise) and entropy (uncertainty). Cheung et al. 2019 showed that the uncertainty × surprise interaction from such a model predicts chord pleasure and limbic responses ([doi:10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.067](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.067)).
- **Causal evidence for chill cues:** removing chill sections (crescendo climax, string theme, guitar solo) reduced chill reports from 30 to 21 (χ² = 3.85, p = .049, φ = 0.23), with no significant change in skin conductance — [Bannister & Eerola 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full) [read]. This is the closest manipulation evidence found. Its effect is small, and the sample was small (N = 24).
- **Pharmacological evidence:** dopamine manipulation shifts musical pleasure in both directions (Ferreri et al. 2019 PNAS, [doi:10.1073/pnas.1811878116](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1811878116)) [prior]. This shows that the reward pathway is causal, but it says nothing about which notes drive it.

### Inferences
- **Recommended engine architecture**, synthesized from the above:
  1. A **tension/arousal curve** planner that controls loudness, register, density, and harmonic tension, in the spirit of Farbood and MorpheuS. Arousal is the dimension generators already control well.
  2. An **expectancy scorer** (IDyOM-style surprise and entropy) that aims for a low-entropy context just before key moments, followed by a high-surprise event (Cheung).
  3. **Chill-moment templates** placed at structural points: crescendo plus voice or solo entry plus register expansion plus unprepared harmony; or thinning followed by vocal re-entry on a surprising note (the project's YouTube pattern).
  4. **Tear-moment templates:** appoggiatura chains and descending sequences at low tempo and soft dynamics, with an exposed voice on the sad or tender lyric.
- **Valence is the hard dimension.** Generators control it poorly (Ferreira 2022), and it depends most on learned cues such as mode and harmony. Rule-based mode and harmony choices may beat learned conditioning here.
- **Validation is the open frontier.** The project's own YouTube data (loved moments, comment timing) could serve as an outcome measure. No published study has done prospective A/B testing of chill-engineered vs. control songs.
- **Expect large individual differences.** Chills are probabilistic and person-dependent (Grewe 2007; de Fleurian & Pearce review, unverified details; removal study). Design can raise the probability of a response but cannot guarantee it.

### Gaps
- No published study found where music **generated to maximize chill cues** was tested against controls for chill frequency. This is a clear gap in the literature, not just in this search.
- Listener validation of MorpheuS and similar tension-curve generators was not retrieved.
- Work after mid-2026 on emotion-conditioned audio models (for example, text-to-music systems with emotion prompts) was not surveyed. Its status as of November 2026 is unverified.
