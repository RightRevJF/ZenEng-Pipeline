# Auditory scene analysis, voice separation and masking for a symbolic songwriting engine

Research date: 2026-11-15. Verification status is marked per item. "Verified (full text)" = read in the primary document; "Verified (secondary)" = read in a reliable secondary/review that quotes the primary; "Unverified" = known from background only, not confirmed in this session (kept in Gaps or Inferences, never as a cited finding).

## Q1. Bregman-style streaming: pitch proximity vs tempo (van Noorden), onset synchrony, common fate, timbre

### Takeaway
Two successive tones stay in one stream when their pitch distance is small relative to their rate: below the fission boundary (about 1-2 semitones, nearly tempo-independent) a line always coheres; above the temporal coherence boundary (about 4 semitones at 50 ms per tone, about 13 semitones at 150 ms per tone) it always splits; between the two, attention and build-up decide. Simultaneous notes fuse when onsets are synchronous (within roughly 30-50 ms), when they move in parallel (common fate / co-modulation), and when timbre and location match.

### Cited Findings
**Pitch proximity vs rate (van Noorden 1975 and related)**
- Van Noorden (1975): as tempo slows from 50 to 150 ms per tone, the frequency separation that still allows a single coherent stream (temporal coherence boundary) grows from about 4 to about 13 semitones; the fission boundary (below which one stream is always heard) changes little with tempo. Verified (secondary, Deutsch's review chapter; figure values read from axes) — [Deutsch, "Grouping Mechanisms in Music," Psychology of Music 3rd ed., ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)
- Huron restates the fission boundary as "normally 2 semitones or less for tones less than 700 ms in duration"; the boundary is flat up to about 400-500 ms and then rises, and "in the main region of musical activity" approximates about 1 semitone. Verified (full text) — [Huron 2001, Music Perception 19(1):1-64](https://www.musanim.com/articles/Huron.ToneAndVoice.2001.pdf)
- Miller & Heise (1950): at 10 tones/s in an ABAB alternation, a difference of about 1-2 semitones is heard as a trill (one stream); larger differences split into two streams. Verified (secondary) — [Deutsch ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)
- Dowling found Telemann never uses (in the relevant compound-melody context) intervals below the Miller & Heise trill threshold. Verified (full text, as reported by Huron) — [Huron 2001](https://www.musanim.com/articles/Huron.ToneAndVoice.2001.pdf)
- Level difference alone: alternating same-frequency tones form one stream when the level difference is under about 5 dB and two above it (van Noorden 1975). Verified (secondary) — [Deutsch ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)
- Streaming builds up over time: steeply over the first ~10 s, still incomplete at 60 s (Anstis & Saida 1985); Roberts, Glasberg & Moore (2002) found it still incomplete after ~25-30 s. Verified (secondary) — [Deutsch ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)
- Temporal grouping can beat pitch: in Hamaoui & Deutsch (2010), pause increases as small as 30 ms often overrode pitch jumps as large as 11 semitones (200 ms tones, 100 ms gaps). Verified (secondary) — [Deutsch ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)
- Folk-song corpus (~4,600 songs, Huron 2006): mean melodic interval 2.0 semitones within phrases, 2.9 semitones across phrase boundaries. Verified (secondary) — [Deutsch ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)
- Temporal continuity: van Noorden found coherence degrades beyond about 800 ms inter-onset interval; Huron's principle states intermittent sounds should be separated by no more than roughly 800 ms of silence. In a 13,178-note sample, 85% of notes were under 500 ms, 3% over 1 s; median duration 0.22 s instrumental, 0.38 s vocal; 93% of vocal tones (98%+ instrumental) are followed by another tone; vocal rests typically 1-2 s. Verified (full text) — [Huron 2001](https://www.musanim.com/articles/Huron.ToneAndVoice.2001.pdf)

**Onset synchrony**
- Rasch (1978): delaying the lower of two tones improves its detectability by about 10 dB per 10 ms of delay; at 30 ms asynchrony its threshold is about the same as when it is played alone. Rasch (1988): ensemble onset asynchronies are 30-50 ms (mean 36 ms). Verified (secondary) — [Deutsch ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)
- Huron's onset-synchrony principle: onsets of nominally distinct sounds "should be separated by 100 ms or more" (e.g., a sixteenth-note offset at quarter = 80 gives 187 ms). Listeners can order clicks 20 ms apart (Hirsh 1959), but instrument attack transients may spread over 80 ms or more. Verified (full text) — [Huron 2001](https://www.musanim.com/articles/Huron.ToneAndVoice.2001.pdf)
- Darwin & Ciocca (1992): a mistuned harmonic that leads the others by >80 ms contributes less to the pitch, and not at all at 300 ms; Darwin (1984): a ~30 ms lead alters perceived vowel formant. Moore, Glasberg & Peters (1985): harmonics mistuned <3% fuse fully into the pitch, ~8% contribute virtually nothing. Verified (secondary) — [Deutsch ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)
- F0 separation: concurrent-vowel/speech separation improves up to about 1-3 semitones of F0 difference (Assmann & Summerfield 1990; Scheffers 1983); a 1-semitone difference aided separation of orchestral tones (Sandell & Darwin 1996). Verified (secondary) — [Deutsch ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)

**Common fate / co-modulation, timbre, location**
- Huron's pitch co-modulation principle: positively correlated pitch motion promotes fusion, "most enhanced when the correlation is precise with respect to log frequency" (i.e., exact parallel intervals fuse more than diatonic-parallel). Verified (full text) — [Huron 2001](https://www.musanim.com/articles/Huron.ToneAndVoice.2001.pdf)
- Timbre: Wessel's illusion, a rising three-tone figure with alternating timbres, is heard as two descending streams at fast tempo, strongest with largest timbre contrast; Huron's timbral-differentiation principle says independent parts should have distinct timbres, though choirs and keyboards often forgo this for blend/balance. Verified (full text) — [Huron 2001](https://www.musanim.com/articles/Huron.ToneAndVoice.2001.pdf)
- Spatial location strongly affects streaming but only weakly affects tonal fusion (Divenyi & Oliver 1989); barbershop singers cluster to increase homogeneity. Verified (full text) — [Huron 2001](https://www.musanim.com/articles/Huron.ToneAndVoice.2001.pdf)
- Grouping by pitch range overrides ear/location in the scale illusion (Deutsch 1975; Butler 1979 found near-universal pitch-range grouping over loudspeakers); in Tchaikovsky's Sixth the theme alternates between two violin sections but is heard as one line. Verified (secondary) — [Deutsch ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)
- Bregman/Singh timbre-streaming work and Brochard et al. (2000, JEP:HPP) on streaming exist at McGill but were not read in this session. — [Singh & Bregman 1997](https://webpages.mcgill.ca/staff/Group2/abregm1/web/pdf/1997_Singh_Bregman.pdf); [Brochard et al. 2000](https://www.mcgill.ca/mpcl/files/mpcl/brochard_2000_jephpp.pdf)

### Inferences
- Checkable from notes: for each voice, compute interval (semitones) against inter-onset interval (ms, from tempo). Flag a "within-voice split risk" when |interval| exceeds an interpolated temporal coherence boundary (about 4 st at 50 ms IOI, rising to about 13 st at 150 ms IOI; extrapolating above 150 ms is unverified). Leaps of a 4th+ in 16th-note runs at quick tempi are where melodies "break" into two streams (useful deliberately for compound melody).
- Conversely, to make two parts that alternate in close register (e.g., hocket, call-and-response fills) heard as two lines, keep them farther apart than the coherence boundary at that rate; within ~2 semitones they will merge into one trill-like line.
- Accompaniment that should fuse with the vocal (pads, doubled harmony) should share onsets; accompaniment that should stay separate should offset onsets (syncopated comping, pickup fills) by at least ~30-50 ms in performance and ideally a notated subdivision (100 ms+ per Huron).
- A gap longer than ~800 ms in a melodic line weakens its continuity; a vocal line with long rests depends on its timbre/register to be re-identified, so accompaniment fills in those gaps are fine but should not sit in the melody's register immediately before the voice re-enters.

### Gaps
- Exact van Noorden boundary curves (full table of TRT vs semitones, including slower rates above 150 ms) not read in the primary report; the Eindhoven thesis PDF exists ([van Noorden 1975, TU/e](https://pure.tue.nl/ws/files/3389175/152538.pdf)) but was not fetched. Values above are from Deutsch's reading of the figure.
- Bregman's 1990 book itself was not consulted directly; claims are via Huron and Deutsch.
- No numeric threshold was found for how much timbre difference compensates for small pitch separation in a score-checkable way.

## Q2. Huron, "Tone and Voice" (Music Perception 19(1), 2001): principles and the rules they generate

### Takeaway
Huron derives the traditional voice-leading rules from six core perceptual principles (toneness, temporal continuity, minimum masking, tonal fusion, pitch proximity, pitch co-modulation) plus four auxiliary, style-optional principles (onset synchrony, limited density, timbral differentiation, source location). Note: the user's list grouped onset synchrony with the core six; in the paper the core six include temporal continuity, and onset synchrony is the first auxiliary principle. The full text was read (two passes over a 170k-character PDF).

### Cited Findings
All items in this section: Verified (full text) — [Huron 2001, PDF hosted at musanim.com](https://www.musanim.com/articles/Huron.ToneAndVoice.2001.pdf); landing page [Huron, OSU](https://csml.som.ohio-state.edu/Huron/Publications/huron.voice.leading.html); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/215646542_Tone_and_Voice_A_Derivation_of_the_Rules_of_Voice-Leading_from_Perceptual_Principles).

**Core principles (P1-P6)**
- P1 Toneness: tones with the highest virtual-pitch weight are harmonic complex tones "centered in the region between F2 and G5" (about 80-800 Hz), with maximum near 300 Hz; average notated pitch in the corpus is near D4/D#4. Pure tones above about 5 kHz lose pitch.
- P2 Temporal continuity: continuous or recurring sound; gaps no more than about 800 ms.
- P3 Minimum masking: "approximately equivalent amounts of spectral energy should fall in each critical band"; for harmonic tones, "simultaneously sounding notes should be more widely spaced as the register descends."
- P4 Tonal fusion: fusion strongest for unisons, then octaves, then perfect fifths; fusion-promoting intervals "ought to be shunned in direct proportion" when independence is the goal.
- P5 Pitch proximity: fission boundary normally 2 semitones or less for tones under 700 ms.
- P6 Pitch co-modulation: positively correlated pitch motion promotes fusion; strongest for exact log-frequency (chromatic-exact) parallelism.

**Auxiliary principles (P7-P10)**
- P7 Onset synchrony: distinct parts' onsets "should be separated by 100 ms or more."
- P8 Limited density: if parts should be distinguishable, use "three or fewer" concurrent voices. Huron (1989b): with five expert musicians (130 estimation trials, 263 entry-detection trials), accuracy drops sharply from three to four voices. Parncutt (1993): listeners undercount chord tones beyond three pitch classes. In 108 Bach works, Bach thickens two-part writing with pseudo-polyphony but retires voices more in four-plus-voice works, implying a preferred density of about three streams.
- P9 Timbral differentiation: distinct timbres per part aid independence.
- P10 Source location: spatial separation aids streaming.

**Rule -> principle derivations (as given in the paper)**
| Rule (traditional unless noted) | Derived from |
|---|---|
| Registral compass (write within F2-G5) [D2] | Toneness |
| Toneness rule (prefer harmonic complex tones) [D1, nontraditional] | Toneness |
| Sustained tones [D3, nontraditional] | Temporal continuity |
| Chord spacing (wider intervals at bottom) [D4] | Minimum masking |
| Tessitura-sensitive spacing [D5, nontraditional] | Minimum masking |
| Avoid unisons [D6] | Tonal fusion |
| Avoid octaves [D7], avoid perfect fifths [D8] (as harmonic intervals) | Tonal fusion |
| Avoid tonal fusion [D9, nontraditional] | Tonal fusion |
| Common-tone rule [D10] | Pitch proximity |
| Conjunct movement [D11] | Pitch proximity |
| Avoid leaps (corollary C3 of D10, D11) | Pitch proximity |
| Leap-lengthening [D12, nontraditional] | Pitch proximity + temporal factors |
| No part-crossing [D14] | Pitch proximity |
| No part-overlap [D15] | Pitch proximity |
| Semblant motion [D16], parallel motion [D17] (nontraditional), approach rules D18-D20 | Co-modulation (+ fusion) |
| No parallel unisons/octaves/fifths [D21] | Tonal fusion + pitch co-modulation |
| No exposed (hidden/direct) octaves/fifths [D22] | Fusion + proximity + co-modulation |
| Asynchronous preparation of tonal fusion [D23, nontraditional] | Onset synchrony + fusion |
| Textural density of 3+ voices (traditional) | Listed as traditional; the extract did not show a principle-derivation (paper relates density to P8) |

**Corpus evidence**
- Spacing: more than 10,000 four-note sonorities from Haydn string quartets and Bach keyboard works show intervals between adjacent voices widening as register descends (Fig. 4), consistent with critical-band spacing.
- Harmonic interval use in Bach's Sinfonias (Huron 1991b) correlates with sensory dissonance at -0.85 and with tonal fusion at -0.82; together R^2 = 0.88. Unisons and octaves are underused relative to their low dissonance (i.e., Bach avoids fused intervals, not only dissonant ones).
- Maximum sensory dissonance at about 30-40% of a critical bandwidth (Greenwood 1991); Plomp & Levelt estimated 25%.
- Parallel motion is avoided more than similar motion, and most strongly for fused intervals (Huron 1989a).
- Part crossing analysed in 105 Bach works (Huron 1991a); Bach avoids crossing in two-part writing.
- Onset synchrony: Huron (1993a) analysed 10 of Bach's 15 two-part Inventions; their autophase plots dip at zero (Bach avoids simultaneous onsets); Deutsch's review reports that in 11 of 15 Inventions no rhythmic permutation would have produced more asynchrony ([Deutsch ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)). Sinfonia No. 1 dips at synchrony, whereas the hymn "Adeste Fideles" peaks there. Across 143 works, onset synchrony was the strongest of six factors separating homophony from polyphony (Huron 1989c). In fugues most perfect intervals are approached asynchronously; in chorales most are approached synchronously; imperfect and dissonant intervals show no such difference.
- Texture space (173 works) by semblant motion and onset synchrony separates monophony, hymns, Bach polyphony, and barbershop (between homophony and monophony). Huron cautions this shows separability on 2 of 10 principles, not genre definition.
- Homophony puzzle: homophony obeys most voice-leading rules yet ignores onset asynchrony; hypotheses offered are text intelligibility (shared syllable onsets in vocal music) and rhythmic uniformity for dance/march.
- Open issues Huron lists: why octave prohibitions mainly bind outer voices, role of learned schemas, innate vs learned.

### Inferences
- Engine-checkable rules mapped to principles (pitch/onset/duration only):
  - Range: flag sustained parts below F2 (~87 Hz) or above G5 (~784 Hz) as weak-pitch (toneness), noting bass guitar/kick sub-region is acceptable as foundation but not as a melodic stream.
  - Spacing: for adjacent simultaneous voices, require interval to increase as register descends (e.g., compare against one or more ERB at the lower note's fundamental; see Q4 formula). Close thirds below roughly C3 = masking/mud flag.
  - Fusion: count harmonic unisons/octaves/fifths between melody and any countermelody/harmony part; flag parallels and exposed (similar-motion approach) perfect intervals; for intended "independent" parts, prefer asynchronous approach to perfect intervals (D23).
  - Proximity: within-part steps preferred; leaps followed by return; no crossing/overlap of the melody with harmony parts (a harmony part crossing above the lead will steal the top-voice salience, see Q3).
  - Density: no more than three independently moving lines (lead + countermelody + bass is the canonical ceiling); extra parts should be homorhythmic pads/chords so they fuse.
  - Onset: background vocals meant to blend share the lead's onsets (homophony); countermelodies meant to be heard move in the lead's rests or offset by at least a subdivision.
- Different song styles are just different subsets: barbershop/close harmony deliberately keeps co-modulation and onset synchrony (fusion wanted), so the engine should switch rule sets by intended texture ("blend backing vocals" vs "independent countermelody") rather than apply chorale rules universally.

### Gaps
- Exact numeric results of the spacing analysis (mean interval by register band) and whether Huron specifically tests Bach chorales against critical bands were not extracted; the extract mentions Haydn quartets and Bach keyboard works for Fig. 4, not chorales.
- The paper's textural-density derivation was not visible in the extract.

## Q3. Top- and bottom-voice salience (high-voice superiority; low-voice timing superiority)

### Takeaway
In polyphony the highest voice is encoded more robustly than lower voices, in nonmusicians, musicians and 7-month-old infants, and a cochlear model attributes this to peripheral mechanics (middle-ear filtering and two-tone suppression). The bottom voice, by contrast, dominates timing perception (Hove et al. 2014), not verified in full this session. The melody belongs on top; a countermelody placed above the sung melody competes for salience.

### Cited Findings
- Fujioka et al. (2005): MMN to deviants in two simultaneous 5-note melodies was larger for the upper voice, in musicians and nonmusicians (larger overall in musicians). Fujioka et al. (2008): same with simple tones 15 semitones apart, loudness matched (~85 phons), ruling out loudness. Verified (secondary, as reported in Trainor et al. 2014) — [Trainor, Marie, Bruce & Bidelman 2014, Hearing Research 308](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/High%20voice%20superiority%20effect%20Hearing%20Research%202014.pdf)
- Marie & Trainor (2013): in 7-month-old infants, MMN to deviants was larger and earlier for the higher voice, indicating an early, largely bottom-up effect. Verified (secondary) — [Trainor et al. 2014](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/High%20voice%20superiority%20effect%20Hearing%20Research%202014.pdf); record: [Marie & Trainor, McMaster](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1434886)
- Marie et al. (2012): bass-range instrumentalists showed a smaller, non-significant high-voice effect vs significant in soprano-range players; experience reduces but does not reverse it. Verified (secondary) — [Trainor et al. 2014](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/High%20voice%20superiority%20effect%20Hearing%20Research%202014.pdf)
- Auditory-nerve model (Zilany et al. 2009; Ibrahim & Bruce 2010): middle-ear filtering and two-tone suppression favour the upper tone's harmonics; removing them predicts low-voice superiority. The higher-tone bias is stronger in lower registers. Verified (full text) — [Trainor et al. 2014](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/High%20voice%20superiority%20effect%20Hearing%20Research%202014.pdf)
- Model salience ratio (high/low) correlated with voice separation in the Bach chorale BWV 4 at r = 0.85; maximal high-voice advantage at about 24-30 semitones (2-2.5 octaves) of separation, matching typical soprano-bass spacing in Bach chorales. Authors call the link to compositional practice "speculative." Verified (full text) — [Trainor et al. 2014](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/High%20voice%20superiority%20effect%20Hearing%20Research%202014.pdf)
- Hove, Marie, Bruce & Trainor (2014, PNAS 111(28)): lower-pitched voice superiority for time/rhythm encoding (MMN and motor tapping). Existence verified from search listings only; full text blocked (PMC captcha), so numbers not extracted — [PMC4104866](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4104866); [Trainor lab page](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/HoveEtAl2014)

### Inferences
- Placement rule: keep the sung melody as the highest sounding pitched line during vocal passages; flag any accompaniment or harmony note sustained above the melody (especially piano right-hand top notes or high synth lines) as a top-voice salience conflict. Harmony vocals written above the lead (e.g., a high third) are a deliberate exception that pulls salience upward; flag them as such rather than forbid.
- Countermelodies work best below the melody (inner voice) or in the melody's rests; if placed above, they will tend to be heard as the tune.
- Outer-voice framing of about 2-2.5 octaves between melody and bass maximizes the modelled top-voice advantage; much narrower framing (melody and bass within an octave or so) reduces it.
- Put rhythmic drive (syncopation, groove-defining onsets) in the bass line; the low voice is where timing is best heard (pending verification of Hove et al. numbers).

### Gaps
- Hove et al. 2014 effect sizes (MMN amplitudes, tapping adjustment percentages) not obtained.
- No study found that measures high-voice superiority specifically with a sung (vocal timbre) melody against instrumental accompaniment.

## Q4. Masking: critical bands/ERB, the singer's formant, accompaniment register/density, mixing

### Takeaway
Masking is governed by auditory filter width (ERB = 24.7(4.37f + 1) Hz, f in kHz), so notes are "crowded" when partials fall within roughly one ERB, which happens at much larger musical intervals in low registers. Classical singers avoid orchestral masking with the singer's formant near 3 kHz where orchestral energy is low (orchestra peak near 500 Hz); pop singers lack it but show a peak near 3.5 kHz, and loud pop accompaniment has a spectral slope similar to a Wagner orchestra.

### Cited Findings
- Glasberg & Moore (1990): ERB(f) = 24.7 x (4.37f + 1) Hz, f in kHz, valid for moderate levels and 0.1-10 kHz; ERB-number scale ERBS(f) = 21.4 x log10(1 + 0.00437f), f in Hz. Example: ERB(500 Hz) ~ 79 Hz, ERB(1 kHz) ~ 133 Hz, ERB(4 kHz) ~ 457 Hz (computed from formula). Verified (secondary, Wikipedia mirror citing Hearing Research 47:103-138) — [Equivalent rectangular bandwidth](https://static.hlt.bme.hu/semantics/external/pages/mel/en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalent_rectangular_bandwidth.html)
- Huron's minimum-masking principle: equal spectral energy per critical band; wider spacing as register descends; maximum roughness at ~25-40% of a critical band. Verified (full text) — [Huron 2001](https://www.musanim.com/articles/Huron.ToneAndVoice.2001.pdf)
- Singer's formant: spectral-envelope peak around 3 kHz; orchestral long-term energy peaks around 500 Hz, so the voice's energy sits in a band the orchestra does not mask, letting the voice carry without extra effort; sopranos can lose it at very high F0 (Sundberg 1987, 1995). Verified (secondary, McGill Timbre and Orchestration resource) — [Timbre and Orchestration: The Singer's Formant](https://timbreandorchestration.org/tor/timbre-lingo/2019/12/10/the-singers-formant)
- Borch & Sundberg (2002), "Spectral distribution of solo voice and accompaniment in pop music," Logopedics Phoniatrics Vocology 27(1):37-41: loud pop accompaniment's level difference between 0.5 and 2.5 kHz resembles a Wagner orchestra; pop singers showed no singer's formant but a peak near 3.5 kHz; authors suggest boosting 3-4 kHz in monitors. Verified (abstract) — [Borch & Sundberg 2002, library record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_pubmed_primary_12375627)
- Rasch (1978): onset asynchrony unmasks: ~10 dB detection gain per 10 ms lead of the target. Verified (secondary) — [Deutsch ch. 6](https://deutsch.ucsd.edu/pdf/PsyMus_3_Ch6_Grouping_Mechanisms.pdf)
- Related sources located but not read: an RPI study on whether vibrato and formant shaping affect vocal detection in accompaniment ([RPI DSpace](https://dspace.rpi.edu/items/56bb4d3c-6951-43df-9f15-22aed4fc1905)); NCVS tutorial on the singer's formant ([NCVS](https://ncvs.org/tutorials/the-singers-formant/)); Millhouse (SST 2002) on singer's formant ([ASSTA PDF](https://assta.org/proceedings/sst/sst2002/Papers/Millhouse070.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Register/placement check from notes (score-only proxy for masking):
  1. Compute the vocal melody's F0 range per phrase. Flag accompaniment notes (sustained, i.e., duration >= the melody note) whose fundamental lies within the vocal F0 band or within about +-1 ERB of the concurrent melody note; these compete directly for the voice's fundamental and low harmonics.
  2. Weight conflicts by density: count sounding accompaniment notes inside the vocal octave per beat; more than one or two sustained pitches in the melody's octave is a "crowding" flag, especially when they move with the voice's onsets.
  3. Because masking spreads upward from low/loud maskers (textbook; unverified in this session), dense mid-low accompaniment (roughly C3-C5, the 130-520 Hz region where the orchestra/band spectrum peaks near 500 Hz) is the main threat to vowel intelligibility; prefer leaving a "pocket" of about an octave around the vocal register, voicing pads either below (with wide low spacing) or above the voice (but then watch high-voice salience, Q3).
  4. Prefer accompaniment onsets offset from vocal syllable onsets for busy parts (fills in the gaps), synchronized only for blended backing vocals.
- What the score cannot capture: whether the voice has a singer's formant or a pop ~3.5 kHz peak, instrument brightness (cymbals, distorted guitar, bright synth in 2-4 kHz), level balance, EQ carving, panning. The engine should output these as mixing notes (e.g., "keep 2-4 kHz clear on guitars during verses") rather than score rules.

### Gaps
- Upward spread of masking (asymmetry of masking toward higher frequencies at high levels) and its numeric slopes were not verified from a source this session.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that measures lyric intelligibility or melody salience as a function of accompaniment register and note density in songs; this remains the biggest evidence gap for numeric thresholds. Mixing-practice literature (engineer guidance on vocal EQ pockets) was not gathered.
- Pop accompaniment spectrum numbers (dB per octave) from Borch & Sundberg not available beyond the abstract.

## Q5. Computational models and code for voice separation and salience from symbolic data

### Takeaway
Voice separation from symbolic music is a mature MIR task: rule-based perceptual models (Temperley, Chew & Wu contig mapping, Madsen & Widmer, Cambouropoulos/Karydis VISA allowing chords), HMMs (McLeod & Steedman), and recent neural/GNN models (Karystinaios et al.; CPJKU piano_svsep with public code). None of them is a masking or salience estimator; a masking checker for the engine would have to be built from ERB/register heuristics.

### Cited Findings
- Survey of methods split into monophonic-voice methods (Temperley 2001; Chew & Wu 2004; Kirlin & Utgoff 2005; Madsen & Widmer 2006; Jordanous 2008; Rafailidis et al. 2008; de Valk et al. 2013) and chord-allowing methods (Kilian & Hoos 2002; Cambouropoulos 2006; Karydis et al. 2007 VISA; Rafailidis et al. 2009). Reported F-measures: Madsen & Widmer P 95.94 / R 70.11 / F 81.01 on 30 Inventions + 48 Fugues; de Valk et al. F 93.74 on 10 fugues. Their own neural models (Gray & Bunescu line of work): chord-level F 88.26 on 20 pop pieces, 97.30 on 50 Bach chorales (music21 corpus). Principles named: pitch proximity, temporal continuity (Huron 2001), rarity of crossing / "bounced" percept. Code "will be made publicly available" (no link/licence given). Verified (full text) — [From Note-Level to Chord-Level Neural Network Models for Voice Separation in Symbolic Music, arXiv 2011.03028](https://arxiv.org/html/2011.03028v1)
- VISA (Voice Integration/Segregation Algorithm; Karydis, Nanopoulos, Papadopoulos, Cambouropoulos 2007) treats synchronous notes as possibly fusing into one stream (vertical integration) and separates horizontally by proximity. ### Inferences
- For ZenEng the cheap path is to implement Huron's rules directly (Q2 table) plus the Q1 rate-dependent proximity check and the Q4 register pocket check, since the engine generates labelled voices already and does not need voice separation; voice-separation models are useful only as a validator ("does an off-the-shelf separator recover my intended lines?"). If a VISA-like or GNN separator merges the countermelody with the lead or splits the melody, that is a perceptual-streaming warning.
- A practical salience score: for each melody note, add penalties for (a) any concurrent accompaniment pitch above it, (b) accompaniment pitches within 1 ERB of the melody F0 or its 2nd-3rd harmonics, (c) synchronous onsets of dense chords in the vocal octave, (d) parallel perfect intervals with any other part. These are inferences assembled from the cited principles, not a published validated model.
