## 1. How do sung lines begin: on the downbeat or with a pickup? Dependence on unstressed line openings; corpus figures

### Takeaway
The governing rule in every source is that stressed syllables go on strong beats, so a line whose text opens with unstressed syllables starts before the strong beat (a pickup), and the commonest English folk line shape is exactly that: weak syllable, then strong beat. I found no corpus figure for anacrusis frequency by metre (3/4 vs 6/8 vs 4/4) in any source I could open.

### Cited Findings
- EMPIRICAL (folk verse, 364 four-beat lines chosen from 640, settings from nine native speakers): "the most common kind of line for the grid we are examining is /σ.Σ.σ.Σ.σ.Σ.σ.Σ./", i.e. eight syllables, alternating, beginning with a stressless syllable placed before the first strong beat. The corpus grid itself "begins with a Medium rather than a Strong position". No frequency count for upbeat starts is given. — [Hayes 2005, Textsetting as Constraint Conflict (PDF)](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL/MODEL: Hayes names four competing goals of English text setting: (a) stress matches strong position; (b) avoid long lapses (stretches with no syllable onsets); (c) avoid extreme syllable compression; (d) align phonological phrase boundaries with line boundaries. Lines sacrifice a lower goal for a higher one. — [Hayes, Textsetting page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)
- EMPIRICAL: native consultants gave on average 2.4 distinct settings per line; Hayes's ten-constraint ranked grammar matched the favourite setting for 70.6% of lines (a further 6.9% tied); the earlier Halle–Lerdahl style algorithm set only 84 of 364 lines (23.1%) correctly. So line placement is rule-governed but not fully determined. — [Hayes 2005 (PDF)](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL (rock corpus, 80 songs, 23,129 notes, almost all 4/4; plus a 10-song 19th-century comparison set of 1,397 notes): in the 19th-century songs 0 of 602 stressed syllables were syncopated at the eighth-note level; in rock 2,382 of 10,433 stressed syllables were (22.8%), and 77.2% of those syncopations were anticipations (the stressed syllable arrives early, before the strong beat it belongs to), not delays. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley, Anticipatory Syncopation in Rock: A Corpus Study, Music Perception 36(4)](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL (same corpus): function words sit on weak positions. Mean metrical strength of "it" .122 (254 tokens), "I" .313 (761 tokens), "in" .562 (283 tokens). Stress make-up of the corpus: 43.5% primary stress, 1.8% secondary, 50.3% unstressed, 4.4% non-words. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL (perception, stimuli all in 4/4): a short-short-long opening is heard as an upbeat (mean "upbeatness" .79 on a 0–1 scale in melodic sequences) while long-short-short is heard as starting on the downbeat (.12). Duration pattern was the strongest cue; ascending contour and starting on the dominant gave small extra pushes toward an upbeat hearing; articulation had no significant effect. The paper contains no corpus counts and no comparison by metre. — [London, Himberg & Cross 2009, The Effect of Structural and Performance Factors in the Perception of Anacruses, Music Perception 27(2)](https://cdn.carleton.edu/uploads/sites/721/2021/12/London-Himberg-Cross-2009-MP.pdf)
- THEORY (as cited by London et al.): in Cooper & Meyer (1960), all 11 examples of "iambs in duple and triple meter" involve anacruses. — [London, Himberg & Cross 2009](https://cdn.carleton.edu/uploads/sites/721/2021/12/London-Himberg-Cross-2009-MP.pdf)
- THEORY (Great American Songbook, 207 songs 1905–1973): iambic lyric lines are described as "measure-long anacruses", placing their accents on specific hypermetric positions. — [Callahan 2013, Sentential Lyric-Types in the Great American Songbook, Music Theory Online 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)
- DEFINITION/NOTATION: an anacrusis is "a note or sequence of notes, a motif, which precedes the first downbeat in a bar in a musical phrase"; by convention the last bar is shortened by the same amount so the total is whole bars. In verse, anacrusis is extrametrical unstressed syllables before the first lift; it does not form its own metrical position. Examples given: "Happy Birthday to You" (opening word is the pickup; note this tune is in 3/4, though the article does not say so), "The Star-Spangled Banner". — [Wikipedia, Anacrusis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anacrusis)
- CRAFT ADVICE (Pattison): phrases that start on the downbeat "or pick up to the downbeat" are both "front-heavy" and "feel solid, stable"; phrases that start after the downbeat are "back-heavy", and the later the start "the less stable it feels". In 4/4, "most phrases starting after beat 3 tend to function as a pickup to the next downbeat". He adds "Obviously, these concepts work in any time signature" and counts 3/4 in two-bar units: "strong 2 3, weak 2 3…". — [Pattison, The Art of Phrasing](https://www.patpattison.com/art-of-phrasing)
- CRAFT ADVICE (Pattison): stressed syllables belong on strong beats; a weak syllable or a relative pronoun on a downbeat damages the natural meaning. — [Pattison, Setting Words to Music](https://www.patpattison.com/setting-words-to-music)
- HYMNODY: a hymn text may be sung to any tune of the same syllable metre "provided the poetic foot also matches"; common, long and short metre are iambic by convention, most 8.7.8.7 texts trochaic. — [Wikipedia, Metre (hymn)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_(hymn))

### Inferences
- A pickup is not a stylistic option the engine may or may not take; it falls out of stress-to-beat alignment. If the first stressed syllable of a line is to sit on the first strong beat of its slot, every unstressed syllable before it must sit before the bar line, i.e. inside the tail of the previous line's slot. An iambic opening needs one pickup note, an anapaestic opening two. A line that opens on a stress starts on the bar line.
- So the slot model needs to be "N bars measured from the first stressed syllable's downbeat", with the pickup borrowed from the previous slot. That in turn requires the previous line to stop early enough to leave the pickup room plus a breath (see question 2).
- For fault (a): the title line has seven syllables, five stressed, so it almost certainly opens on or within one syllable of a stress. It needs its bar line. In 6/8 a pickup is naturally one eighth (iamb) or two eighths (anapaest) ahead of the bar; the preceding line should therefore end no later than the fourth eighth of its last bar, and earlier if a rest is wanted.
- London et al.'s result gives a recipe for making a pickup sound like a pickup: short notes leading to a longer one on the downbeat. In 6/8 that is eighth(s) into a quarter or dotted quarter; in 3/4 a quarter (or two eighths) into a half or dotted half.
- Rock practice (anticipation) is the opposite fault from the engine's: stressed syllables may come an eighth early, almost never five eighths late. A hook pushed late past its bar line has no support in any corpus I found.
- From general knowledge, not verified in this session: many standard English iambic-text tunes in triple time start each line with a one-beat pickup (e.g. "Amazing Grace"/NEW BRITAIN in 3/4, "Happy Birthday" in 3/4), and 6/8 folk tunes with iambic or anapaestic words start with an eighth or two eighths. The writer should treat this as background, not as a cited finding.

### Gaps
- No corpus percentage for anacrusis by metre or genre. I could not open any Essen-based count of upbeat-initial melodies, nor Huron's work on phrase starts; searches returned London et al. (no corpus counts) and unrelated items. This is the main unanswered item.
- No hymn-tune count (e.g. share of iambic-metre tunes that open with an upbeat) was found; the Wikipedia hymn-metre page does not discuss upbeats or time signatures.
- Nothing 6/8- or 12/8-specific on line entry was found in any empirical source.

## 2. Usual phrase length in bars; how lines of different syllable counts are fitted; where the breath or rest falls

### Takeaway
English strophic song is built on a four-strong-beat line in a strictly binary hierarchy (line, couplet, quatrain), and lines of differing length are fitted into equal time, with the slack left at the right-hand end of the line as a held note or silent beat. Sources agree that the gap belongs at the end of a line, not the start of the next.

### Cited Findings
- EMPIRICAL/THEORY (folk verse): the line is a fixed-length unit of four strong beats on a 16-position grid (16 weak, 8 medium, 4 strong positions); every line has the same number of slots and final positions may be empty. The hierarchy is binary throughout: 1 quatrain = 2 couplets = 4 lines = 8 hemistichs = 16 feet = 32 metrical positions. — [Hayes 2005 (PDF)](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf); [Hayes & Schuh course notes, Quatrain I](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12QuatrainI.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL: "Lines can be taxonomized according to the omissions they make at their right edge" (English truncates at the right edge of the line). — [Hayes & Schuh notes, Quatrain I](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12QuatrainI.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL (1,028 Southern Appalachian folk songs): the end of the line grid is allocated "to silent beats, which serve to highlight the constituency of the quatrain"; these silent beats are "clearly noticeable as an obligatory pause, preserving the isochronous rhythm". Only 107 songs (10.4%) show metrical-position "thefts" between neighbouring lines. A 16-terminal-position grid typically suffices for one line. — [Hayes & MacEachern 1996, Are There Lines in Folk Poetry? (PDF)](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachern1996AreThereLinesInFolkPoetry.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL: inside a line, gaps are avoided. A lapse is "three empty grid positions in a row"; "Lapses are quite rare among the settings given by the Hayes/Kaun consultants" and are confined to lines "so short that they cannot be set without a lapse". Lines do not always have four stresses for the four strong positions; "sometimes there are just two". — [Hayes 2005 (PDF)](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- THEORY (Great American Songbook): the basic idea n is two bars; a typical eight-bar phrase is 2n + 2n + 4n in the sense presentation, continuation, whole; lyric lines usually fill n-length (two-bar) units; continuations fragment to half-n or quarter-n lines (one bar or half a bar), so short lines of five or six syllables speed up the surface. The cadential arrival most often falls at the start of the last two-bar unit and a sustained final word can fill that whole unit; surface rhythm slows to half and whole notes at the end of a section. — [Callahan 2013, MTO 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)
- CORPUS COUNTS (same): 207 songs, 111 with at least one sentential phrase; by composer Rodgers 67%, Porter 63%, Gershwin 59%, Loewe 42%, Kern 37%. — [Callahan 2013](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)
- THEORY: "the downbeat rest is a deft way of avoiding an energy-sapping pause" at a line boundary (the next line enters just after the downbeat instead of the previous one ending with a long gap). — [Callahan 2013](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)
- CRAFT ADVICE (Pattison): bars pair as strong and weak; "weak-bar phrasing requires the preceding strong bar to be empty"; a seven-bar section makes "the expected weak eighth bar turn into a strong bar", which is felt as instability. — [Pattison, The Art of Phrasing](https://www.patpattison.com/art-of-phrasing)
- CRAFT ADVICE (Pattison): "Two musical phrases should equal 2 lyric ideas"; when lyric and musical phrases disagree, "The differing roadmaps –musical phrases and lyric phrases – split the grammatical unit unnaturally and meaning evaporates." — [Pattison, Setting Words to Music](https://www.patpattison.com/setting-words-to-music)
- EMPIRICAL (cross-cultural, registered report): song is slower than speech in every recording set (effect size D = 1.6), uses more stable pitches (D = 0.7), and appears to have longer stretches between breaths. — [Ozaki et al. 2024, Science Advances 10, eadm9797](https://research.gold.ac.uk/36542/1/Ozaki_2024.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL (2,371 English popular songs, abstract only): placement of syllables on the metrical grid is sensitive to the syllable's natural duration, and the effect survives controlling for final lengthening. — [Syllable weight and natural duration in textsetting popular music in English, English Language and Linguistics](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-language-and-linguistics/article/abs/syllable-weight-and-natural-duration-in-textsetting-popular-music-in-english/50F8885341914C37D6F1BD22A632A0B6)

### Inferences
- Mapping the four-beat line onto bars: in 3/4, where there is one strong beat a bar, a four-beat line is four bars. In 6/8, with two strong beats a bar, it is two bars at a lilting tempo, or four bars when the song moves slowly enough that each bar carries one main stress. In 12/8 it is one bar, or two for a slow ballad. This is my mapping of Hayes's grid, not a statement in the sources.
- That mapping reproduces the user's lead-sheet figure for waltzes. The commonest line, eight syllables for four strong beats, spread over four bars of 3/4 gives 2.0 syllables a bar; a six-syllable line with its last syllable held gives 1.5 a bar with one or two bars of a single held note. An average near 2.3 notes a bar with roughly one bar in five a single held note is what a four-bars-a-line waltz with long line-final notes looks like. Two bars a line in 3/4 (the engine's choice) puts two strong beats in each bar's worth of text and doubles the density to about 3.5–4.
- Fault (b) fix implied: in 3/4 the default slot should be four bars for a four-stress line (three or four bars for a three-stress line, see question 5), not two. Two-bar slots in 3/4 suit only two-stress half-lines.
- Three-bar slots conflict with the binary hierarchy that every source describes (2-bar basic idea, strong/weak bar pairs, 2-4-8 grouping). A three-bar line leaves the next line starting on what was expected to be a weak bar. In fault (a) the 13-syllable line was given three bars of 6/8; under the binary model it should have four (three bars of text plus a bar that holds the final note and the rest), or two bars with denser rhythm, with three reserved for deliberate asymmetry.
- Where the slack goes: at the right edge. The line ends on a long note that falls on or near a strong beat, the rest of the slot is silence or sustain, and the next line's pickup uses the final beat. Gaps inside the line are disfavoured (lapses rare); gaps at the left edge of the slot (late entry, Callahan's downbeat rest, Pattison's back-heavy phrase) are an expressive choice associated with instability, not a way of absorbing overflow.
- The 10.4% "theft" figure suggests that lines borrowing time from their neighbours are a minority device in folk song. I read "theft" as a line using positions that belong to the adjacent line's grid; the fetch summary did not spell out the direction, so check the paper before leaning on this.
- Fitting order suggested by Hayes's constraint ranking: first put stresses on strong beats; then avoid squeezing syllables below the weakest grid level; then avoid internal gaps; let the line end fall short. An engine that fills the slot evenly to its end has that priority upside down.

### Gaps
- No source I reached states phrase length in bars specifically for waltzes or for 6/8 songs (Rothstein, Lerdahl & Jackendoff, Temperley and Hasty were not reachable as open text in this session). The 16- and 32-bar waltz chorus of Tin Pan Alley, with one lyric line per four bars, is common knowledge among musicians but I have no citation for it here.
- No quantitative data on the proportion of the line slot occupied by the final note plus rest, in any metre.
- No data on melisma versus held notes versus rests as the preferred way of absorbing a short line; Hayes's grid does not distinguish a held syllable from silence.
- Ozaki et al. report no absolute rates and nothing English-specific.

## 3. Leaving room before an important line (title or hook); hook placement; "power positions" and "spotlight"

### Takeaway
The pedagogy is consistent and partly backed by chart analysis: titles go at the first or last line of the chorus, and the lines that carry a song's essence are set front-heavy in strong bars, which presupposes that the bar before them is clear. I found no experiment testing whether a rest before a hook improves memorability.

### Cited Findings
- INDUSTRY ANALYSIS (Jason Blume citing David Penn's analysis of Billboard Hot 100 top-ten hits 2022–2023): "In most instances, the title occurs in the first or last line of the chorus." Title-in-chorus is more common in pop than in hip hop. "Bookending" puts the title at both the start and end of the chorus; songs without a chorus often put the title at the start or end of each verse; one publisher advised title-at-end-only "to provide more of a payoff". The article says nothing about rests or note length around the title. — [Blume, The Best Place for Your Song Title, BMI](https://www.bmi.com/news/entry/the-best-place-for-your-song-title)
- CRAFT ADVICE (Pattison): "If it's a line you really mean, a fact or a commitment, look for downbeats and look for strong bars." In his analysis of a song by the Cars, the only front-heavy strong-bar phrases were those that "make up the song's essence". Anticipating the downbeat slightly makes "the punch even stronger". For regret, uncertainty or longing he recommends back-heavy or weak-bar phrasing instead. — [Pattison, The Art of Phrasing](https://www.patpattison.com/art-of-phrasing)
- CRAFT ADVICE (Pattison): "weak-bar phrasing requires the preceding strong bar to be empty", i.e. space before a phrase is what defines where it is heard to start. — [Pattison, The Art of Phrasing](https://www.patpattison.com/art-of-phrasing)
- THEORY/CORPUS (Callahan): in the largest lyric type, "List + Reveal" (35% of sentential phrases), the reveal "is often the title" and comes at the climax, i.e. at the end of the phrase, with the cadential arrival at the start of the last two-bar unit and the final word free to sustain through it. "Bookended" lyrics (16%) begin with the title and return to it. One example sets the title word on a rising leap to a whole note. — [Callahan 2013, MTO 19.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.13.19.3/mto.13.19.3.callahan.php)
- EMPIRICAL (folk verse): temporal breaks create salience. Saliency is "perception of separateness created by temporal breaks"; truncated line ends mark the couplet and quatrain boundaries; but "any effort to create salience must do violence to the realization of the grid" and one cannot make several levels salient at once. — [Hayes & Schuh notes, Quatrain I](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12QuatrainI.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL (intelligibility, 27 listeners): words set with stress matching the metre were recognised better than mismatched ones (χ² = 5.24, p = .01 one-tailed); syllabic settings were recognised 84% of the time against 77% for melismatic settings (χ² = 4.80, p = .03). — [EMR article following Collister & Huron 2008 (title page not captured by the fetch; believed to be Johnson, Huron & Collister 2014, Empirical Musicology Review 9)](https://www.osu.tests.sfulib4.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/EMR/article/view/3729/3619)

### Inferences
- A hook is "spotlit" by three things the sources agree on: it starts front-heavy on a strong bar; it is preceded by a temporal break (the right-edge silence of the previous line); and it ends on a long note. The engine's fault (a) removed all three at once: no break before, a late (back-heavy) entry, and by implication less room for a long last note.
- The cheapest reliable rule: the line before a title line must finish by the downbeat (or first strong beat) of its last bar, hold or rest through that bar, and leave at most a one- or two-note pickup for the title line. If the preceding line has too many syllables to do that in its slot, lengthen that line's slot or raise its density; never move the title line.
- A title line with five stresses in seven syllables is slow by nature: each stress wants a strong beat. In 6/8 (two strong beats a bar) five stresses need three bars with the last stress held, or four bars with a full bar of sustain; setting it syllabically in eighths would put stresses on weak eighths, which the stress-matching result above says costs intelligibility.
- Since stress-matched and syllabic settings are measurably easier to understand, the hook line is the place to enforce both strictly, and to spend melisma elsewhere.

### Gaps
- The "power positions" articles (howtowritesongs.org, which redirects to a domain the session could not fetch) could not be read; I can only confirm from search-result titles that the term is in use in lyric-writing pedagogy and is associated with Pat Pattison's teaching. Its usual statement (first and last lines of a section are the strongest positions) is therefore not cited here.
- No empirical test of rests or held notes before hooks, and no measurement of the gap before title lines in any corpus, was found. Perricone, Citron and Webb were not reachable.
- Penn's underlying Hit Songs Deconstructed figures (actual percentages for first-line vs last-line titles) were not in the BMI article.

## 4. Syllables per bar and notes per beat typical of 3/4 and 6/8; how fast syllables can come before a line is crowded

### Takeaway
I found no published syllables-per-bar norms for 3/4, 6/8 or 12/8 in any genre, and no measured syllable-rate ceiling for sung intelligibility. What the sources do give is a structural limit: at most three unstressed syllables between consecutive strong beats, with squeezing below the weakest grid level strongly avoided.

### Cited Findings
- EMPIRICAL/MODEL (folk verse): between two strong beats the grid has three intermediate positions, so "up to three stressless syllables" fit; "more than three stressless syllables between two stressed ones" forces an "Extra Weak" row, and the constraint against filling it is ranked near the top of the grammar. The commonest line has one unstressed syllable per strong beat (eight syllables, four beats). — [Hayes 2005 (PDF)](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL: sung words are much harder to identify than spoken ones: a 75% drop in intelligibility and 7.3 times as many lexical identification errors; three quarters of phonetic errors involved consonants (Collister & Huron 2008, as reported in the follow-up study). In the follow-up, sung intelligibility ranged from 78% to 87% across singers; familiar words were identified 83% of the time, archaic words 48%. — [EMR follow-up to Collister & Huron](https://www.osu.tests.sfulib4.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/EMR/article/view/3729/3619)
- EMPIRICAL: song is produced more slowly than speech across languages (D = 1.6). — [Ozaki et al. 2024](https://research.gold.ac.uk/36542/1/Ozaki_2024.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL (rock, 4/4): tempo in the 80-song corpus ran from 58 to 234 BPM (mean 117.2, median 114; 75% of songs between 60 and 140). Sixteenth-level placement of stressed syllables drops off sharply above 120 BPM, i.e. writers stop using the fastest subdivision for stressed syllables as tempo rises. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- USER-SUPPLIED DATUM (from the brief, not from a source I found): popular waltz lead sheets run about 2.3 notes a bar with one bar in five a single held note; the engine wrote about 3.5.

### Inferences
- Hayes's limit translated to compound time: in 6/8 the strong beats are the two dotted quarters, with two eighth positions between them. So the plain capacity is three syllables per beat, six per bar, and anything denser needs sixteenths, which corresponds to the "extra weak" level the folk grammar avoids. A sensible ceiling for ordinary lines is six syllables per 6/8 bar, with typical lines well below that because most beats carry two syllables (quarter plus eighth) or one.
- 13 syllables in three bars of 6/8 is 4.3 a bar and fits under that ceiling, but only by using 13 of the 18 eighth positions, leaving no line-final note longer than a beat or so and no rest. The line is not too fast; it is too long for a slot that must also contain its own ending. Four bars gives 3.25 a bar with a bar and a half free.
- A useful engine measure is therefore not syllables per bar alone but "free tail": the number of beats between the onset of the last syllable and the end of the slot. The sources imply it should normally be at least one strong beat (Hayes's cadence "3": fourth beat empty) and, before a hook, a full bar less the hook's pickup.
- In 3/4, by the same mapping (one strong beat a bar, up to two subdivisions between), the plain ceiling is three syllables a bar in quarters, and the typical value is two; this agrees with the user's 2.3 figure once held bars are included.
- For slow 12/8 soul or rock ballads I would expect the same per-beat limits (up to three syllables per dotted-quarter beat, more only in melismatic or conversational delivery), but I have no source for it.

### Gaps
- No syllable-rate threshold (syllables per second) for intelligible singing was found. The intelligibility studies I reached varied melisma, stress match, word frequency and vocal style but not rate.
- No per-genre syllables-per-bar or notes-per-beat table for folk, hymn, Tin Pan Alley, country waltz or 6/8 and 12/8 ballads was found; de Clercq & Temperley's corpus and its derivatives are overwhelmingly 4/4 (notes outside 4/4 were under 0.5% in the Tan et al. sample).
- The Tan et al. paper's regression table and its prose disagree on the sign of the tempo effect on sixteenth-level syncopation (positive coefficients, "decreases" in the text, per the fetch summary). I have reported only the "sudden decrease above 120 BPM" statement; check the paper before quoting coefficients.

## 5. Alternating long and short lines (common metre 8.6.8.6, ballad stanza) in triple and compound time

### Takeaway
The short line is given the same time as the long line: its three stresses occupy the first three of four strong beats and the fourth beat is silent or sustained. This equal-slot, right-truncated setting (pattern 4343) is, with 4444, by far the commonest quatrain type in the one large counted corpus.

### Cited Findings
- EMPIRICAL (Appalachian folk-song quatrains, Hayes & MacEachern data as tabulated in course notes): quatrain types by count: 4444: 203; 4343: 188; 4G4G: 38; 4443: 35; F3F3: 29; 3f33f3: 28; G3G3: 26; 43f43f: 21; 3343: 6. Types the theory does not predict total 26 (e.g. 4433: 6; 4344: 3). The digits name the cadence of each line on a four-beat grid. — [Hayes & Schuh notes, Quatrain I](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12QuatrainI.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL: cadence "4" fills all four beats; "3" leaves the fourth beat empty; "3f" is a three-beat line with a feminine (unstressed) ending; "G" is a further truncated type; degree of truncation, and hence salience of the boundary, is ranked G, 3f, 3. Burling is cited for "the actual rendition of empty beats that come after short lines, in quasi-isochronous recitation". — [Hayes & Schuh notes, Quatrain I](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12QuatrainI.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL: couplets are made perceptually separate by truncations at the ends of the second and fourth lines; the silent beats are an "obligatory pause, preserving the isochronous rhythm". — [Hayes & MacEachern 1996 (PDF)](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachern1996AreThereLinesInFolkPoetry.pdf)
- EMPIRICAL: the same device can mark different levels: 3333 makes each line salient, 4343 each couplet, 4443 the whole quatrain. 3343 (short metre) is tolerated while 4333 is "completely out"; long-last patterns are preferred. — [Hayes & Schuh notes, Quatrain I](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12QuatrainI.pdf)
- DEFINITIONS: common metre is 8.6.8.6, alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter; long metre 8.8.8.8; short metre 6.6.8.6. — [Wikipedia, Metre (hymn)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_(hymn))
- CONTEXT: in accentual ballad verse "The number of syllables in each line is not important for the rhythm, but the stresses follow a pattern"; the ballad quatrain is linked to the fourteener (seven iambic feet). The page does not discuss how the short line is sung. — [EBBA, Ballad Measure in Print](https://ebba.english.ucsb.edu/page/ballad-measure-in-print)

### Inferences
- Equal bars, not unequal. An 8.6.8.6 stanza is two fourteeners, each really eight strong beats with the last one silent. In 3/4 at one stress a bar that is four bars for the eight-syllable line and four for the six-syllable line, whose third stress lands on the downbeat of bar three and is held, with bar four free for the remainder of the hold, a rest, and the next line's pickup. In 6/8 at two stresses a bar it is two bars and two bars, the short line ending on the first beat of its second bar. From general knowledge (not verified here), NEW BRITAIN ("Amazing Grace") in 3/4 is laid out exactly this way, sixteen bars for the stanza.
- The engine's 2/3/4-bar menu should therefore pick slots by stress count rounded up to the binary size, not by syllable count: a three-stress line gets the same slot as its four-stress partner. A three-bar slot for the short line of a 4343 stanza in 3/4 would delete the silent beat that marks the couplet, and is the "unequal bars" option that the counted corpus does not support as the norm.
- The silent or sustained fourth beat of each short line is also the structural place where room for the following line's pickup already exists. A stanza whose title is its second or fourth line (a three-stress line after a four-stress line) is the hard case: the long line fills its four beats and leaves only its final weak positions for the pickup. That is acceptable for a one-syllable pickup; for a hook that must start cleanly on the bar, the preceding four-stress line should end with a masculine cadence on beat four and no extra trailing syllables.
- 4343 and 4444 together are 391 of the quatrains listed; types with an early short line (3343) are rare and 4333 is absent. So shortening is a closing gesture: put the short, held line second in the pair.

### Gaps
- The exact definition of cadence "G" (Green O) could not be recovered from the pages I reached; the course notes give the ranking but not the beat-by-beat realisation, and the 1998 Language article (Hayes & MacEachern, Quatrain Form in English Folk Verse) is paywalled at muse.jhu.edu. In the published account it is the type where the third stress is prolonged across the fourth beat, but I could not confirm that wording here.
- The folk corpus does not record the notated metre of each tune, so there is no count of how 4343 stanzas are distributed between duple, triple and compound time, nor of how often the fourth beat is a held note versus a rest.
- No hymnological source on triple-time common-metre tunes (e.g. the long-short "half note, quarter note" iambic pattern of tunes such as NEW BRITAIN or ST. COLUMBA) was fetched; the hymn-tune tutorial at hymntune.library.illinois.edu and hymnary.org appeared in search results but were not read.
