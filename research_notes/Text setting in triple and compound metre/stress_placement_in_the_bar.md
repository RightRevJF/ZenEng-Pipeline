# Stress placement in the bar: text-setting in triple (3/4) and compound (6/8, 12/8) metre

Scope note for the report writer. The headline result of this search is negative: I found no peer-reviewed corpus study that reports where stressed syllables fall, beat by beat, in English 3/4 or 6/8 songs. The main English text-setting corpora either analyse 4/4 only or force binary rhythm. The engine's own figures (538 lead sheets in 3/4: 65% of stressed syllables on beat one, 28% on beats two or three, 7% off the beat; six 6/8 hymns: 52% on the first main beat, 33% on the second) therefore have no published benchmark to be checked against. What the literature does supply is (a) the general principle and its strength, (b) which mismatches are tolerated, (c) effect sizes for intelligibility, and (d) 4/4 comparison numbers. Items marked "craft advice" are pedagogy, not empirical findings.

## 1. Metrical hierarchy of 3/4 and 6/8, and how strongly stress aligns with it in sung English

### Takeaway
Theory and corpus work agree that stressed syllables are preferentially set on metrically strong positions and that ternary metres (3/4, 6/8) show ternary strong-weak-weak alternation, but no source I could read gives numeric per-beat weights for 3/4 or 6/8 or a per-beat stress distribution for those metres. The stress-to-strong-position rule is strict for word-internal (lexical) stress and looser for phrasal stress.

### Cited Findings
- Empirical. Palmer & Kelly (1992) state that binary metres (2/4, 4/4) show binary alternation and ternary metres (3/4, 6/8) show ternary alternation of strong and weak; they report no counts split by metre type. Their corpora were vocal themes from 14 Gilbert & Sullivan operettas plus poems set by nine composers. — [Palmer & Kelly 1992, J. Memory and Language](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- Empirical. In Palmer & Kelly's Gilbert & Sullivan sample, compound words took the metrical accent on the first syllable in 43 of 48 cases, and two-word phrases took it on the second word in 79 of 95 cases (chi-square(1) = 66.6, p < .01). In the art-song sample, compounds were 96 of 105 first-syllable, but phrases were only 67 of 158 second-word (chi-square(1) = 33.5, p < .01). Percentages derived from those counts: about 90% and 83% (G&S), 91% and 42% (art song). — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- Empirical. Singers lengthen syllables that fall on metrical accents: musical accent effect F(1,9) = 23.5, p < .01, and linguistic accent effect F(1,9) = 18.0, p < .01, with no interaction (10 vocalists). So metre and stress add independently in performance. — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- Empirical. Hayes & Kaun (1996) had ten native speakers chant 670 lines of English folk song (Sharp collection) to a binary grid of 16 positions with four strong positions per line. They report that Stress Matching is "obeyed rather strictly", and that averaged stress profiles "closely reflect the metrical pattern" for all but one consultant. Each line received on average 3.41 different settings, and the most popular setting drew on average 6.7 of 11 possible votes. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Empirical, and important for this project: Hayes & Kaun treated ternary rhythms as out of scope. Consultants who volunteered ternary settings were corrected until they produced binary ones. The largest English chanting data set therefore says nothing direct about 3/4 or 6/8. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Theory. Hayes (2005/2009, "Textsetting as Constraint Conflict") models English text-setting as four competing goals: match stress to strong position, avoid long lapses (stretches with no syllable onset), avoid extreme syllable compression, and align phonological phrase boundaries with line boundaries. Stressed syllables may land in weak positions when that avoids a long lapse. Data: 346 folk-verse lines with the modal setting from nine speakers. — [Hayes, Textsetting as Constraint Conflict](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)
- Theory. Kiparsky (2006) states the correspondence constraints as "a Strong position must contain a stress peak" and "A Weak position cannot contain a stress peak", and says that in text-setting "stressed syllables, especially of polysyllabic words, are preferentially aligned with Strong musical beats". — [Kiparsky 2006, A modular metrics for folk verse](https://web.stanford.edu/~kiparsky/Papers/Metrics/LI06_Kiparsky_update.pdf)
- Theory. Kiparsky also notes an asymmetry between verse and music that bears on triple metre: "Ternary measures, ubiquitous in music, are in verse constructed from binary ones by beat-splitting", whereas in music and dance they are "often irreducibly ternary". — [Kiparsky 2006](https://web.stanford.edu/~kiparsky/Papers/Metrics/LI06_Kiparsky_update.pdf)
- Empirical (secondary report). Palmer & Krumhansl (1990) found that note-onset frequency by metrical position in classical keyboard music (Bach, Mozart, Brahms, Shostakovich) correlates strongly with the theoretical accent pattern in 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 and 6/8, and that listeners' goodness-of-fit ratings reflect the same hierarchy, with musicians showing deeper differentiation than non-musicians. Huron & Ommen (2006) found a similar correlation in 1,537 Essen folk songs. — [London, Polak & Jacoby 2016](https://core.ac.uk/download/83231804.pdf); [van der Weij, Pearce & Honing 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)
- Caveat. The assumption that metrical accent is proportional to onset frequency is challenged by Holzapfel (2015) and London et al. (2016) for non-Western repertoires. — [van der Weij, Pearce & Honing 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)
- Empirical. A forced-choice experiment (Dutch listeners, four-beat pattern) found matched stress-prominence settings preferred over mismatched ones, with the favoured member of a pair chosen in about 73 to 75% of trials. A stressed syllable in a weak position was penalised more than an unstressed syllable in a strong position. — [De Castro-Arrazola 2018, ch. 7](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2951725/view)
- Craft advice. A popular songwriting explainer counts 3/4 as accent on beat 1 only, and 6/8 as six eighths with accents on 1 and 4; it calls the two "truly interchangeable" in notation. — [iZotope, Time signatures explained part 2](https://www.izotope.com/community/blog/time-signatures-explained-part-2-writing-songs-in-3-4-and-6-8)

### Inferences
- The engine's 65% (3/4) and 52% + 33% (6/8) are consistent in direction with every source above, but nothing published says whether 65% is high or low for 3/4. The engine's own corpus is the best available target distribution, and it is arguably more data than the literature holds on this specific question.
- In 6/8 the two dotted-quarter beats together carry 85% of stresses in the hymn sample. That matches the standard two-level description (beat 1 strongest, beat 4 secondary, the other four eighths weak). A sensible engine weighting is therefore two-tiered in 6/8 and one-tiered in 3/4, where beats 2 and 3 are equal in the standard grid. The equal status of beats 2 and 3 in 3/4 is textbook Lerdahl & Jackendoff, but I did not retrieve a citable page for it in this session.
- Because the asymmetry found by De Castro-Arrazola is that stress in a weak slot is worse than a weak syllable in a strong slot, the engine's penalty for a stressed syllable off beat one should be larger than its penalty for an unstressed syllable on beat one.

### Gaps
- No per-beat stress distribution for English 3/4 or 6/8 song in any peer-reviewed source I could access. Palmer & Kelly do not split by metre; Hayes & Kaun exclude ternary; Nichols et al. and Tan et al. exclude non-4/4.
- Palmer & Krumhansl (1990) numeric profiles for 3/4 and 6/8 (onset frequencies and ratings per position) were not retrievable; only secondary descriptions were read. The primary paper is in J. Exp. Psych.: Human Perception and Performance 16(4).
- Halle & Lerdahl (1993), Dell & Halle (2009), Proto & Dell (2013), and Lerdahl & Jackendoff's metrical preference rules were only seen through citations in other papers, not read directly.
- The final 20,000 characters of Hayes & Kaun (significance tests section) were not read.

## 2. Differences between metres (duple, triple, compound) and between genres

### Takeaway
I found no study that directly compares strictness of stress-beat matching across metres. The clear, quantified contrast is between eras and genres in 4/4: nineteenth-century song has essentially no stressed syllables on weak eighth positions, while rock anticipates about a quarter to a third of them.

### Cited Findings
- Empirical. Tan, Lustig & Temperley (2019) analysed 80 Rolling Stone corpus songs (23,032 notes in 4/4). Of 10,433 stressed syllables, 2,382 were syncopated at the eighth-note level (syncopation quotient .228); at the sixteenth level the quotient was .105; combined .333. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019, Music Perception](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Empirical. A comparison corpus of ten nineteenth-century English songs (1,397 notes) had an eighth-level syncopation quotient of 0. The difference from rock was chi-square(1) = 240.1, p < .0001. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Empirical. Rock syncopations are mostly anticipations: 77.2% of eighth-level syncopations fall on the eighth just before a strong quarter beat, and 74.0% of sixteenth-level ones just before a strong position. Syncopation is defined as a stressed syllable on a weak beat with no syllable on or before the following stronger beat. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Empirical. That study excluded 17 of 100 candidate songs for having time signatures other than 4/4 or 2/4, so triple and compound rock songs are unmeasured. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Theory. Temperley (1999) holds that in rock, stressed syllables set on weak beats are heard as belonging on the following strong beat: "we retain, on principle, the assumption that stressed syllables should occur on strong beats." Liberman adds that some cases are better read as a competing 3+3+2 grouping than as displacement, and gives a James Taylor stanza in 4/4 where only 1 of 11 strong syllables is on a beat and 8 are off it. — [Liberman, Language Log 2007](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/~myl/languagelog/archives/005154.html)
- Empirical. Nichols et al. (2009) analysed 679 pop, rock, R&B, country and jazz lead sheets, 4/4 bars only. Base rates: 46% of syllables primary-stressed, 48% unstressed. Stress and metric position were dependent (p < .0001), with downbeat and half-bar positions favouring stressed syllables and non-stopwords; exact per-position values appear only in figures. — [Nichols, Morris, Basu & Raphael 2009, ISMIR](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Empirical, low confidence. The only study found that includes 3/4 alongside 4/4 is a non-peer-reviewed preprint on 56 piano-book songs (26 in 3/4, 30 in 4/4). Pooled across both metres it reports about 60% of stressed syllables on strong beats and 40% on weak beats, with P(downbeat | stressed syllable) about 0.5, and about 77 to 81% of "keywords" on strong beats. It gives no 3/4-only beat percentages, and its own figures are internally inconsistent (80.8% versus 76.8% for the same quantity). — [Liao, Liao & Zhang, arXiv 2412.04202](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04202)
- Empirical. Stress coding base rates in rock: 43.5% primary stress, 1.8% secondary, 50.3% unstressed, 4.4% non-words. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Language comparison. Gordon et al. note that Spanish and French may tolerate stress-beat misalignment more than English does. — [Gordon, Magne & Large 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00352/full)

### Inferences
- The engine's 7% off-beat share in 3/4 sits between nineteenth-century song (0% at eighth level in 4/4) and rock (23 to 33%). That is plausible for a mixed popular 3/4 corpus that leans towards older and folk-like repertoire, and it suggests off-beat stress should be a style parameter, not a constant: near zero for hymn and folk settings, higher for pop and rock in 6/8 or 12/8.
- If rock practice carries over to compound metre, off-beat stresses in 6/8 and 12/8 pop should cluster on the eighth immediately before a dotted-quarter beat (positions 3 and 6 of the bar) and resolve by tying over it. This is an extrapolation from 4/4 data; no compound-metre measurement supports it.
- The pooled 60% strong-beat figure in the Liao preprint is close to the engine's 65% beat-one figure, but the preprint's "strong beat" definition and pooling make the comparison loose.

### Gaps
- No empirical comparison of stress-matching strictness between duple, triple and compound metres.
- No genre-split figures for 3/4 (hymn versus Tin Pan Alley waltz versus country waltz) or for 6/8 and 12/8 (hymn versus doo-wop or rock ballad). The search for analytical work on Tin Pan Alley waltz prosody returned only craft blogs.
- Temperley (1999) and Temperley & Temperley (2013, French versus English) were not read directly.

## 3. Content versus function words, primary versus secondary stress, and which mismatches are tolerated

### Takeaway
Lexical stress inside polysyllabic words is close to inviolable; the stress of monosyllables and phrase-level prominence is negotiable, and function words gravitate to weak positions. Mismatches are tolerated when they avoid a long gap, when they repeat the first stanza's alignment, or when they are short anticipations of a strong beat.

### Cited Findings
- Empirical. Word-internal stress sequences are set more strictly than sequences spanning a word boundary, in the original folk settings and for nine of ten consultants. Word-final, word-internal mismatches are "avoided without exception" in the original settings and by seven consultants. After accounting for known exceptions only about 4 to 8 lexical inversions in the corpus contradict the theory. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Empirical. Strictness rises towards the ends of larger phrases. Rising-stress sequences at the end of a clitic group were mismatched 334 times against 2,639 matched (about 11% by my calculation); at the end of an intonational phrase 12 against 1,102 (about 1%). The spoken-verse licence for inversion at the start of a phrase largely fails to appear in sung settings. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Theory. "Lexical stresses of polysyllabic words are more salient than those of monosyllabic words." Kiparsky ranks stressed syllables of polysyllables as most prominent and unstressed syllables of polysyllables as least prominent, with monosyllables in between. — [Kiparsky 2006](https://web.stanford.edu/~kiparsky/Papers/Metrics/LI06_Kiparsky_update.pdf)
- Empirical. Phrase-level stress is honoured less reliably than word-level stress: in Palmer & Kelly's art-song sample compounds matched 91% of the time but two-word phrases only 42%. For adjective-noun phrases, 66 of 91 determiner-initial phrases aligned the adjective with the strong beat, and 31 of 56 clause-initial phrases aligned the noun (chi-square(1) = 10.3, p < .01); the authors call this evidence weaker. — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- Empirical. In 4/4 pop lead sheets, 59% of words are stopwords; downbeat and half-bar positions and melodic peaks favour non-stopwords (each p < .0001). 55% of notes on primary-stressed syllables are long, and 55% of notes on unstressed syllables are short. — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Theory. An empty or sustained position counts as weaker than any unstressed syllable (the "Zero Provision"), which explains where mismatched stress is most restricted: a stressed syllable just before a held or empty strong position is a worse fault than one followed by a filled position. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Theory. Positions with no new syllable tend to be filled by prolonging the syllables that are naturally longest in English. Ryan (2022), on 2,371 English popular songs, confirms that setting to a discrete grid is sensitive to natural duration as well as categorical weight, even after controlling for phrase-final lengthening. — [Hayes, Textsetting in English and German handout](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/11TextsettingInEnglishandGerman.pdf); [Ryan 2022, English Language & Linguistics](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-language-and-linguistics/article/abs/syllable-weight-and-natural-duration-in-textsetting-popular-music-in-english/50F8885341914C37D6F1BD22A632A0B6)
- Theory. Later stanzas inherit the first stanza's grid alignment, so mismatches in verses two onwards are tolerated where the first verse fitted ("stanzaic responsion"; Sacred Harp inversion is given as a clear case). — [Hayes, Textsetting in English and German handout](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/11TextsettingInEnglishandGerman.pdf)
- Empirical. Scotch-snap and dotted-rhythm choices in the chanting data were not significantly consultant-dependent (p = .104 to .295), and 10.9% of settings (760 of 6,998) were "headless", meaning the line's first strong position was left unfilled. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Craft advice. Pattison: primary stresses belong on the downbeat, secondary stresses on weaker strong positions; pronouns, articles and relative pronouns should not land on downbeats. He criticises a setting that put a word's secondary stresses on downbeats and its primary stress on beat 3 as "turning it upside down" (examples are in 4/4). — [Pat Pattison, Setting words to music](https://www.patpattison.com/setting-words-to-music)
- Craft advice. One-syllable nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs go on strong positions; articles, prepositions, conjunctions and most pronouns on weak ones; in a line of monosyllables the writer chooses which take the stress. — [Songcage, Fitting lyrics to a melody](https://songcage.com/blog/fitting-lyrics-to-a-melody/)
- Craft advice. Good prosody will not put unimportant syllables on the beat or give them accents or long notes (citing Adams 2025, Berklee Press). — [Wikipedia, Prosody (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosody_(music))

### Inferences
- A workable penalty ordering for the engine, from worst to mildest: (1) unstressed syllable of a polysyllabic word on beat one while that word's stressed syllable sits on a weaker position; (2) primary stress weaker than the same word's secondary stress; (3) stressed monosyllabic content word off the strong beat next to a function word on it; (4) stressed monosyllable on a weak beat with the strong beat empty or held; (5) content monosyllable on beat 2 or 3 with another content word on beat one, which is not really a fault.
- In 3/4 the common tolerated "mismatch" is a stressed monosyllable on beat 3 as a weighted pickup into the next bar, and in 6/8 a stress on the second dotted-quarter beat. Both leave the bar's first beat occupied by something at least as strong, so they pass the relative test in the first three levels above. This fits the engine's 28% and 33% figures, but the sources do not discuss beat 3 of 3/4 specifically.
- Because Hayes ranks lapse avoidance above strict matching, the engine should prefer a stressed syllable on beat 2 or 3 over leaving a bar and a half with no syllable onset.

### Gaps
- Nothing found on hemiola text-setting in English song (two bars of 3/4 stressed as three twos) or on its tolerance.
- No source quantifies secondary-stress placement by beat. Secondary stress is only 1.8% of syllables in the rock corpus, so data are thin.
- Nothing empirical on the Scotch snap in triple or compound metre.

## 4. Mapping poetic metres (iambic, trochaic, dactylic, anapaestic; common metre) onto 3/4 and 6/8

### Takeaway
Sources describe the poetic side well and the musical side hardly at all: no source read here states the mapping from feet to bars in 3/4 or 6/8. The mapping below under Inferences is derived from the grid logic and should be labelled as such.

### Cited Findings
- Common metre (8.6.8.6) is a quatrain alternating iambic tetrameter and iambic trimeter, so four stresses then three; long metre is 8.8.8.8 iambic tetrameter; short metre is 6.6.8.6. Most hymns in 8.7.8.7 are trochaic. Dactylic patterns are an occasional alternative ("Great Is Thy Faithfulness" is the example given). A text can be sung to any tune of the same metre only if the poetic foot also matches. — [Wikipedia, Metre (hymn)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_(hymn))
- Iambic is the commonest English hymn pattern, trochaic second. Dactylic and anapaestic feet are rare in classic hymnody; the anapaestic pattern gives a light, skipping feel that was traditionally avoided for serious texts, with "Immortal, Invisible, God Only Wise" an exception whose slower tempo masks it. — [Religious Affections, Poetic meter in Christian hymnody](https://religiousaffections.org/featured/poetic-meter-christian-hymnody/)
- English folk-song lines are set to a grid of four strong positions; a "three-beat" line is a four-beat line with a silent fourth beat, and the pattern of such truncations is fixed by stanza type. This is the grid behind ballad and common metre. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Ballad quatrains (4-3-4-3 beats) can be printed as fourteener couplets, and 3-3-4-3 quatrains correspond to poulter's measure. In verse, ternary feet arise from binary ones by splitting a beat. — [Kiparsky 2006](https://web.stanford.edu/~kiparsky/Papers/Metrics/LI06_Kiparsky_update.pdf)
- Gordon et al.'s stimuli show the basic binary case in practice: six-syllable trochaic or iambic sentences sung one syllable per quarter note with the strong beat on alternate syllables. — [Gordon, Magne & Large 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00352/full)

### Inferences
These follow from the strong-weak-weak grid and the cited foot definitions; they are not quoted from a source.
- Dactylic (strong-weak-weak) is the native fit for 3/4: one foot per bar, three syllables per bar at one syllable per beat, starting on the downbeat. In 6/8 it gives two feet per bar, six syllables per bar.
- Anapaestic (weak-weak-strong) is the same stream offset: a two-syllable pickup on beats 2 and 3, stress on the next downbeat. Amphibrachic (weak-strong-weak) is the one-beat pickup version. All three put every stress on beat one in 3/4 and on beats 1 and 4 in 6/8.
- Iambic verse fits triple time by lengthening: stressed syllable two beats (half note), unstressed one beat (quarter), with a one-beat pickup. That gives one foot and two syllables per bar in 3/4, so an 8-syllable line takes four bars and a 6-syllable line takes three bars plus a bar of held note or rest, making common metre a 16-bar strophe in 3/4. In 6/8 the same long-short pattern gives two feet per bar and common metre in eight bars.
- Trochaic verse fits the same way without the pickup (long on beat 1, short on beat 3).
- On these pure schemes 100% of stresses would land on beat one in 3/4. The engine's observed 65% therefore implies that about a third of stresses come from denser settings, where two stressed syllables share a bar, or from weighted pickups. A plausible reading of the 6/8 hymn split (52% versus 33%) is the long-short iambic scheme with two feet per bar, giving a near-even split between the two main beats, tilted to the first by line endings and held notes.
- Notes-per-bar expectations that follow: about 2 syllables per bar for iambic or trochaic texts in 3/4, about 3 for ternary feet in 3/4, and 4 to 6 per bar in 6/8.

### Gaps
- No citable source found that states the foot-to-bar mapping for 3/4 or 6/8, the half-plus-quarter convention for iambic hymns in triple time, or typical syllables per bar. The claims under Inferences need a hymnology or text-setting reference (for example a hymn tune survey by time signature) before being presented as established.
- No frequency data on which hymn metres are most often set in 3/4 or 6/8.
- Dell & Halle's and Halle & Lerdahl's treatment of pickups (anacrusis) was not read.

## 5. Quantitative figures: downbeat share, notes per bar, and effect sizes for singability, memorability, intelligibility

### Takeaway
The measured perceptual benefit of stress-beat matching is real but small: about ten percentage points of word intelligibility (Phi = .11) and a few tens of milliseconds in lexical processing. The strongest quantitative statements concern how consistently composers and singers follow the rule, not how much listeners suffer when it is broken.

### Cited Findings
- Intelligibility. Sung words in stress-matched settings were identified correctly in 183 of 216 trials against 163 of 216 for mismatched settings (about 85% versus 75%); chi-square = 5.24, p = .01 one-tailed, Phi = .11. Split by word type the effect was marginal (iambic p = .08, trochaic p = .06). — [Johnson, Huron & Collister 2014, Empirical Musicology Review](https://core.ac.uk/download/20329824.pdf)
- Intelligibility, for scale. In the same study melisma cost about seven points (84% syllabic versus 77% melismatic, Phi = .09), while word familiarity dwarfed both (Phi = .35). The authors call the study exploratory, with some hypotheses resting on 8 or 16 stimuli. — [Johnson, Huron & Collister 2014](https://core.ac.uk/download/20329824.pdf)
- Processing. After sung sentences with misaligned stress, lexical decision on pseudoword targets was slower (659 ms versus 639 ms after well-aligned), while word targets were not slower (565 ms versus 586 ms); the alignment-by-lexicality interaction was F(2,30) = 4.12, p = .026 (16 participants). Accuracy stayed at 93 to 98% in all conditions. Misaligned primes also changed induced alpha and beta activity on the following target (p = .01 and p = .002). — [Gordon, Magne & Large 2011, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00352/full)
- Note on that study. A later review summarises it as "executed more quickly and more accurately in the well-aligned trials" and criticises the design because alignment differed for every word in a trial. The reaction-time table shows the benefit only for pseudowords, so the summary overstates it. The stimuli were isochronous quarter notes in a binary pattern, not triple metre. — [De Castro-Arrazola 2018](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2951725/view); [Gordon, Magne & Large 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00352/full)
- Learning (secondary report). Children learned songs with misaligned settings less easily than well-aligned ones (Gingold & Abravanel 1987), and emphasising linguistic or musical rhythm aids adult song learning (Wallace & Rubin 1988; Wallace 1994; Purnell-Webb & Speelman 2008). No effect sizes were given in the source read. — [Gordon, Magne & Large 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00352/full)
- Preference. Listeners chose the stress-matched setting over the mismatched one in about 73 to 75% of paired trials. — [De Castro-Arrazola 2018](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2951725/view)
- Agreement among lay setters. The modal setting of a folk line drew 6.7 of 11 votes on average, and each line had 3.41 distinct settings, so there is usually one preferred setting and two or three acceptable alternatives. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Comparison figures in 4/4. Stressed syllables off the strong eighth position: 0% in nineteenth-century English song, 22.8% in rock at the eighth level and 33.3% including sixteenths. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Comparison figures, low confidence. In a pooled 3/4 and 4/4 piano-book sample, about 60% of stressed syllables fell on strong beats and about 50% on the downbeat; about 90% of syllables on strong beats were stressed. — [Liao, Liao & Zhang, arXiv 2412.04202](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04202)
- Duration. Bertheau (1994), reported by Hayes & Kaun, found phrasing and stress together predicted sung syllable durations with r-squared = .532 across 100 lines. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)

### Inferences
- The engine's corpus numbers are the only per-beat figures for 3/4 and 6/8 on the table. A reasonable design target is to reproduce them as a distribution (roughly 65 / 28 / 7 in 3/4; roughly 52 / 33 / 15 in 6/8, the last figure being the remainder), with the lexical-stress constraints of section 3 applied as near-hard rules inside that distribution.
- The reverse conditional probably matters more than the forward one. The pooled preprint figure suggests about 90% of syllables on strong beats are stressed while only about 60% of stressed syllables are on strong beats. In engine terms: beat one should almost always carry a stressed syllable (or a held one), but not every stressed syllable needs beat one.
- Given the small intelligibility effect (about ten points, Phi = .11), stress placement is better treated as a naturalness and style constraint than as a clarity requirement. Word familiarity and melisma affect intelligibility as much or more.

### Gaps
- No published share of stressed syllables on the downbeat for 3/4 or 6/8, and no published syllables-per-bar figures for either metre.
- No effect size found for stress-beat alignment on memorability or singability in adults; the child and adult learning studies were only seen as citations.
- Exact per-position conditional probabilities in Nichols et al. are in figures only and were not extracted.
- Hayes (2009) model accuracy figures were not retrievable (the page with the slides was blocked).
