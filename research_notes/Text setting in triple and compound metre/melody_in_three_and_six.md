# Melody in three and six: melodic and rhythmic character of songs in 3/4, 6/8 and 12/8 versus 4/4

Reader's note on evidence quality. The web yielded good sourced material on metre definitions, tempo ranges, dance-tune rhythm cells, and a handful of song analyses. It yielded almost no corpus statistics that split melodic range, interval size, or repetition by metre. Where a point below is the researcher's own craft knowledge and not something read in a fetched source, it is placed under Inferences and marked "(uncited)". Nothing under Cited Findings is from memory. Several obvious sources could not be opened in this session (Wikipedia pages for Hemiola, Sea shanty, and '50s progression were blocked by the fetch allowlist; Grove Music Online is paywalled; the Consensus paper search was out of quota).

## 1. Characteristic bar rhythms of the waltz and of 6/8; hemiola; fast, slow and rock waltzes

### Takeaway
Sources agree on the cells (strong first beat with two light beats in 3/4; quarter-eighth "lilt" or three running eighths per beat in 6/8) and on which dance type uses which cell, but no source found gives frequencies of bar-rhythm patterns in a vocal waltz repertoire. The engine's own 538-lead-sheet corpus is the better evidence for pattern frequency than anything published that this search could reach.

### Cited Findings
- Waltz accent pattern: beat 1 is the heaviest (bass note, low chord tone or kick), beats 2 and 3 are lighter chordal pulses; "eighth-note motion may appear in melodies, fills, ornaments, or bass lines"; performers may lean into beat 2 or stretch time; village, salon, country and Viennese types share the triple base but feel different (descriptive, instructional source) — [Soundbrenner, Waltz rhythm](https://www.soundbrenner.com/blogs/articles/waltz-rhythm)
- In jazz usage "waltz" means any piece in 3/4, danced or not; the slow waltz became the predominant 20th-century ballroom form around 1910, derived from the Valse Boston of the 1870s — [Wikipedia, Waltz (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waltz_(music))
- 3/4 groups six eighths as three twos (one pair per beat); 6/8 groups them as two threes; 6/8 has a "skipping" or "hopping" character against the waltz "oom-pa-pa" (descriptive) — [School of Composition](https://www.schoolofcomposition.com/?p=3298)
- Single jig: 6/8 (less often 12/8) with the bar pattern quarter-eighth, quarter-eighth. Double jig: 6/8 with three eighths on each beat (six running eighths a bar). Slip jig: 9/8. The commonest jig form is two eight-bar parts — [Wikipedia, Jig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jig)
- Single jig "tends to follow the pattern of a quarter note followed by an eighth note" twice per bar, eight bars per part; slide is 12/8, phrased in four beats, four bars per part, often about 150 bpm; there are very few native Irish waltzes, and airs are adapted as waltzes — [FolkWorks, Guide to Irish Tune Types](https://folkworks.org/article/guide-to-irish-tune-types/)
- In Chopin's 17 waltzes the literal oom-pah-pah bar is far from universal: it occupies on average 46% of bars in the "virtuosic" group (range 31% to 72%, one waltz 0%) and 44% in the "sentimental" group (range 9% to 83%). Hemiola appears as a special device in introductions (E major waltz of 1829; Op. 18 bars 183 to 188), not as the default. Single-tone repetition is a favoured melodic device in the virtuosic waltzes (empirical count, but instrumental art music, not song) — [Monteiro, MPhil thesis, Glasgow 2022](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/83309/1/2022MonteiroMPhil.pdf)
- Country music commonly uses swung eighths inside 3/4 (normal, half-time and double-time drum feels all occur), so a country waltz melody that moves in eighths is long-short, not even (framework paper, examples not counts) — [de Clercq, 2017 poster](https://www.midside.com/presentations/declercq_2017_pip_poster.pdf)
- Elliott Smith's "Waltz #1" (indie/rock waltz): spacious melody of long held notes over a piano figure counted "one-trip-let-two-trip-let"; hearable as 3/4 or 6/8 — [Soundfly Flypaper](https://flypaper.soundfly.com/write/exploring-the-lydian-mode-with-elliott-smiths-waltz-1/)

### Inferences
- (uncited) The standard vocal waltz bar cells, in roughly descending order of how often a songwriter meets them in Tin Pan Alley and country waltzes: dotted half (one note a bar, typical at phrase ends and in fast waltzes); half plus quarter (long-short, the commonest "moving" bar); three quarters (the walking or arpeggio bar); quarter plus half (short-long, often with a pickup); dotted quarter, eighth, quarter (the "lilt" bar); and a pickup of one quarter or two eighths before the barline. This ordering is craft knowledge and should be checked against the 538-sheet corpus rather than trusted.
- (uncited) Fast waltzes felt in one (Viennese, many show tunes) carry about one to two melody notes a bar, with many dotted halves and ties across the bar; slow waltzes (country, folk) carry two to four, with eighth-note pickups and dotted-quarter-eighth cells; rock and punk songs in three tend to drive even quarters or even eighths with little of the long-short lilt and place syncopation on the "and" of 2. No source read in this session quantifies these densities.
- The Chopin count (oom-pah-pah in fewer than half the bars) suggests the accompaniment formula should not be treated as a rule for every bar even in the genre's art-music core; for melody the transferable point is that hemiola is an occasional marked event, concentrated at cadences and introductions.
- The engine's finding that density is right only when a line occupies three bars of a four-bar phrase fits the common waltz layout in which the fourth bar is a held note or rest (a dotted half or tied note at the phrase end).

### Gaps
- No published frequency table of bar-rhythm patterns in vocal waltzes or 6/8 songs was found. The engine's corpus should be mined directly for the share of bars in each cell, split by tempo band.
- Hemiola frequency in popular waltz melodies: no data. The Wikipedia Hemiola article and the aural-skills chapter at uen.pressbooks.pub ("3s and 2s: Hemiola and Other Across-the-Beat Rhythms") were found by search but not read.
- "Punk waltz" as a category has no analytic literature that this search could find; only listicles (Complex, American Songwriter, Song Bar) surfaced and were not read.
- Grove Music Online "Waltz" (Lamb) and McKee's "Decorum of the Minuet, Delirium of the Waltz" were not accessible.

## 2. Melodic range and contour in triple and compound metre versus 4/4: corpus evidence

### Takeaway
No corpus study was found that reports range, interval size or triadic outlining separately for triple or compound metre versus 4/4. The general baselines that were found (European folk song median range of 12 semitones) sit below the 14 to 16 semitones in the engine's waltz lead-sheet corpus, which supports treating the engine's 9-semitone, 6-pitch waltz output as too narrow by any yardstick.

### Cited Findings
- Median pitch range in the Essen Folk Song Collection is 12 semitones for the European subset and 17 semitones for the Asian subset; Essen and the MeloSol sight-singing corpus (783 melodies) both show convex (arch) contours most often, then ascending, then descending; Huron's 1996 arch study used 6,251 European Essen melodies (empirical) — [Baker, "MeloSol Corpus", Empirical Musicology Review](https://www.osu.tests.sfulib4.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/EMR/article/view/7645/6267)
- In 1,268 German Essen songs (5,289 phrase pairs), a duration discontinuity (long note or gap) marks about 50% of phrase joints, while a pitch leap marks only about 32%; that is, phrase ends are signalled more by a long note than by a leap (empirical; not split by metre) — [Rodríguez-López and Volk, CMMR 2013](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/289643/MRodriguez-CMMR13-corrected.pdf?sequence=1)
- Rock corpus (200 songs, 194 with melodic transcriptions): scale degree 1 is most frequent, then 5; flat 7 is more common than natural 7 in melodies; the major scale is the commonest pitch set (24 songs), with the "pentatonic union" second (18 songs). The paper reports no range, interval or metre breakdown (empirical) — [Temperley and de Clercq 2013](https://www.midside.com/publications/temperley_declercq_2013.pdf)
- Lullabies: a musicological feature analysis (pitch, pitch range, phrase length, contour) found no single feature that reliably separates lullabies from matched non-lullabies, but songs with prominent descending contours were more often judged lullabies and songs with many contour changes were rejected; 43% of 40 listeners named repetitiveness and 28% simplicity as their cue (empirical, perception study) — [Trehub, Unyk and Trainor 1993](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/1993_Trainor_Unyk.pdf)
- "Norwegian Wood": the hook is a descending line spanning an octave over a static tonic, described as a "downward spiral" not an arch, mixing steps and widening leaps (two jumps down, one up), with a modal flat 7 (analysis) — [Pollack, Notes on "Norwegian Wood"](https://www.icce.rug.nl/~soundscapes/DATABASES/AWP/nw.shtml)

### Inferences
- The engine's corpus figure (median 14 to 16 semitones, 11 distinct pitches across 538 waltz lead sheets) is wider than the Essen European median of 12. Lead sheets of commercial songs generally run wider than folk tunes, so the gap may be a genre effect more than a metre effect; the comparison needed is the same corpus's 4/4 songs.
- (uncited) Waltz melodies are widely described as triadic: with one chord a bar and three beats, three quarters naturally spell root, third, fifth, and the long first note of a bar is normally a chord tone. An engine that restricts a waltz to stepwise motion within a sixth will under-produce both range and distinct pitches; allowing chord arpeggiation across a tenth and octave leaps at phrase starts is the cheapest way to reach 14 to 16 semitones.
- The phrase-joint finding (long notes mark boundaries more than leaps) transfers directly: in 3/4 the boundary marker is a dotted half or a note tied over the bar; in 6/8 it is a dotted quarter or dotted half.

### Gaps
- No metre-split statistics for Essen, hymn-tune, Wikifonia, Hooktheory, Billboard or rock corpora were found. Essen kern files carry metre, so the split is computable locally but was not found published.
- No source was found testing whether triple-metre melodies arpeggiate more than duple ones.
- The ISMIR 2005 paper (archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000049.pdf) and the arXiv paper "Information and motor constraints shape melodic diversity across cultures" (arxiv.org/abs/2408.12635) surfaced in search and may hold relevant numbers; they were not read.

## 3. How much literal repetition waltz and 6/8 melodies have

### Takeaway
High rhythmic repetition is described as a feature of dance-derived triple and compound tunes (jigs repeat one cell per beat for whole parts; each part is played twice), and repetitiveness is the leading cue by which listeners recognise lullabies, but no source found measures repetition in vocal waltzes against 4/4 songs.

### Cited Findings
- Jig types are defined by a single repeating beat cell: quarter-eighth for single jigs, three eighths for double jigs, with two eight-bar parts — [Wikipedia, Jig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jig); [FolkWorks](https://folkworks.org/article/guide-to-irish-tune-types/)
- Repetitiveness was the most cited criterion (43% of listeners) for identifying a lullaby; schematic comparison showed lullabies repeating at shorter spans than matched comparison songs, though this was not quantified — [Trehub, Unyk and Trainor 1993](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/1993_Trainor_Unyk.pdf)
- "Norwegian Wood": the eight-bar hook is stated twice in the 16-bar intro and every verse repeats it; only the bridge contrasts — [Pollack](https://www.icce.rug.nl/~soundscapes/DATABASES/AWP/nw.shtml)
- Halyard shanties alternate a solo line with a fixed refrain, and "rhythm matters more than word sense"; pump shanties may use a longer solo verse and a chorus of four or more lines (descriptive) — [EFDSS, Introduction to English Sea Songs and Shanties](https://media.efdss.org/resourcebank/docs/An_Introduction_To_English_Sea_Songs_and_Shanties_2016.pdf)
- Single-tone repetition and re-use of an opening repeated-note theme in later sections are noted as characteristic in Chopin's virtuosic waltzes — [Monteiro 2022](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/83309/1/2022MonteiroMPhil.pdf)

### Inferences
- (uncited) In sung waltzes the usual scheme is a two-bar or four-bar rhythm repeated exactly while the pitches move in sequence (same shape a step lower or higher), with the fourth statement altered for the cadence. Rhythm repeats much more literally than pitch. This is standard craft description but was not confirmed from a fetched source.
- For the engine: favour one bar-rhythm motif per phrase pair, repeated in at least two of every four bars, and vary pitch by sequence and by chord arpeggiation. That raises distinct-pitch count and range without raising rhythmic entropy.

### Gaps
- No quantitative measure (for example, share of bars whose rhythm equals the bar two or four bars earlier) by metre was found. This should be computed on the 538-sheet corpus and its 4/4 counterpart.

## 4. 6/8 as two beats, 3/4 as three, 12/8 as a slow four, and shuffle 4/4 written as 12/8

### Takeaway
The metres differ in where the beats are, not in how many eighths fill the bar: 3/4 has three beats that split in two, 6/8 two beats that split in three, 12/8 four such beats. In pop and rock the choice between 6/8, 12/8 and swung 4/4 is largely a matter of bar length in seconds and of how even the subdivision is.

### Cited Findings
- Compound metre: the beat divides into three, the beat unit is always a dotted note (dotted quarter in 6/8, 9/8, 12/8); the top number divided by three gives the number of beats; beaming must show the beat — [Open Music Theory, Compound Meters and Time Signatures](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/compound-meters-and-time-signatures/)
- 3/4: strong beat every three quarter beats. 6/8: strong beat every two dotted-quarter beats; eighths beamed in threes — [School of Composition](https://www.schoolofcomposition.com/?p=3298)
- 6/8 accents fall on eighths 1 and 4; 3/4 accents on beat 1 of each bar — [iZotope, Writing Songs in 3/4 and 6/8](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/time-signatures-explained-part-2-writing-songs-in-3-4-and-6-8)
- In a 6/8 drum pattern kick and snare fall on the two main beats with hi-hat on the subdivisions; the threefold subdivision gives lyrics a different prosody from 4/4 and suits "conversational or flowing" vocal lines (craft claim) — [Native Instruments blog](https://www.native-instruments.com/blogs/blog/6-8-time-signature)
- 12/8 is "4/4 time with a 3/4 hidden inside of each beat": a steady four-count, each beat split in three; usable for ballads and for mid and up-tempo grooves (craft claim) — [iZotope, Writing Songs in 12/8](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/time-signatures-explained-part-3-writing-songs-in-12-8)
- Swing eighths are a "quasi-triplet" with the first roughly twice the second, but the ratio varies by piece and player; faster tempos are straighter, slower tempos swing harder; swing is written as straight eighths in 4/4, not as triplets — [Open Music Theory, Swing Rhythms](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/swing-rhythms/)
- De Clercq prefers "4/4 with swung eighths" over 12/8 for shuffles because the swing ratio varies from song to song, and keeps straight 6/8 as the useful compound signature; 2/4, 9/8 and 12/8 are treated as deprecated in his scheme — [de Clercq, 2017 poster](https://www.midside.com/presentations/declercq_2017_pip_poster.pdf)
- De Clercq's "two-second measure": other things equal, listeners and transcribers settle on bars near 2 s. Applied cases: a swung country ballad at about 76 BPM would give 3.15 s bars in 12/8, so 6/8 (1.58 s) is chosen; a slow blues at about 55 BPM is kept in 12/8 because that shows the 12-bar form; "Norwegian Wood" is heard as 3/4 by Pollack, 6/8 by Covach and 12/8 by Everett, with 6/8 giving bars of almost exactly two seconds — [de Clercq, Music Theory Online 22.3 (2016)](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.declercq.php)
- Slow harmonic rhythm in a song in three: "Norwegian Wood" verse is a static tonic for eight bars, and bridge chords last about four bars each — [Pollack](https://www.icce.rug.nl/~soundscapes/DATABASES/AWP/nw.shtml)

### Inferences
- What a composer must respect, derived from the definitions above: in 3/4 the chord normally changes at the barline and the melody's long note starts on beat 1; in 6/8 chords may change on either of the two beats (eighth 1 or 4) and long notes are dotted quarters starting on those beats; in 12/8 chords change on beats 1 and 3 (eighths 1 and 7) or every beat, and a backbeat sits on beats 2 and 4 (eighths 4 and 10). (The backbeat placement is uncited; de Clercq's article does not state it.)
- A melody note of a plain quarter starting on eighth 3 or eighth 5 of a 6/8 bar cuts across the beat and reads as 3/4; done for two beats it is a hemiola. Conversely a 3/4 bar filled dotted-quarter plus dotted-quarter reads as 6/8. The engine should treat both as marked devices, permitted mainly approaching a cadence.
- Because swing ratio flattens as tempo rises, a "12/8" rendering is accurate for slow shuffles and ballads, and progressively wrong for fast shuffles, which are better generated as 4/4 with a swing parameter.
- A fast 3/4 with one chord per bar and four-bar phrases is perceptually close to a slow 12/8 (four bars of 3/4 equal one bar of 12/8): the three-way disagreement over "Norwegian Wood" is an example. The engine can therefore reuse 12/8 phrase logic for fast waltzes felt in one.

### Gaps
- No source read states where harmonic changes statistically fall in 3/4, 6/8 or 12/8 songs. The one-chord-per-bar waltz norm is common knowledge but was not confirmed in a fetched source (the Wikipedia waltz page text was truncated at that sentence).
- The '50s progression article (doo-wop harmonic rhythm) was blocked and not read.

## 5. Documented examples by metre and style, with what the melody does

### Takeaway
Only a few examples were confirmed with an actual analysis in this session; most sources only list titles by metre, and several lists contradict each other or common transcription practice, so title lists should be treated as leads, not data.

### Cited Findings
- Rock/folk-rock in three: "Norwegian Wood" (Beatles, 1965). Listed as 3/4 (6/8); "most lilting and very un-waltz-like"; eight-bar descending-octave hook over a tonic drone with flat 7; bridge in parallel minor, still mostly descending — [Pollack](https://www.icce.rug.nl/~soundscapes/DATABASES/AWP/nw.shtml)
- Indie waltz: "Waltz #1" (Elliott Smith). Lydian colour from a sustained raised fourth as the first sung note; long held notes; I, vi, IV harmony — [Soundfly Flypaper](https://flypaper.soundfly.com/write/exploring-the-lydian-mode-with-elliott-smiths-waltz-1/)

### Inferences
- The conflicts are themselves a finding: for mid-tempo songs with triple subdivision, 3/4, 6/8 and 12/8 labels are assigned inconsistently, so any corpus of "3/4 songs" taken from lead-sheet metadata will contain 6/8-feel material and the reverse. The engine's 538-sheet statistics should be re-checked with a tempo or bar-duration filter.
- (uncited) Melodic thumbnails from the researcher's knowledge, to be verified against scores before use: "Tennessee Waltz" is largely major-pentatonic with a range a little over an octave and an AABA layout; "Amazing Grace" (3/4 hymn) is pentatonic, opens with a rising fourth pickup and spans an octave, in half-plus-quarter bars; "Edelweiss" is one or two notes a bar with a rising triad outline; "My Favorite Things" runs even quarters outlining fifths and octaves at a fast one-in-a-bar tempo; "Greensleeves" and "What Shall We Do with the Drunken Sailor" are respectively a 6/8 dotted-eighth lilt tune and a duple (not 6/8) shanty; "Blow the Man Down" is 6/8 with a triadic refrain; "Rock-a-bye Baby" and Brahms's Lullaby are in three with descending phrase ends; "Unchained Melody" and "Earth Angel" exemplify slow 12/8 with sustained vocal notes over piano triplets.

### Gaps
- No analyses were retrieved for Tin Pan Alley waltzes ("After the Ball", "Let Me Call You Sweetheart"), country waltzes ("Tennessee Waltz"), doo-wop 12/8 ballads, hymns in triple metre, or named lullabies. The Hooktheory TheoryTab pages for "After the Ball" and "Tennessee Waltz" exist (found in search) and would give scale-degree and chord data, but were not read.
- Sea shanty metre by type (which are 6/8, which 2/4 or 4/4) was not confirmed; the Wikipedia article was blocked.

## 6. Tempo ranges and how tempo changes feel and syllable rate

### Takeaway
Sourced waltz tempi run from about 80 to 190 quarters a minute across four recognised bands, pop and rock 6/8 averages about 60 dotted quarters a minute and rarely passes 80, and bars tend toward two seconds; from these, bar duration and available syllable slots can be derived for each band.

### Cited Findings
- Dance tempi for waltz (quarter notes per minute, preferred value and acceptable range): slow American ballroom waltz 88 (80 to 100); cross-step waltz 114 to 116 (108 to 120); moderate rotary waltz 144 (138 to 160); fast Viennese waltz 168 (160 to 190) — [Powers, Stanford Social Dance, DJ tips](https://socialdance.stanford.edu/Syllabi/DJtips.htm)
- Slow waltz around 80 to 90 bpm on the quarter — [Soundbrenner](https://www.soundbrenner.com/blogs/articles/waltz-rhythm)
- Across pop and rock corpora: 4/4 songs average about 120 BPM; 6/8 songs average about 60 BPM and none exceeds 80; 3/4 songs average 99.9 BPM (12 songs in the Burgoyne et al. Billboard sample); 12/8 songs show the same average as 4/4 in the Covach and Temperley/de Clercq samples; the ideal bar is about two seconds (empirical, small samples for triple metres) — [de Clercq 2016](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.declercq.php)
- Irish competition speeds: light jig about 116, slip jig 113, treble jig 92 (traditional) or 72 (slow); slides about 150 — [Wikipedia, Jig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jig); [FolkWorks](https://folkworks.org/article/guide-to-irish-tune-types/)
- In 6/8, slower tempi read as gentle and reflective (ballads), faster tempi as energetic and driving (craft claim, no numbers) — [Native Instruments blog](https://www.native-instruments.com/blogs/blog/6-8-time-signature)
- Lullabies and matched non-lullabies did not differ in notes per minute when tabulated, although a quarter of listeners named slow tempo as a cue — [Trehub, Unyk and Trainor 1993](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/1993_Trainor_Unyk.pdf)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic from the figures above. Waltz at 90 quarters a minute: bar 2.0 s, quarter 0.67 s, eighth 0.33 s; eighth-note syllables are comfortable, so two to five syllables a bar. Waltz at 180: bar 1.0 s, quarter 0.33 s; the quarter is now the fastest practical syllable, felt in one, so one to three syllables a bar and a four-bar group (4 s) behaves like one 12/8 bar. The slow waltz bar and the fast waltz two-bar unit both land on de Clercq's two-second ideal.
- 6/8 at 54 dotted quarters a minute: bar 2.2 s, eighth 0.37 s (162 a minute); all six eighths can carry syllables and sixteenth pickups are possible; the feel is a ballad sway. At 80: bar 1.5 s, eighth 0.25 s (240 a minute); running eighths are at the edge of clear diction, so the quarter-eighth cell (two syllables a beat) becomes the norm. At jig speed (about 116) the eighth is 0.17 s and texts sit mostly on the quarter-eighth cell or one syllable a beat.
- A syllable-rate ceiling of roughly 3 to 4 a second reconciles the bands: it permits eighths in a slow waltz, quarters only in a Viennese waltz, all eighths in slow 6/8, and quarter-eighth pairs in fast 6/8. (The ceiling is the researcher's working figure, uncited.)
- The engine's "three bars sung, one bar held" result for waltz density should hold for the 80 to 120 band; at 140 and above expect lines to span seven of eight bars with the long note in bars 7 and 8.
- The jig figures are assumed to be dotted quarters a minute (two beats to a bar); the sources do not state the unit explicitly. The same caution applies to de Clercq's 6/8 average of 60.

### Gaps
- No source gives measured syllable rates by metre and tempo for sung repertoire.
- No tempo data specific to country waltz, hymn in three, doo-wop 12/8 ballad, sea shanty or lullaby was retrieved. Typical doo-wop 12/8 ballad tempi (commonly put at roughly 55 to 75 dotted quarters a minute) are uncited here.
- The 3/4 average of 99.9 BPM rests on only 12 songs.
