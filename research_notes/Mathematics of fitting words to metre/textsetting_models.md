# Formal mathematical models of English textsetting (constraint sets, probability models, fitted numbers)

Reading notes on reliability. Most primary PDFs were read through an automated page reader, so table cells were sometimes garbled; every such case is flagged "(check original)". Figures marked "derived" were computed from a source's published counts and are not printed in the source. Nothing from TheSession.org was used, downloaded or processed.

## 1. Bruce Hayes's work: constraints, weights, grid definition, and whether triple metre is treated

### Takeaway
Hayes's textsetting grammars are ranked (Optimality Theory) or range-sampled (early stochastic OT) systems on a 16-position, binary-branching, four-beat grid; the only published numeric "weights" are the eight strictness ranges of Hayes & MacEachern (1998), and Hayes (2009) reports a ranking plus accuracy (70.6% vs 23.1% for Halle & Lerdahl's algorithm), not weights. None of these papers models triple metre: ternary settings were explicitly corrected away or excluded, so a maxent version with fitted weights has to be built by the engineer, using the Hayes, Wilson & Shisko (2012) maxent metrics formula as the template.

### Cited Findings

**Hayes (2005 draft / 2009), "Textsetting as constraint conflict"**

- Grid: three active rows S (Strong), M (Medium), W (Weak); the row above S is omitted. In the "Drunken Sailor" grid the S row has 4 positions, the M row 8, the W row 16, so a line is 16 positions with the column pattern S W M W S W M W S W M W S W M W. The Hayes/Kaun data use a grid that "begins with a Medium rather than a Strong position" (so M W S W M W S W ...). An "Extra Weak" row (32nd level) is used only when syllables must be squeezed in. — [Hayes, Textsetting as Constraint Conflict (draft PDF)](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Candidate set: "the full set of logically-possible" alignments of the line's syllables, in order, to grid positions; footnote 9: "if the grid has 16 positions, no line will have more than about 13,000 possible settings." Search is in two steps: find the "contender" candidates, then search rankings over contenders only. — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- The ten constraints, in the ranked order of the paper's (26), highest first: (1) *FILL EXTRA WEAK, (2) *RUN-ON, (3) *LAPSE, (4) *NULL IN S, (5) *STRESSLESS IN S, (6) *STRESSED IN W, (7) *STRESSLESS IN W, (8) STRONG IS LONG, (9) *STRESS(ED) IN M, (10) *NULL IN M. The order was found by "a greedy hill-climbing search"; "Quite a few other rankings give the same outputs that (26) does." — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Definitions given in the text: *FILL EXTRA WEAK is "violated whenever a syllable is placed in an extra weak position"; *RUN-ON is "violated whenever the syllables of a line exceeded the allotted grid for that line"; *LAPSE is "violated whenever there are three empty grid positions in a row". The six "cell" constraints are defined by a 3 x 3 table of {stressed, stressless, null} x {S, M, W}: *STRESSED IN M, *STRESSED IN W, *STRESSLESS IN S, *STRESSLESS IN W, *NULL IN S, *NULL IN M (three cells, stressed-in-S, stressless-in-M and null-in-W, carry no constraint). "Natural" fixed rankings are assumed, e.g. "*STRESSED IN W will outrank (be stricter than) *STRESSED IN M." — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- STRONG IS LONG violation count: for each S position, count "the number of positions on the W level that follow an S position going up to the next S," and deduct that from 3; sum over the four S positions of the line. In symbols: V = sum over the four S positions of (3 - e_k), where e_k is the number of W-level positions after strong position k that are not occupied by a new syllable before the next S. The paper's two examples score 2 and 3 violations. — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Data: the Hayes & Kaun corpus (640 lines, nine consultants in this paper's description, average 2.4 distinct settings per line); "From the Hayes/Kaun corpus I selected 364 lines" (four-beat lines with a consensus setting, at least four of nine consultants). The project web page instead describes the posted data file as "346 lines of folk verse with stress transcriptions and the most common textsetting from nine native English speakers". — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf); [Hayes textsetting project page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)
- Accuracy: Halle & Lerdahl's Syllabic Distribution Algorithm "set 84 of them, or 23.1%, correctly"; the OT grammar: "70.6% of this grammar's outputs matched the favorite setting of the native speaker consultants", plus "An additional 6.9% of the settings derived also matched the consultants' settings" as ties with another candidate. — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- No numeric weights, GLA ranking values or maxent weights are reported in the paper; stochastic OT (Boersma 1997; Boersma & Hayes 2001) is proposed as future work for modelling consultants' ambivalence. Larger unpublished grammars are said to add multiple stress levels, word and phrase boundary effects, and duration matching (pre-boundary lengthening, syllable weight), raising accuracy "to a fair extent" with no figure given. — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Stress was transcribed on a multi-level scale "largely following the system of Chomsky and Halle (1968)", but the grammar itself uses binary stressed/stressless. — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Triple metre is not discussed; all grids are duple/quadruple. — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)

**Hayes & Kaun (1996), "The role of phonological phrasing in sung and chanted verse"**

- Corpus: 670 English folksong lines collected by Cecil Sharp and colleagues, chanted by ten native-speaker consultants (the web page describes the posted file as 640 lines); 3.41 different settings per line on average; the most-voted setting received on average 6.7 of a possible 11 votes (ten consultants plus the original setting). — [Hayes & Kaun 1996 PDF](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Grid: four-beat lines on 16 terminal positions; hierarchy 16 positions, 8 feet, 4 dipods, 2 hemistichs, 1 line. Three-beat lines leave the fourth beat silent. "no line fills more than 14 of the 16 positions." Headless settings (nothing before the first strong beat): 10.9% (760/6998). — [Hayes & Kaun 1996 PDF](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Coding: stress on a 1-4 scale (0 = null position); stressed syllables coded heavy or light; five-level phrasing hierarchy (Utterance, Intonational Phrase, Phonological Phrase, Clitic Group, Word). Transcription reliability: 99.6% agreement on metrical positions, 97.0% complete agreement on all 16 positions of a line; stress transcriptions 88.2% identical between transcribers (8.8% differ by 1 level); phrasing 85.0% identical (13.6% differ by 1). — [Hayes & Kaun 1996 PDF](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Stated principles (not weighted): Stress Matching (stress rises and falls should match the metrical pattern); Zero Provision (unfilled positions count as weaker than any stressless overt syllable); Syllable Duration (syllable durations should match the number of grid positions they receive); Beginnings Free, Endings Strict; line boundaries coincide with Intonational Phrase breaks and are never weaker than Phonological Phrase breaks; lexical inversion is used only when nothing better exists ("faute de mieux", which the authors call an Optimality Theory-style choice and for which they announce a future OT grammar). — [Hayes & Kaun 1996 PDF](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Matching becomes stricter at the right edge of larger phrases. Matched vs mismatched counts (footnote 14): stress-3 syllables that are Clitic Group-final 2639 matched / 334 mismatched (derived: 11.2% mismatched); Phonological Phrase-final 2437 / 75 (derived: 3.0%); stress-4 syllables Phonological Phrase-final 1485 / 60 (derived: 3.9%); Intonational Phrase-final 1102 / 12 (derived: 1.1%); both comparisons chi-square p < .0001. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996 PDF](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Duration: a 100-line measurement (Bertheau 1994) gave r-squared = .532 between predicted and measured syllable durations. Heavy stressed syllables are "resolved" (given a short slot) 59.4% of the time in penultimate position vs 86.5% in antepenultimate position (chi-square p = .0002). The "Short First" rhythm "never occurs in the original versions". — [Hayes & Kaun 1996 PDF](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Triple metre: "Ternary rhythms volunteered by consultants were gently corrected to binary ones; unscannable settings were excluded" (reader's paraphrase of the method section). The study therefore contains no triple-metre data by design. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996 PDF](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)

**Hayes & MacEachern (1998), "Quatrain form in English folk verse"**

- Line (cadence) types on the same four-strong-beat grid: 4 = fourth strong position filled, with at least one syllable between beats 3 and 4; G ("Green-O") = third and fourth strong positions filled with no syllable between them; 3f = third strong position is the last one filled, plus one weaker syllable between beats 3 and 4; 3 = "initiates no syllables after the third strong position of the line"; F = free variation of 4 and G. Cadentiality scale: 3 >> 3f >> G >> 4. Final-syllable length in grid positions: 3 = 6, 3f = 4, G = 2, 4 = 2. — [Hayes & MacEachern 1998 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachernQuatrainForm1998.pdf)
- Saliency: a constituent is salient if (a) its final cadence is more cadential than all its nonfinal cadences and (b) its nonfinal cadences are uniform. — [Hayes & MacEachern 1998 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachernQuatrainForm1998.pdf)
- Constraints. Violable: LINES ARE SALIENT, COUPLETS ARE SALIENT, QUATRAINS ARE SALIENT, PREFER LONG-LAST, TOTAL LONG-LAST COHESIVENESS, FILL STRONG POSITIONS ("Fill the four strongest positions in the line"), AVOID LAPSE, MATCH STRESS ("Employ G in feminine endings, 4 elsewhere"). Inviolable: PARALLELISM ("The cadences ending the units of the maximal analysis of a quatrain must be identical"), STANZA CORRESPONDENCE, PARTIAL LONG-LAST COHESIVENESS. — [Hayes & MacEachern 1998 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachernQuatrainForm1998.pdf)
- Candidate space: 5 cadences to the 4th power = 625 quatrain types; the inviolable constraints leave 52; 8 freely rankable constraints give 8! = 40,320 rankings; the factorial typology yields 26 well-formed types. — [Hayes & MacEachern 1998 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachernQuatrainForm1998.pdf)
- The probability model (an ancestor of stochastic OT). Each violable constraint has a strictness range on an arbitrary scale; on each trial every constraint draws a random value from its range, the draws define a ranking, and the OT winner is found; predicted frequency of a quatrain type = share of trials it wins (20,000 trials during search, one million for final predictions). There is no closed-form probability. Fitted ranges (Table 9): COUPLETS ARE SALIENT .900-1.900; TOTAL LONG-LAST COHESIVENESS .895-1.395 (as read; the stated rule is width 1, check original); FILL STRONG POSITIONS .876-1.876; AVOID LAPSE .800-1.800; QUATRAINS ARE SALIENT .492-1.492; LINES ARE SALIENT .272-1.272; MATCH STRESS .111-1.111; PREFER LONG-LAST .009-1.009. Ranges were fitted by hill-climbing (accept a perturbation only if accuracy improves). — [Hayes & MacEachern 1998 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachernQuatrainForm1998.pdf)
- Fit: corpus of 1028 songs, 627 quatrains after filtering, 601 (95.9%) of predicted types; random baseline 26/625 = 4.2%. Predicted vs observed counts: 4444 202.7 / 203; 4343 194.3 / 188; 4G4G 40.8 / 38; 4443 28.5 / 35; 3f33f3 30.8 / 28; G3G3 17.7 / 26; 444G 10.4 / 7; 3333 6.7 / 1; 3343 0 / 6. Average error "about 35, versus 2.4 for the real data" when frequencies were randomly reassigned as a control. Refrain quatrains are worse fitted: 14 of 130 (10.8%) outside predictions vs 12 of 497 (2.4%) without refrain. — [Hayes & MacEachern 1998 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachernQuatrainForm1998.pdf)
- Triple or compound time is not modelled. The only mention is in Appendix A: the authors "have never seen a 9/8 folksong other than 'Nottamun Town'". Five-line stanzas "form 5.6% of our folksong data corpus". — [Appendices to Hayes & MacEachern 1998](https://brucehayes.org/papers/AppendicesToHayesAndMacEachern1998.pdf)
- The ROA abstract describes the frequency model as assigning each constraint "a range of strictness values" in arbitrary units, divided into central and peripheral subranges (peripheral values give "?" and "??" forms). — [Rutgers Optimality Archive entry](https://roa.rutgers.edu/article/view/130.html)

**The maxent template (Hayes, Wilson & Shisko 2012, spoken verse, not song)**

- Probability of a line L: p(L) = exp(-sum_i lambda_i * chi_i(L)) / Z, with Z = sum_j exp(-sum_i lambda_i * chi_i(L_j)), lambda_i a nonnegative weight and chi_i(L) the number of violations of constraint i. Weights are fitted by maximum likelihood with a Gaussian prior, sigma-squared = 10^10 (i.e. nearly no regularisation). The sample space is every metrically distinct filling of the ten positions, about 2.15 x 10^14. — [Hayes, Wilson & Shisko 2012](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesWilsonShiskoMaxentMetrics2012.pdf)
- Constraint selection uses a likelihood-ratio test, -2 log(P(corpus | simpler) / P(corpus | full)), compared with chi-square at 1 degree of freedom, with a lenient threshold of 0.15. — [Hayes, Wilson & Shisko 2012](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesWilsonShiskoMaxentMetrics2012.pdf)
- Example fitted weights (Shakespeare / Milton): *STRESS IN W 2.94 / 2.95; *CONSECUTIVE STRESSLESS IN S 1.34 / 1.27; *NO FALL FROM S 0.28 / 0.21; ALIGN(Line, Intonational Phrase) 2.52 / 0.36; ALIGN(Line, Clitic Group) 3.09 / 2.53; ALIGN(Line, Word) 11.40 / 14.12; ALIGN(P-phrase, Foot) 0.41 / 0.28. These are for iambic pentameter and are a guide to typical magnitudes only. — [Hayes, Wilson & Shisko 2012](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesWilsonShiskoMaxentMetrics2012.pdf)

### Inferences
- The "about 13,000" candidate bound equals the largest binomial coefficient C(16, 8) = 12,870: a setting is a choice of which n of 16 ordered positions start a syllable, so the candidate count for an n-syllable line on a P-position grid is C(P, n). For one bar-pair of 6/8 at sixteenth resolution (24 positions) or two bars of 3/4 at eighth resolution (12 positions) the same enumeration stays small enough for exhaustive search or dynamic programming.
- A conditional maxent textsetting model follows directly by combining Hayes (2009)'s candidate set and constraints with the 2012 formula: P(setting s | line x) = exp(-sum_i w_i * v_i(x, s)) / sum over all C(P, n) settings s' of exp(-sum_i w_i * v_i(x, s')). The ten Hayes (2009) constraints give ten weights; the log-likelihood is concave, so fitting is ordinary multinomial logistic regression over candidates.
- Because the cell constraints are indexed by strength class (S, M, W), not by position number, the weights are in principle portable to any grid whose positions can be labelled with strength classes. Moving to triple metre needs only a relabelling of positions (for 3/4 at eighth resolution: S w M w M w or S w W w W w; for 6/8: S w w M w w) and a re-statement of *LAPSE and STRONG IS LONG in units of that grid. Whether the fitted weights survive the move is untested in the literature (see Gaps).
- The reported discrepancy in Hayes's line counts (364 lines in the text, 346 on the data page; "84 right, 260 wrong", which sum to 344) could not be resolved from the readable copies; 84/364 = 23.1% is internally consistent with the stated percentage.
- The Hayes & MacEachern range model is equivalent in spirit to a noisy ranking; a maxent reimplementation over the 52 surviving quatrain candidates with 8 weights would be a cleaner way to control stanza-level line lengths (which lines end early, which run to the fourth beat).

### Gaps
- No published Hayes textsetting grammar with maxent or GLA weights was found; the published version of the 2009 chapter on Hayes's site is an image-only PDF and could not be read, so any difference from the 2005 draft is unverified.
- Hayes's "natural ranking" arrows among the cell constraints could not be recovered from the text.
- No Hayes paper treats 3/4, 6/8 or other compound metres.
- Hayes & Kaun's figure values (percentages per phrasing level for Short First, Scotch snaps, dotted rhythms) are shown only in graphs and were not recoverable.

## 2. The other models: Halle & Lerdahl, Dell & Halle, Temperley & Temperley, Palmer & Kelly, Kiparsky, Proto and pop/rap work, and what they report

### Takeaway
Outside Hayes, the usable numbers are corpus statistics, not fitted grammars: Temperley & Temperley's metrical-level tables (the most reusable measurement scheme, and one that is defined for 3/4 and 6/8), Palmer & Kelly's compound vs phrase counts, the rock and pop syncopation quotients, and one small maxent study of art song (Girardi & Plag) with per-song weights. Halle & Lerdahl is a three-step procedure with no parameters; Dell & Halle and Kiparsky give categorical principles with no numbers recoverable here.

### Cited Findings

**Halle & Lerdahl (1993), as restated by Hayes from Halle (1999)**

- The Syllabic Distribution Algorithm: "a. Map stressed syllables one-to-one, left-to-right onto S positions; b. Map stressless syllables one-to-one, right-to-left onto the highest grid level able to accommodate them; c. Once (a) and (b) have applied as many times as they can, map any remaining stressless syllables one-to-one, left-to-right onto the highest grid level able to accommodate them." The row used in step (b) for C stressless syllables is given in Hayes's footnote as y = -(int(log2(C)) + 1). — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Its measured accuracy on consultants' preferred settings is 23.1% (84 lines), against 70.6% for Hayes's ranked grammar. — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- A later summary gives the first step as "assign all accented syllables to available [strong musical] beats from left to right" and notes the model addresses only prominence, with "The Drunken Sailor" as case study. — [De Castro Arrazola 2018, Leiden dissertation, ch. 6](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2951724/view)
- On a Billboard pop sample (first 20 of 42 songs in 4/4; 554 bars; 1,718 beat positions), the algorithm had 486 apparent exceptions; after excluding 133 half-beat-late cases, 353 were analysed: 230 (65.2%) were leftward (anticipatory) shifts, 45 (12.7%) rightward shifts, leaving 78 true exceptions. Leftward shift accounted for 100% of exceptions at beats 1 (104) and 3 (96) but only 16.5% at beat 2 (14 of 85) and 23.5% at beat 4 (16 of 68). 3/4 songs were excluded. — [Hattori, ICPhS 2019](https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/icphs-proceedings/ICPhS2019/papers/ICPhS_2950.pdf)

**Dell & Halle (2009)**

- Abstract-level claims only: in French strophic songs, stanzas sharing a tune have the same number of lines, and the i-th line of each stanza has the same syllable count and the same distribution of melismas ("Positional Parallelism"); violations are "rather infrequent" in traditional French songs but "quite common" in English songs; English aligns stress with beat anywhere in the line, French rigidly only at line ends. — [Dell & Halle 2009, abstract page](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/lfab.2.03del/html)
- They also suggest that a final unstressed syllable on a relatively prominent note is more acceptable after a melismatic syllable. — [De Castro Arrazola 2018, citing Dell & Halle p. 71](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2951724/view)

**Temperley & Temperley (2013), "Stress-meter alignment in French vocal music"**

- Metrical-level scoring, defined for simple and compound metres: level 2 (tactus) = quarter note in 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 and dotted quarter in 6/8, 9/8, 12/8; level 3 = one-measure level in 2/4, 3/4, 6/8, 9/8 and half-measure level in 4/4 and 12/8; level 4 = one-measure level in 4/4 and 12/8 only; level 1 = everything below the tactus. Each syllable scores the highest level present at its onset. — [Temperley & Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- Syllable classes: s (stressed syllable of a polysyllable), u (other syllables of polysyllables), c (monosyllabic content word), f (monosyllabic function word), x (French word-final schwa); stressable = s, c; unstressable = f, u, x. Measure: SMAV (stress-meter alignment value) = mean level of stressable minus mean level of unstressable syllables. — [Temperley & Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- French corpus (54 songs, 1193 lines, 9003 syllables), column shares by level 1/2/3/4: s .16/.19/.46/.20; c .28/.28/.33/.11; f .40/.32/.23/.04; u .58/.28/.11/.03. Mean level: s 2.69, c 2.26, f 1.91, u 1.59; stressable 2.57, unstressable 1.80; SMAV 0.77, or 0.49 with line-final syllables excluded (line-final syllables average 3.14 vs 1.88 elsewhere). Per-song test t(53) = 14.7, p < .0001. — [Temperley & Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- English comparison (first ten songs of Songs of England, 1900): SMAV 1.15 (stressable mean 3.08, unstressable 1.93); in all 94 unstressed-stressed pairs within a word, the stressed syllable was metrically stronger (100%, against 64% in French). French vs English SMAV: t(15.01) = 3.63, p < .01. — [Temperley & Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- Scrambling baseline: for 8-syllable lines, original mean SMAV 0.99 vs 0.24 after scrambling the text against the tune. — [Temperley & Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- The paper pools all time signatures and does not report counts or results by time signature. — [Temperley & Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- Temperley's 2022 review states that alignment matters less at higher levels: what counts is that local pairs match, and that in recent popular music a stressed syllable may shift to the weak beat just before a strong one "in restricted and quite systematic ways". It gives no numbers and does not mention triple metre. — [Temperley 2022, Annual Review of Linguistics](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/temperley-arl22.pdf)

**Palmer & Kelly (1992), "Linguistic prosody and musical meter in song"**

- Metrical accent was read from the notated time signature: binary metres alternate strong/weak, ternary metres (3/4 and 6/8 are named) go strong/weak/weak. Results are pooled across metres. — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- Gilbert & Sullivan themes (words and music written together): compound nouns took the stronger metrical accent on syllable 1 in 43 cases vs 5 on syllable 2 (derived: 89.6%); adjective-noun phrases 16 vs 79 (derived: 83.2% on the noun); chi-square(1) = 66.6, p < .01. Duration and melodic contour did not distinguish the syllables. — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- Existing poems set to music: compound nouns 96 vs 9 (derived: 91.4% on syllable 1); adjective-noun phrases 91 vs 67 (derived: only 42.4% on the noun); chi-square(1) = 33.5, p < .01. — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- Performance: singers lengthened syllables under both musical accent (F(1,9) = 23.5) and linguistic accent (F(1,9) = 18.0), with no interaction (the two effects add). — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)

**Kiparsky (2006), "A modular metrics for folk verse" and related**

- The readable Kiparsky text (a 2006 update on metrics, not the folk-verse chapter itself) sets out a three-module architecture: metrical patterns (verse design), correspondence constraints linking verse to language, and performance conventions (recitation, textsetting). "The metrical structure of a text is invariant and does not change with the way it is set to music or performed." Constraints include "a Strong position must contain a stress peak", "A Weak position cannot contain a stress peak", and "A position contains one syllable", with resolution licences letting two syllables fill one position. No numerical rankings or probabilities are given. Ternary measures "are in verse constructed from binary ones by beat-splitting." — [Kiparsky, metrics update (Stanford)](https://web.stanford.edu/~kiparsky/Papers/Metrics/LI06_Kiparsky_update.pdf)
- The folk-verse chapter is described in its catalogue entry as a reply to Hayes & MacEachern's stochastic OT quatrain study, arguing that stress and metre are governed by separate constraint systems that must be jointly satisfied. — [PhilArchive entry](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/KIPAMM)

**Girardi & Plag (2019), maxent grammars for six English art songs**

- Six songs, 161 lines, 1,646 syllables (952 W and 663 S templatic positions): Haydn's Mermaid's Song (2/4), The Wanderer (3/4), Fidelity (4/4), and three settings of "Annabel Lee" (Gendel 4/4, 6/4, 3/4; New 2/2; Quick 9/8, 6/8, 3/4). Musical strength coded with Temperley & Temperley's levels 1-4; note value as a fraction of the bar. Weights from Hayes's Maxent Grammar Tool with a Gaussian prior, mu = 1 and sigma-squared = 5.0E8. — [Girardi & Plag 2019 manuscript](https://www.anglistik.hhu.de/fileadmin/redaktion/Fakultaeten/Philosophische_Fakultaet/Anglistik_und_Amerikanistik/Ang3_Linguistics/Dateien/Detailseiten/Plag/2019/Girardi_Plag_Textsetting_2019-09-19.pdf)
- Fitted weights, stress x musical strength (BEAT1 = weakest level), for The Wanderer in 3/4: *BEAT1 ON STRESSED 5.00, *BEAT2 ON STRESSED 0.00, *BEAT3 ON STRESSED 0.00, *BEAT3 ON UNSTRESSED 5.00, *BEAT2 ON UNSTRESSED 0.00, *BEAT1 ON UNSTRESSED 0.00. For Fidelity in 4/4: 5.00, 0.00, 0.00, 6.36, 0.31, 4.54 in the same order. For Quick's mixed 9/8, 6/8, 3/4 setting: 5.61, 0.47, 0.27, 5.85, 3.90, 0.15. (Column alignment partly uncertain in the readable copy; check original.) — [Girardi & Plag 2019 manuscript](https://www.anglistik.hhu.de/fileadmin/redaktion/Fakultaeten/Philosophische_Fakultaet/Anglistik_und_Amerikanistik/Ang3_Linguistics/Dateien/Detailseiten/Plag/2019/Girardi_Plag_Textsetting_2019-09-19.pdf)
- Note-length constraints got weights of about 5-6 for the strict versions (*LONGER AFTER S 5.1-6.0; *SHORTER AFTER W 5.0-5.9) and 0.0-3.9 for the "or equal" versions; pitch-height constraints were much less discriminating. Variance explained in metrical strength: by templatic position r-squared 0.76 (Mermaid), 0.74 (Wanderer, 3/4), 0.82 (Fidelity), 0.79 (Quick); by word stress 0.52, 0.68, 0.39, 0.73. In note value: 0.18-0.32 by template, 0.10-0.34 by stress. Template position predicts the music better than raw stress in most songs. — [Girardi & Plag 2019 manuscript](https://www.anglistik.hhu.de/fileadmin/redaktion/Fakultaeten/Philosophische_Fakultaet/Anglistik_und_Amerikanistik/Ang3_Linguistics/Dateien/Detailseiten/Plag/2019/Girardi_Plag_Textsetting_2019-09-19.pdf)
- The paper does not analyse triple metre separately; it only remarks that a difference between two Haydn songs "may arise from the different time signatures, which is binary in The Mermaid's Song and ternary in The Wanderer." — [Girardi & Plag 2019 manuscript](https://www.anglistik.hhu.de/fileadmin/redaktion/Fakultaeten/Philosophische_Fakultaet/Anglistik_und_Amerikanistik/Ang3_Linguistics/Dateien/Detailseiten/Plag/2019/Girardi_Plag_Textsetting_2019-09-19.pdf)

**Popular music and rap (all 4/4)**

- Rock (80 songs from the Rolling Stone corpus, 23,129 notes, 1955-1997; 17 non-4/4 songs excluded). Stress from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary with monosyllabic function words set to unstressed: primary 43.5% of syllables, secondary 1.8%, unstressed 50.3%. Syncopation quotient SQ = stressed syllables on weak beats (not followed by a syllable on or before the next strong beat) / all stressed syllables: 8th-note level .228 (2,382 of 10,433), 16th-note level .105, combined .333. Share of syncopations falling just before a strong beat (anticipatory): .772 (8th level), .740 (16th level). A sample of 19th-century English songs (10 songs, 1,397 notes) had 0 syncopations in 602 stressed syllables. Probability that a syllable is anticipated rather than placed on the beat, by target beat: onto beat 3 (5 to 4) .554; onto beat 1 (1 to 8) .504; onto beat 2 (3 to 2) .439; onto beat 4 (7 to 6) .351. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/tan-lustig-temperley.pdf)
- Twentieth-century pop (100 songs, one per year 1900-1999, 25,124 notes; only 4/4, 2/4, 2/2). Fourth-position (anticipatory) syncopations rose from 1.5% of syllables in 1900 to 19.3% in 1999 (r-squared .36), second-position from 5.0% to 10.9% (r-squared .07); stressed syllables on weak beats with positional syncopation rose from 1.8% to 17.6%. Fourth-position syncopated syllables are stressed 67% of the time (1,855/2,754), second-position 31% (629/1,994). Change points: 1930 for second-position, 1966 for fourth-position. — [VanderStel & Temperley, J. New Music Research](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/vanderstel-temperley-jnmr.pdf)
- Hip hop (18 songs, 6 artists, 8,312 syllables, all 4/4; levels 1 = beats 1 and 3, 2 = beats 2 and 4, 3 = eighth offbeats, 4 = sixteenths). Shares at levels 1/2/3/4: stressable syllables 27.59% / 28.85% / 26.19% / 17.37%; unstressable 6.60% / 6.18% / 31.41% / 55.81%. Primary-stressed syllables: 30.52 / 34.63 / 21.43 / 13.41. Stress shift occurred in 10.62% of polysyllabic words (148 of 1,393). — [Stress-Meter Alignment in American Hip Hop](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339016445_Stress-Meter_Alignment_in_American_Hip_Hop)
- Pop syllable weight (DALI 2 corpus, 2,371 usable English songs): heavy stressed initial syllables were given a quarter-note slot 42% of the time vs 24% for light ones (p < .0001); in a model with both, scaled coefficients were 0.32 for weight category and 0.21 for natural vowel duration. Proposed constraints HEAVY-IS-LONG and HEAVIER-IS-LONG are not given weights. The study assumes a 1:2 eighth-to-quarter grid and does not address triple metre. — [Ryan 2022 preprint](https://www.meluhha.com/articles/ryan_2022_textsetting_preprint.pdf)
- German folk song (Proto 2018): qualitative only; stress-beat mismatches where a stressed syllable sits on a weaker beat but is longer or higher are reported by three informants to have "no perceptual reality". No percentages. — [Proto, Speech Prosody 2018](https://www.isca-archive.org/speechprosody_2018/proto18_speechprosody.pdf)
- Dutch folk song (Meertens Tune Collections, 3,724 songs, 176,708 syllables): observed vs expected (independence) frequencies of stress and prominence contours over two-syllable windows; word-internal "stress falls, prominence rises" has log ratio -4.14 (270 observed vs 4,768 expected); the reverse -3.60. The mismatch of an unstressed syllable on the more prominent note is avoided more strongly than the opposite. — [De Castro Arrazola 2018](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2951724/view)

### Inferences
- From Temperley & Temperley's table (derived): the share of syllables on levels 3-4 is s 65.3%, c 43.5%, f 27.2%, u 14.2% in French; English art song is markedly stricter (SMAV 1.15 vs 0.77), and hip hop stricter again on the weak side (unstressable syllables only 12.8% on beats). These give target ranges for a "stressable minus unstressable mean level" check: about 1.0-1.2 for English.
- Temperley & Temperley's level scheme is the only measurement scheme in this literature that is already defined for 3/4 and 6/8: in 3/4, level 3 = beat 1, level 2 = beats 2 and 3, level 1 = offbeats; in 6/8, level 3 = first dotted beat, level 2 = second dotted beat, level 1 = the other four eighths. Using it gives a metre-independent feature (level 1-3 or 1-4) for a maxent model.
- Girardi & Plag's Wanderer weights say that in a 3/4 art song the mapping is close to categorical at the extremes (stressed syllables barred from the sub-beat level, unstressed barred from the downbeat) and free in the middle (beats 2 and 3 take either). That is one song, so it is an indication, not a fitted norm.
- The pop syncopation quotients imply that a pop engine in 4/4 should let roughly a quarter to a third of stressed syllables sit on a weak position immediately before a strong one; the older-songbook figure is near zero. Whether the same rate applies to 3/4 or 6/8 pop is not measured anywhere found.

### Gaps
- Halle & Lerdahl (1993) and the full text of Dell & Halle (2009) and of Kiparsky's folk-verse chapter were not accessible; their content is reported second-hand or from abstracts.
- Temperley & Temperley give no breakdown by time signature even though their level scheme covers compound metres.
- No fitted probabilistic textsetting grammar for pop or rap was found; the pop literature reports quotients and regressions on year and tempo only.

## 3. Published statistics on where stressed syllables fall in triple metre, and pop vs older song

### Takeaway
Almost nothing is published. The single explicit figure found is from a small 56-song piano-book study: stressed syllables fall on strong beats 50.8% of the time in 3/4 against 65.7% in 4/4 (keywords 71.1% vs 85.5%). No source found gives beat 1 vs beats 2 and 3 of 3/4, or the two dotted beats of 6/8, and every pop corpus study found excluded non-duple songs.

### Cited Findings
- 56 songs "collected from various piano books" (26 in 3/4, 30 in 4/4; 2,713 words, 3,516 syllables). "Stress Matching" (proportion of the stressed items that land on strong beats): stressed syllables 0.594 overall, 0.508 in 3/4, 0.657 in 4/4; keywords 0.796 overall, 0.711 in 3/4, 0.855 in 4/4. The paper does not state exactly which beats count as strong in 3/4 beyond defining the downbeat as the first beat of the bar, and it has no 6/8 songs. The licence of the dataset is not stated. — [Relationships between Keywords and Strong Beats in Lyrical Music, arXiv 2412.04202](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04202)
- A related study on the Children's Song Dataset (English songs) reports an average probability of 0.77 that a stressed syllable is on a strong beat and 0.81 for keywords, without splitting by time signature. — [Multimodal Lyrics-Rhythm Matching, arXiv 2301.02732](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.02732)
- Girardi & Plag's one 3/4 song (Haydn, The Wanderer, 130 syllables) yields maxent weights of 5.00 against a stressed syllable below the beat level and 5.00 against an unstressed syllable on the downbeat, with 0.00 for both stressed and unstressed syllables on beats 2 and 3; word stress explains 68% of the variance in metrical strength in this song against 52% and 39% in the two duple Haydn songs. — [Girardi & Plag 2019 manuscript](https://www.anglistik.hhu.de/fileadmin/redaktion/Fakultaeten/Philosophische_Fakultaet/Anglistik_und_Amerikanistik/Ang3_Linguistics/Dateien/Detailseiten/Plag/2019/Girardi_Plag_Textsetting_2019-09-19.pdf)
- Exclusions that create the gap: the rock study removed 17 songs with non-4/4/2/4 signatures; the 1900-1999 pop study kept only simple duple songs and dropped songs that divide the beat in three; the Billboard study said 3/4 was excluded because strong/weak interactions are rare in it; Hayes & Kaun corrected ternary chanting to binary. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/tan-lustig-temperley.pdf); [VanderStel & Temperley](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/vanderstel-temperley-jnmr.pdf); [Hattori 2019](https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/icphs-proceedings/ICPhS2019/papers/ICPhS_2950.pdf); [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Pop vs older song, duple only: 19th-century English songs show 0 of 602 stressed syllables syncopated, against .333 combined in rock; anticipatory syncopation in chart pop rose from 1.5% to 19.3% of syllables across the 20th century. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/tan-lustig-temperley.pdf); [VanderStel & Temperley](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/vanderstel-temperley-jnmr.pdf)

### Inferences
- The lower 3/4 figure (0.508 vs 0.657) is at least partly arithmetic, not style: if only beat 1 counts as strong, a 3/4 bar offers one strong slot per three beats where 4/4 offers two per four, while English speech supplies a stress roughly every second or third syllable. A fair comparison needs a chance baseline per metre (scramble the lyric against the tune, as Temperley & Temperley did: 0.99 real vs 0.24 scrambled).
- The safest transferable quantity is therefore not "share on strong beats" but the level-conditional weights of a maxent model (penalty for stressed at sub-beat level, unstressed at bar level), which already normalise for how many slots of each level a metre offers.
- The pop-vs-songbook difference in triple metre can only be assumed by analogy with duple metre (more anticipation in pop); no data confirms it.

### Gaps
- No published split of stressed syllables between beat 1 and beats 2-3 of 3/4, or between the two dotted beats of 6/8, was found in any style.
- No published comparison of pop and older song in triple or compound metre was found.
- Whether anticipatory syncopation in 6/8 pop targets the eighth before each dotted beat at rates like the 4/4 figures is unmeasured.

## 4. Mismatch between syllable count and positions: melisma, splitting, dropping, pickups, and adapting a strophic tune to later verses

### Takeaway
The formal models handle count mismatch almost entirely through empty positions and their penalties (*NULL, *LAPSE, FILL STRONG POSITIONS, STRONG IS LONG, *RUN-ON); melisma is treated as a held syllable, not a separate object, and pickups are not formalised beyond the observation that about 89% of chanted lines start before the first strong beat. Strophic adaptation has one principle (Dell & Halle's Positional Parallelism, routinely violated in English) and one usable set of numbers (Temperley & Temperley's verse 1 vs verse 2 table).

### Cited Findings
- Too few syllables: empty positions are penalised by *NULL IN S, *NULL IN M and *LAPSE (three empty positions in a row); lapses appear in the data only when a line is too short to avoid them. Too many syllables: *RUN-ON (syllables exceed the line's grid) and *FILL EXTRA WEAK (a syllable on the extra-weak level) are the two top-ranked constraints. — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Melisma is not a separate category in Hayes's model: a syllable's continuation over later positions counts as absence of a new syllable, and STRONG IS LONG rewards exactly that after strong positions. — [Hayes draft PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Hayes & Kaun: unfilled positions are "freely tolerated"; extrametrical syllables do not exist in sung verse (every syllable must get a grid position); 10.9% of settings are headless, so about 89% begin with at least one syllable before the first strong beat; for a feminine ending the options are Green-O truncation, three-beat truncation, stealing a position from the next line, or a mismatched position, and the mismatched option frequently wins. — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Hayes & MacEachern: line-length variation is modelled as a choice among cadences 4, G, 3f, 3, with FILL STRONG POSITIONS and AVOID LAPSE opposed by the saliency constraints; under cadence 3 "all subsequent positions are filled either by pause or by lengthening of the line-final syllable." — [Hayes & MacEachern 1998](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndMacEachernQuatrainForm1998.pdf)
- Melisma rates in corpora: under 1% of notes in French traditional song (13 of 1899 and 11 of 1899 in two verses); 4.46% of syllables in Dutch folk song; 2,139 non-initial melisma notes against 25,124 syllable notes in 1900-1999 chart pop (derived: about 7.8% of all notes). — [Temperley & Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf); [De Castro Arrazola 2018](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2951724/view); [VanderStel & Temperley](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/vanderstel-temperley-jnmr.pdf)
- After a melisma the stress-matching requirement relaxes: in the Dutch data all three unstressed alignments, including the normally avoided unstressed-on-prominent, are over-represented after a melismatic syllable. — [De Castro Arrazola 2018](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2951724/view)
- Strophic principle: Positional Parallelism (same syllable count and same melisma distribution in the i-th line of every stanza) holds in traditional French song and is "quite common[ly]" violated in English song. — [Dell & Halle 2009, abstract](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/lfab.2.03del/html)
- Strophic numbers (French, 48 multi-verse songs, 1899 note-matched syllable pairs, line-final excluded): of 598 stressable verse-1 syllables, 331 (54%) are matched by a stressable syllable on the same note in verse 2; of 1301 unstressable ones, 1019 keep an unstressable syllable (so 21% receive a stressable one); chi-square = 211, p < .0001. Alignment is weaker in later verses: SMAV 0.51 in verse 1 vs 0.33 in verse 2, t(47) = 2.2, p < .05. — [Temperley & Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- A worked constraint-based treatment of adapting text to a fixed bar structure (Kaytetye, Australia): grid of four positions per measure labelled S W M W; candidates generated as integer compositions of the syllable count across measures, plus text modifications (vowel deletion, consonant epenthesis, syllable transfer), 607,543 candidates for 89 texts; constraints *INCREASE, *STRESSLESS IN STRONG, LINEARITY(ROOT), *FILL, SALIENT, FINAL IS LONG (violations = 3 minus positions held by the measure-final syllable), MAX(V); the ranked grammar picks the attested setting for 71 of 89 texts (79.7%). — [Textsetting in Kaytetye, Proceedings of the Annual Meeting on Phonology](https://journals.linguisticsociety.org/proceedings/index.php/amphonology/article/download/4911/4648/9197)
- Kiparsky's verse-side devices for count mismatch: resolution (two syllables in one position), an optional extrametrical weak position at line end, syncope and contraction; "empty beats" are metrically real but silent. — [Kiparsky, metrics update](https://web.stanford.edu/~kiparsky/Papers/Metrics/LI06_Kiparsky_update.pdf)

### Inferences
- In the Hayes framework, "split a note" and "drop a note" need no extra machinery if the grid, not the verse-1 rhythm, is the fixed object: each verse is set afresh against the same grid, and extra syllables simply occupy positions that verse 1 left empty (penalised by nothing if the position is W, by *FILL EXTRA WEAK if it needs a finer level), while missing syllables leave positions empty (penalised by *NULL IN S/M and *LAPSE, rewarded by STRONG IS LONG after a strong position).
- To keep later verses recognisably the same tune, a faithfulness term is needed that Hayes's grammar lacks. The natural form is one weight per kind of departure from the verse-1 rhythm (inserted onset, deleted onset, moved onset), which is Dell & Halle's Positional Parallelism made violable. Temperley & Temperley's 54% / 21% figures and the SMAV drop from 0.51 to 0.33 show that real later verses do trade stress matching for tune identity; in English the trade is reported to go the other way more often (rhythm bends to the words), but no English numbers were found.
- Pickups: the only number is that about 89% of four-beat folk lines start before the first strong beat. A model can treat pickup positions as ordinary W or M positions at the end of the previous line's grid, which is what the Hayes/Kaun grid starting on M effectively does.
- The relaxation after melisma suggests the stress-matching penalty should be switched off or down-weighted for the syllable following a melisma.

### Gaps
- No formal, fitted model of English strophic adaptation (how often notes are split or dropped in later verses, at which grid levels) was found; Dell & Halle's English examples and counts were not accessible.
- No quantitative treatment of anacrusis length or of pickups in triple metre was found.

## 5. Computational implementations, fitted parameter tables, data and licences

### Takeaway
There is public data and a public fitting tool, but almost none of it carries an explicit licence, and there is no ready-made open textsetting grammar with fitted weights for English. The practical route is Hayes's posted folk-line data for the constraint design, the Maxent Grammar Tool (or any multinomial logistic fitter) for the weights, and Temperley's rock and pop corpora for pop statistics, each needing a licence check before reuse.

### Cited Findings
- Hayes textsetting data: a plain tab-delimited file of folk verse lines with stress transcriptions and the most common setting from nine speakers, plus chanted audio examples; no licence is stated on the page and no software is offered. — [Hayes textsetting project page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)
- Hayes & Kaun data: "640 lines of English folksong, annotated for rhythmic textsetting, stress, phrasing, and syllable weight" as an ASCII file (FolkLine.txt), with sound examples; Hayes & MacEachern materials: tableaux, a violations file for all 625 quatrain candidates, and an annotated Appalachian folk song spreadsheet. No licence terms are stated for any of them. The old grid-playing program is "no longer available". — [Hayes, Research in metrics page](https://brucehayes.org/Metrics.htm)
- Maxent Grammar Tool: takes inputs, rival candidates, winner frequencies, a constraint list and violation counts, and returns maxent weights and each candidate's predicted probability; needs a Java runtime; originated in Colin Wilson's code, interface by Ben George. Download and manual are on the page; source availability and licence are not stated. — [Maxent Grammar Tool page](https://brucehayes.org/MaxentGrammarTool/)
- Girardi & Plag used that tool with prior settings mu = 1 and sigma-squared = 5.0E8 and published per-song weight tables (the only fitted maxent weight tables for English song found). — [Girardi & Plag 2019 manuscript](https://www.anglistik.hhu.de/fileadmin/redaktion/Fakultaeten/Philosophische_Fakultaet/Anglistik_und_Amerikanistik/Ang3_Linguistics/Dateien/Detailseiten/Plag/2019/Girardi_Plag_Textsetting_2019-09-19.pdf)
- Hayes, Wilson & Shisko state that their article website holds the databases, grammars, tableaux and software for the maxent metrics study; no licence is stated in the paper. — [Hayes, Wilson & Shisko 2012](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesWilsonShiskoMaxentMetrics2012.pdf)
- Kaytetye textsetting: an R package for candidate generation on GitHub (fauxneticien/akwelye), constraints implemented as regular expressions over candidate strings, ranking found with OTSoft 2.5, inputs and outputs archived on Zenodo. The licence was not confirmed. — [Textsetting in Kaytetye](https://journals.linguisticsociety.org/proceedings/index.php/amphonology/article/download/4911/4648/9197)
- Rock corpus with lyrics, stress and metrical position (80 songs): "publicly available" at rockcorpus.midside.com; licence not stated in the article. Pop corpus 1900-1999 (100 songs): publicly available at popcorpus.com; licence not stated. Both contain duple-metre songs only for the analysed portion. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/tan-lustig-temperley.pdf); [VanderStel & Temperley](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/vanderstel-temperley-jnmr.pdf)
- Filipino textsetting study (19 songs, all 4/4; one 3/4 song excluded): five-level beat-strength scale (5 = downbeat, 4 = beat 3, 3 = beats 2 and 4, 2 = eighth offbeats, 1 = sixteenths); syncopation handled by giving each syllable the strongest beat it contains within its first 1.25 beats; significance by shuffling lyrics across lines 1,000 times. The article is open access under the Creative Commons Attribution licence, with annotated R code in the supplementary material. — [Evidence for stress in Filipino textsetting, Phonology 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/749B69E22EEFE610B29535C6830D4575/S0952675725000041a.pdf/evidence_for_stress_in_filipino_textsetting.pdf)
- DALI 2 (used by Ryan 2022): syllable start times and durations for 5,913 English songs, but no grid or note values; the paper states no licence and links no code. — [Ryan 2022 preprint](https://www.meluhha.com/articles/ryan_2022_textsetting_preprint.pdf)
- Stress lookup in the pop studies is the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary with monosyllabic function words forced to unstressed (31% of word tokens in the rock corpus). — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/tan-lustig-temperley.pdf)

### Inferences
- A minimal implementable model, assembled from the above: (1) label every grid position with a Temperley & Temperley level (works in 4/4, 3/4, 6/8, 12/8); (2) classify syllables as s, c, f, u from a pronouncing dictionary plus a function-word list; (3) candidates = all C(P, n) order-preserving placements per line; (4) features = counts of {syllable class x level} cells (Hayes's cell constraints, generalised), *LAPSE, *NULL at beat level and at bar level, STRONG IS LONG, an anticipation feature (stressed syllable on the position just before a strong beat with nothing on the beat), a phrase-final-length feature, and for later verses a parallelism feature; (5) fit by conditional maximum likelihood with a Gaussian prior, P(s | x) proportional to exp(-w . v(x, s)); (6) select features with the likelihood-ratio test.
- Transfer to a metre with no worded corpus: fit the weights on 4/4 songs, keep the weights tied to level and syllable class (not to position index), and re-derive only the grid labels for the new metre. The Filipino "strongest beat within the first 1.25 beats" rule and the Temperley anticipation counts give a way to treat pop syncopation as a feature rather than as noise before fitting. Scramble-the-lyric baselines per metre are the way to validate the transfer without a triple-metre worded corpus.
- Girardi & Plag's very large sigma-squared and Hayes, Wilson & Shisko's 10^10 mean the published weights are essentially unregularised; with a small corpus a much smaller sigma-squared would be prudent, and their near-identical values of 5.00 and 0.00 look like weights pinned by categorical data, not graded estimates.

### Gaps
- Licences: none of Hayes's data files, the Maxent Grammar Tool, the Temperley rock and pop corpora, the piano-book dataset or DALI 2 has a licence stated in the sources read; each needs to be checked at source (or permission asked) before any reuse in the engine. The akwelye GitHub licence and the Zenodo archive licence were not checked.
- No open Python implementation of a Hayes-style textsetting grammar was found.
- No published fitted weight table exists for English pop textsetting in any metre; the Girardi & Plag tables are per-song fits on six art songs.
