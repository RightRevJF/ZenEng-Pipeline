# Mathematics of carrying a rhythm rule from one metre or style to another (metre transfer math)

Reader note: "Cited Findings" are things a source said and I read. "Inferences" are my own arithmetic or design reasoning and are not published results. Several primary sources (Palmer & Krumhansl 1990, Witek 2014, Huron & Ommen 2006, Temperley 2021) could not be opened in full; where their numbers are missing I say so in Gaps rather than fill from memory. No data from TheSession.org was used or downloaded.

## 1. Formal metrical weights: how strength is defined position by position in 4/4, 3/4, 6/8, 12/8

### Takeaway
Every formal scheme builds the weights the same way: a tree of subdivisions in which the first child inherits its parent's strength and all other children get the weight of the new, lower level. So 3/4 and 6/8 use the same six eighth-note slots but different trees (3 then 2, versus 2 then 3), and in every theoretical scheme beats 2 and 3 of 3/4 are exactly equal; only measured data (not found for triple metre) could separate them.

### Cited Findings
- General construction (Song's syncopation-model thesis, the basis of the SynPy toolkit): each level L has a subdivision factor lambda_L and a weight w_L. Start with H0 = <w0>. To go down a level, each existing weight h becomes <h> followed by (lambda_L - 1) copies of w_L. "The first child of a node keeps its parent's weight; the others get w_L." — [Song, QMUL thesis (CORE)](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)
- Subdivision sequences: 4/4 = <1,2,2>, 3/4 = <1,3,2>, 6/8 = <1,2,3>. — [Song thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)
- Longuet-Higgins & Lee (LHL) weights use w_L = -L (0 for the bar, -1, -2, ...). Worked tables from the source:
  - 4/4 to quarter-note level: <0, -2, -1, -2>
  - 3/4 to eighth level: <0, -2, -1, -2, -1, -2> (beat 1 = 0; beats 2 and 3 both = -1; all off-beat eighths = -2)
  - 6/8 to eighth level: <0, -2, -2, -1, -2, -2> (beat 1 = 0; second dotted-quarter beat = -1; the other four eighths = -2)
  — [Song thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)
- LHL syncopation score: write the rhythm as a sequence of notes (N) and rests/ties (R), each with its weight. For each rest R_i find the nearest preceding note N_j. If weight(R_i) >= weight(N_j), add weight(R_i) - weight(N_j). The total is the score. Worked example, son clave: two qualifying pairs each scoring 2, total 4. — [Song thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)
- LHL in words (independent confirmation): syncopation occurs when a rest or tied note sits at a higher metrical level than the preceding sounded note; its strength is the difference of the two levels. The LHL and Lerdahl-Jackendoff schemes both give five distinct levels for a 4/4 bar at sixteenth resolution. — [Ladinig & Honing, "Complexity judgments as a measure of event salience"](https://eprints.illc.uva.nl/id/document/1077)
- Pressing's cognitive-complexity model (PRS) uses pattern costs instead of weights: null 0, filled 1, run 2, upbeat 3, syncopated 5; costs are summed at each level and divided by the number of units at that level. Son clave = 2/1 + 7/2 + 12/4 + 5/8 = 9.125. — [Song thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)
- Which models handle triple subdivisions: LHL, Pressing, Toussaint's metrical complexity (TMC), weighted note-to-beat distance (WNBD) and Toussaint's off-beatness (TOB) do; Sioros & Guedes (SG) and Keith (KTH) are duple only. The thesis tested only 4/4 and 6/8. — [Song thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)
- Measured (not theoretical) salience in 4/4 departs from the tidy tree. Ladinig & Honing's listener-derived salience for sixteenth positions 2 to 16 (position 1 excluded): 0.78, 0.96, 0.83, 1.86, 0.77, 0.92, 0.80, 1.69, 0.68, 0.83, 0.70, 1.33, 0.79, 1.00, 0.79. Beat 2 (1.86) came out stronger than beat 3 (1.69) and beat 4 (1.33): a primacy effect, strength decaying through the bar, with a small rise at the very end. Triple metre was not tested. — [Ladinig & Honing](https://eprints.illc.uva.nl/id/document/1077)
- Palmer & Krumhansl (1990) reported profiles for 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 and 6/8 (their Fig. 2, panels a to d), from note-onset counts in classical keyboard music and from listener ratings, and found "a strong correlation between onset frequencies and patterns of accent"; musicians showed "deeper hierarchical differentiation" than non-musicians. — [London, Polak & Jacoby 2016](https://core.ac.uk/download/83231804.pdf); [van der Weij, Pearce & Honing 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)
- Onset frequency tracking metrical weight is a property of Western repertoires, not a law. In Malian drumming the downbeat is most frequent but the upbeat just before each beat is more frequent than the beat itself; in Turkish makam the fit to a hierarchy is weaker. — [London, Polak & Jacoby 2016](https://core.ac.uk/download/83231804.pdf)
- Huron & Ommen's "lacuna" definition of syncopation (the same idea as LHL, stated on a numbered hierarchy where 1 = downbeat, 2 = beat 3 in 4/4): an onset at a weaker level is held through a stronger point that has no onset. — [Shanahan, empirical methods workshop page](https://shanahdt.github.io/mus-345-445/emp_methods_workshop/syncopation.html)

### Inferences
- 12/8 weights by the same rule (subdivisions <1,2,2,3>), LHL style, twelve eighths: <0, -3, -3, -2, -3, -3, -1, -3, -3, -2, -3, -3>. That is: bar 0, half-bar (beat 3) -1, beats 2 and 4 = -2, all other eighths -3. This is my application of the cited rule; the source did not print 12/8.
- 12/8 is 6/8 with one more duple level on top, exactly as 4/4 is 2/4 with one more level. So a rule stated "per level counted from the beat" (beat level, one above, one below) transfers between 6/8 and 12/8 with no change.
- For the engine, index positions by level relative to the tactus (the felt beat), not by slot number: T+1 (bar or half-bar), T (beat), T-1 (first division), T-2 (second division). 4/4 pop: T = quarter, T-1 = eighth (2 per beat). 6/8 and 12/8: T = dotted quarter, T-1 = eighth (3 per beat). 3/4: T = quarter, 3 per bar, T-1 = eighth (2 per beat). The only structural novelties in triple and compound are (i) a level with three children, giving two equal-weight "weak" siblings where duple has one, and (ii) whether those two siblings behave alike.
- Do beats 2 and 3 of 3/4 differ? In theory, no: every tree scheme gives them identical weight. The measured 4/4 primacy effect (earlier weak positions stronger than later ones) suggests beat 2 could be slightly stronger than beat 3, but the same paper also found a recency rise at the bar's end, and the pickup function of beat 3 argues the other way. Treat it as an open empirical parameter to estimate from the 3/4 songbook data the engine already has, not something to assume.

### Gaps
- Palmer & Krumhansl's actual numbers for 2/4, 3/4, 4/4, 6/8 were not recoverable: no open copy was reachable and every secondary source points to their Figure 2 without tabulating it. Whether their 3/4 profile separates beats 2 and 3 is therefore unverified here.
- Witek et al. (2014) index: the PLoS/PMC pages were blocked. From memory only (unverified): it is LHL-style, summing over syncopations the weight difference between the rest position and the note position plus an instrument-pair weight, on a 4/4 sixteenth grid with weights 0 down to -4; pleasure and wanting-to-move follow an inverted U against the index. Do not quote numbers from this without checking the paper.
- Formulas for Toussaint's metrical complexity, off-beatness, WNBD, Keith and Sioros & Guedes were in a part of the thesis beyond what could be read. From memory only (unverified): off-beatness counts onsets at positions of an n-cycle not hit by any regular polygon dividing n, so it is undefined or trivial for prime n and behaves differently for n = 12 versus n = 16.
- Lerdahl & Jackendoff's own dot grids were not read directly; confirmed only through secondary description as equivalent to the tree above.

## 2. Probabilistic models that predict note onsets from metrical position (Temperley)

### Takeaway
Temperley's test of six models on 4/4 folk songs and string quartets shows that a model with one onset probability per metrical level already captures most of the structure, and adding "is the neighbouring stronger beat occupied?" (the hierarchical model, 13 parameters) nearly matches a 56-parameter note-to-note model. Temperley himself notes that the hierarchical model's lower-level parameters could be reused in 3/4, which is the published seed of a metre-transfer idea.

### Cited Findings
- The six models, with parameter counts for the Essen folk corpus and cross-entropy per song (lower is better), then per movement for Haydn/Mozart quartets:

  | Model | What decides a note | Params (Essen) | Essen | Quartets |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 1 Uniform position | same chance at every slot (P = .51) | 1 | 62.37 | 1150.14 |
  | 2 Zeroth-order duration | each gap between notes drawn independently | 15 | 54.45 | 943.89 |
  | 3 Metrical position | one probability per metrical level | 4 | 42.47 | 965.94 |
  | 4 Fine-grained position | one probability per slot in the bar (8 slots) | 8 | 40.21 | 959.42 |
  | 5 Hierarchical position | level plus whether adjacent stronger beats have notes | 13 | 38.76 | 819.37 |
  | 6 First-order metrical duration | slot of next note given slot of this note | 56 | 37.36 | 783.79 |

  — [Temperley 2010, "Modeling Common-Practice Rhythm", Music Perception](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Scoring formula: cross-entropy per item = -(1/N) * sum over items of log P(item), on held-out test items. — [Temperley 2010](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Data and hold-out design: Essen restricted to 4/4 with nothing shorter than an eighth, 1,235 melodies (988 train, 247 test); quartets restricted to 4/4, 63 movements (32 train, 31 test), first violin only. K-fold cross-validation on Essen moved each score by under 1% and kept the ranking. — [Temperley 2010](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Some of the fitted numbers (Essen): eighth-note level (L1) note probability .21 in the metrical position model; whole-bar level .998 (quartets .891). Hierarchical model at the half-bar level (L2): note probability .713 when both neighbouring stronger beats have notes, .889 when only the preceding one does, .265 when only the following one does, .062 when neither does. — [Temperley 2010](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Style difference visible in the parameters: in folk songs "note before, none after" contexts beat "both sides" contexts at all levels; in the quartets the reverse; the quartets also have a relatively high probability of notes on weak positions with neither neighbour sounded, which Temperley links to syncopation. — [Temperley 2010](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- Transfer to 3/4, as discussed by Temperley (not tested): the hierarchical model would need about 19 parameters in 3/4, of which 6 (the two lowest levels) could be shared with 4/4; the first-order model would need a wholly new set of about 132 (12 x 11) with no obvious sharing. 6/8 is not analysed. — [Temperley 2010](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- A later model in the same family infers metre from onsets by Bayes' rule, with prior p(metre) = share of that metre in the training set, position = (onset - phase) mod bar length, and surprise = -log2 p(event); trained on Essen in 2/4, 3/4 and 6/8. — [van der Weij, Pearce & Honing 2017, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439009)

### Inferences
- The level-based models (3 and 5) are the ones that transfer: their parameters are attached to "level" and "is the neighbour sounded", which exist in every metre. The slot-based models (4 and 6) fit slightly better but their parameters are tied to one metre and cannot be carried over. This is the bias-variance trade the engine faces: for an empty cell, choose the level-indexed form.
- The hierarchical model's four contexts are the natural language for stress placement too: a stressed syllable on a weak slot with no note on the following strong beat ("pre-anchored" absent, i.e. unanchored-after) is exactly a syncopation in the LHL and Tan et al. senses.

### Gaps
- "Music and Probability" (2007) rhythm-model parameters (the per-level onset probabilities used in its metre-finding model) were not retrieved; no number is given here.
- The full per-level and per-slot probability charts of Temperley 2010 are in figures and were only partly recoverable.
- No published fit of these six models to 3/4, 6/8 or 12/8 corpora, or to pop corpora, was found.

## 3. Syncopation and stress-beat alignment: pop versus classical, songbook and folk; and evidence for triple-metre pop

### Takeaway
In 4/4 rock about one stressed syllable in three is syncopated (22.8% at the eighth level plus 10.5% at the sixteenth level), three quarters of those syncopations anticipate a strong beat, and 19th-century English songs measured the same way have none. No measured figure for syncopation or stress alignment in 3/4, 6/8 or 12/8 pop was found anywhere: every corpus study located excluded those songs, so the empty cell is empty in the literature as well as in the engine's data.

### Cited Findings
Tan, Lustig & Temperley (2019), "Anticipatory Syncopation in Rock: A Corpus Study", Music Perception 36(4):
- Corpus: 80 songs, 1955 to 1997, from the 100 top-ranked Rolling Stone songs (20 per decade); 23,032 notes in 4/4. 20 of the 100 were dropped: 17 for time signatures other than 4/4 or 2/4, 3 rap songs. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Stress coding from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary: primary 43.5%, secondary 1.8% (both counted stressed), unstressed 50.3%, non-words 4.4%. All one-syllable function words were forced to unstressed (they are 31% of word tokens). Later notes of a melisma were dropped so each syllable is one note. — [same](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Definition: a syncopation at level n is a stressed syllable on a weak n-th-note position not followed by another syllable on or before the next n-th-note beat. — [same](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Rates. SQ (syncopation quotient) = syncopations / stressed syllables: eighth level 2,382 / 10,433 = 0.228; sixteenth level 0.105; combined 0.333. Comparison corpus (10 songs from "Songs of England", 1,397 notes): 0 of 602 stressed syllables; chi-square(1) = 240.1, p < .0001. — [same](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Anticipation. ASQ = share of syncopations that fall just before (rather than just after) the stronger beats: eighth level 0.772, sixteenth level 0.740, combined 0.762; 66 of 76 songs above 0.5 at the eighth level. — [same](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- By target beat (eighth positions numbered 1 to 8; "1 -> 8" means a stress that could sit on beat 1 sits an eighth early):

  | Target beat | On the beat | An eighth early | Share early |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Beat 1 (1 -> 8) | 1,028 | 1,046 | 0.504 |
  | Beat 2 (3 -> 2) | 389 | 305 | 0.439 |
  | Beat 3 (5 -> 4) | 639 | 794 | 0.554 |
  | Beat 4 (7 -> 6) | 438 | 237 | 0.351 |

  All adjacent differences significant (chi-square 8.2 to 10.8, p < .005). — [same](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Covariates: SQ rises with tempo and with year at the eighth level (logistic coefficients 0.0119 per BPM and 0.0337 per year, both p < .001) and at the sixteenth level (0.0402 per BPM, 0.0361 per year); eighth-level and sixteenth-level SQ are negatively correlated across songs (r = -.52); tempo and year correlate r = -.54. — [same](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Function words are not simply "weak": share landing on a quarter-note beat: I .313, the .250, you .335, a .256, to .355, and .345, my .352, in .562, it .122, me .331. — [same](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)

Other style comparisons:
- Temperley (2021), "The origins of syncopation in American popular music": distinguishes second-position syncopation ("an accent on the second quarter of a half-note or quarter-note unit") from fourth-position syncopation ("on the fourth quarter of such a unit"), which "tends to have an anticipatory character". — [Popular Music, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/popular-music/article/origins-of-syncopation-in-american-popular-music/941F3ADA4C6254F1D26025152F4806F3)
- As summarised by White, Pater & Breen: Temperley found second-position syncopation especially common in 19th-century British folk song and early 20th-century American popular song, and Tan et al. found the anticipatory type characteristic of rock. — [White, Pater & Breen 2022, J. Mathematics and Music](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10356498)
- Huron & Ommen (2006): 437 American popular recordings 1890 to 1939, one randomly chosen bar transcribed per work; syncopation density roughly doubled over the 50 years "from just over one to nearly two syncopations per unit"; variety of patterns peaked in the 1910s then fell while density kept rising. — [Shanahan workshop summary](https://shanahdt.github.io/mus-345-445/emp_methods_workshop/syncopation.html)
- Pop then versus now, by entropy of each song's melodic onset profile over metrical positions (0 = all onsets on one position, 1 = evenly spread): 1955 to 1997 corpus 0.81 to 0.83; 2015 to 2019 corpus 0.89 to 0.93 (pop 0.86 to 0.89; rap/hip-hop/trap 0.93 to 0.96). — [White, Pater & Breen 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10356498)
- Folk versus classical in onset placement: see Temperley 2010 parameters in section 2. — [Temperley 2010](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)

Share of pop in triple or compound metre, and what is known about it:
- de Clercq's tabulation of three pop/rock corpora: 4/4 = 61 songs (75.3%) in Covach 2009, 509 (93.2%) in the McGill Billboard set (Burgoyne et al. 2011), 168 (84.0%) in the Rolling Stone 200 (Temperley & de Clercq 2013). 6/8 = 1 song (1.2%), 5 (0.9%), 6 (3.0%) respectively. 3/4 and 12/8 shares are not given. — [de Clercq 2015 slides](https://www.midside.com/presentations/declercq_2015_ann_arbor_slides.pdf)
- Tempo and bar length: 4/4 songs average 119.5 to 121.4 BPM, bar about 2.0 s; 6/8 songs average 50 to 64 BPM, bar 1.9 to 2.4 s. de Clercq's conclusion: bars last about two seconds whatever the signature, and "absolute time should be considered in bar length decisions". — [de Clercq 2015 slides](https://www.midside.com/presentations/declercq_2015_ann_arbor_slides.pdf)
- Exclusions in the stress and rhythm studies: Tan et al. dropped 17 of 100 songs for not being 4/4 or 2/4; White et al. dropped 8 older songs "annotated in compound or triple meter, or included some encoding error" and kept only quadruple songs (24 of 25) in the recent set. Neither reports anything on the excluded songs. — [Tan et al. 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf); [White et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10356498)
- CoCoPops (Arthur & Condit-Schultz, ISMIR 2023): 398 songs 1949 to 2002, merging McGill Billboard and RS200, 145,822 note onsets, 37,010 chord changes, melodies on a sixteenth grid; 80 RS200 files carry lyrics and a three-level stress track. Licence CC-BY-4.0, on GitHub. The paper reports no time-signature shares and no syncopation results. — [CoCoPops, ISMIR 2023](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000027.pdf)

### Inferences
- The "syncopation shift" in numbers: in 4/4 rock a stressed syllable aimed at a quarter-note beat arrives an eighth early about 35% to 55% of the time, more often the stronger the target (beats 1 and 3 about half, beat 4 about a third). In songbook-era and folk material measured the same way, the rate is near zero.
- Triple and compound pop is small but not negligible: 6/8 alone is 1% to 3%; "not 4/4 or 2/4" is 17% of the top Rolling Stone songs, though that 17% mixes 12/8, 6/8, 3/4, shuffles written in 12/8 and encoding choices, so it is an upper bound. Only the 80 lyric-bearing RS200 files have stress data, and all 80 are 4/4, so even the best open corpus has zero worded triple-metre pop songs. The 17 to 20 excluded RS songs would be the first place to look if someone added lyrics and stress to them.
- Tempo matters for transfer. Tan et al. show syncopation rate depends on tempo, and de Clercq shows 6/8 pop is slow with the same two-second bar. Arithmetic: at 120 BPM in 4/4 an eighth lasts 250 ms and a sixteenth 125 ms; at 62 dotted-quarter BPM in 6/8 an eighth lasts about 320 ms and a sixteenth 160 ms. So the 6/8 eighth sits between the 4/4 eighth and quarter in clock time. If anticipation is a clock-time habit (arrive about 125 to 250 ms early), then in 6/8 pop the likely anticipation is by one eighth (320 ms, the last third of the beat) at slow tempos, and by a sixteenth where sixteenths are sung. This is a hypothesis, not a finding.

### Gaps
- No measurement of syncopation or stress-beat alignment in 3/4, 6/8 or 12/8 pop, rock, soul or country was found. "The Musical Language of Rock" (2018) and "Syncopation in rock: a perceptual perspective" (1999) could not be opened; whether either gives a compound-metre example or rule is unverified.
- Huron & Ommen's decade-by-decade values are in figures only and were not recovered.
- Temperley 2021's per-corpus rates are behind a paywall.
- 3/4 and 12/8 shares in Billboard-type corpora were not found as published figures.
- Licences not verified in this pass: Essen Folksong Collection, RS200/Rolling Stone corpus, McGill Billboard, CMU Pronouncing Dictionary, Hayes's textsetting data. Only CoCoPops (CC-BY-4.0) was confirmed.

## 4. Transfer mathematics: metre factor times style factor, mapping between metres, and how to test it

### Takeaway
No published model was found that factorises a rhythm statistic into a metre effect and a style effect or completes a genre-by-metre table; the nearest published pieces are Temperley's parameter-sharing remark for 3/4, Tan et al.'s logistic regression of syncopation on tempo and year, and the common weight-tree construction. The simplest defensible prediction is an additive model on the log-odds scale, indexed by metrical level rather than slot, and it can be checked by hiding a cell the engine does have.

### Cited Findings
- Parameter sharing across metres is anticipated in print: Temperley's hierarchical model in 3/4 would reuse 6 of about 19 parameters from 4/4 (the lowest two levels); slot-to-slot models share nothing. — [Temperley 2010](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- A published additive (log-linear) model of a stress-syncopation rate exists, but with tempo and year as the factors, not metre: logit-type regression of each song's SQ on BPM and release year, both significant. — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- One construction generates the weights for all metres from a list of subdivision factors (<1,2,2>, <1,3,2>, <1,2,3>), so any rule written on weights is automatically defined in every metre. — [Song thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/77042192.pdf)
- Discrete Fourier transform of a rhythm (Amiot; Yust's summary): for a set A of onset positions in a cycle of n slots, coefficient k = sum over x in A of exp(-2*pi*i*k*x/n). Its size says how strongly the rhythm follows a k-fold periodicity; its phase says where that periodicity sits relative to slot 0. Coefficients k and n-k are mirror images. The transform is invertible, so it holds the same information as the onset list. Sets generated by a constant step (maximally even, i.e. Euclidean-type rhythms) are the ones that maximise a single coefficient. — [Yust, review of Amiot "Music Through Fourier Space", MTO 23.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.yust.html)
- Limits stated in that review: Amiot's broader conjectures linking Fourier properties to musical quality are "undoubtedly bold and unproven". The review does not discuss 3/4 versus 6/8. — [Yust MTO 23.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.yust.html)
- Hold-out practice in this literature: train/test split scored by cross-entropy, checked by k-fold cross-validation. — [Temperley 2010](http://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-mp10.pdf)
- For a two-way table with one value per cell, the additive model y_ij = mu + a_i + b_j + error leaves no degrees of freedom to test a general interaction; Tukey's one-degree-of-freedom test checks the specific form y_ij = mu + a_i + b_j + lambda*a_i*b_j. — [Tukey's test of additivity, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tukey%27s_test_of_additivity)
- Speech-rhythm contrast (nPVI) in instrumental themes does not differ by metre type: binary 42.98 versus ternary 45.25, p = 0.28. — [Patel & Daniele 2003, Cognition](https://ccc.inaoep.mx/~villasen/bib/ritmo%20lenguage%20y%20musical%20-Patel_Daniele_Cognition.pdf)

### Inferences (design proposal; none of this is published)
- The additive model. Let p(m, s, c) be the probability that a stressed syllable lands in context c (for example "on a beat", "on the first division after a beat", "on the last division before a beat with the beat left empty") in metre m and style s. Assume
  logit p(m, s, c) = mu_c + alpha_c(m) + beta_c(s),
  where logit p = ln(p / (1 - p)). Then the missing cell is
  logit p(3/4, pop) = logit p(3/4, songbook) + logit p(4/4, pop) - logit p(4/4, songbook).
  In odds terms: odds(3/4 pop) = odds(3/4 songbook) x [odds(4/4 pop) / odds(4/4 songbook)]. The bracket is the "pop factor". This is the same arithmetic as a difference-in-differences estimate, or a rank-one completion of a 2 x 2 table.
- Worked illustration with the one pair of published numbers available: eighth-level stress syncopation is 0.228 in rock and 0 of 602 in 19th-century song. Zero cannot be put on a log-odds scale, so add half a count: 0.5/603 = 0.00083. The pop factor on odds is then (0.228/0.772) / 0.00083 = about 356. A factor that large, applied to any small songbook rate, gives nonsense or saturates. Lesson: when the base style has almost none of the behaviour, a multiplicative style factor is not identifiable. The pop behaviour should instead be modelled as a separate process added on top.
- The better form: a two-step (mixture) model. Step 1, the "plain" placement: assign each stressed syllable to a position using the metre's own songbook-style distribution (known for 4/4, 3/4, 6/8). Step 2, the "pop shift": with probability q(target strength, tempo) move that syllable one division early and leave the target beat unsung. q is measured in 4/4 pop: about 0.50 before beat 1, 0.55 before beat 3, 0.44 before beat 2, 0.35 before beat 4 at the eighth level (Tan et al. table above). Transfer by level: in 6/8 the targets are the two dotted-quarter beats and "one division early" is the third eighth of the previous beat; in 12/8 the four beats map onto 4/4's four beats one-for-one; in 3/4 the targets are the three quarter beats and the early position is the off-beat eighth. This is exactly Temperley's "syncopation shift" made into a probability, and it has the factorised form metre (step 1) composed with style (step 2).
- The choices that need testing, because the literature cannot settle them: (a) whether q attaches to the notated level or to clock time (see section 3: a slow 6/8 eighth is 320 ms, longer than a 4/4 eighth); (b) in a three-way division, whether the shift goes only to the third division (anticipation) or also to the second; (c) whether beats 2 and 3 of 3/4 take the 4/4 "beat 2/4" rate or something else; (d) whether 12/8 pop behaves as 4/4-with-triplets (the one-to-one map) or as doubled 6/8.
- Why level-indexing and not slot-indexing or Fourier mapping: Temperley's results show the level-based models lose little fit against slot-based ones while keeping parameters that exist in all metres. The Fourier view is useful as a descriptor (in a 12-slot cycle, the size of coefficient 4 measures "four even beats" and coefficient 3 measures "three even beats", so the ratio separates a 6/8 feel from a 3/4 feel in the same six or twelve slots, and a hemiola shows as energy moving between them), but no published rule maps a 16-slot pattern onto a 12-slot one through it, and the review cited does not discuss this use. Treat it as a diagnostic, not a transfer mechanism.
- Hold-out validation with the cells the engine has. With cells 4/4 pop (A), 4/4 songbook (B), 3/4 songbook (C), 6/8 songbook (D) and unknown 3/4 pop and 6/8 pop:
  1. Metre-transfer test (no style change): hide C. Predict 3/4 songbook from 4/4 songbook using the level-indexed mapping alone. Score by cross-entropy per stressed syllable against the real C, and compare with two baselines: the uniform distribution over slots, and the theoretical weight profile. Repeat hiding D, and hiding C while training on D (6/8 to 3/4). This tests the metre half of the model directly on real data.
  2. Style-transfer test inside 4/4: split 4/4 by era or sub-style (for example early versus late pop, since Tan et al. show a year effect). Fit the shift q on one, predict the other. This tests whether the style half is a stable operator.
  3. Additivity test: a 2 x 2 table with one empty cell has zero spare degrees of freedom, so additivity cannot be tested there. Get spare degrees of freedom by (i) treating contexts/levels as repeated measures, giving a metre x style x context table, and (ii) adding a third style or metre level with data, such as hymn or folk in 4/4, 3/4, 6/8, which makes a 3 x 3 table with every cell but pop-triple filled; then hide each known cell in turn, predict it from the rest, and report the error. The spread of those leave-one-cell-out errors is the honest error bar for the pop-triple prediction. Tukey's one-degree-of-freedom test can be run on the filled part.
  4. External spot check: hand-annotate a small set of well-known 3/4, 6/8 and 12/8 pop songs (even 10 to 20, such as the 17 excluded Rolling Stone songs) for stress position and compare observed shift rates with predicted q. Even a small sample distinguishes "q about 0.4" from "q about 0".
- Hierarchical Bayesian version, if wanted later: put a shared prior on q across metres, q_m ~ Beta centred on the 4/4 value, with the prior width set from the leave-one-cell-out error in step 3. With no pop-triple data the posterior is the prior (the transfer estimate with an error bar); every annotated song then updates it. This is standard partial pooling; no music-specific source for it was found.

### Gaps
- No published log-linear, hierarchical Bayesian, matrix-completion, tensor-completion or domain-adaptation model over a genre-by-metre table of rhythm statistics was found. Absence in this search is not proof of absence, but nothing surfaced in Music Perception, JNMR, MTO, EMR or ISMIR results.
- Amiot's book itself, Toussaint's "Geometry of Musical Rhythm" and London's "Hearing in Time" were not opened; statements about Fourier coefficients for divisors of 12 separating 3/4 from 6/8 are my reading of the definition, not a quoted result. London's tempo-dependent limits on metre (the roughly 100 ms shortest subdivision and 5 to 6 s longest bar, from memory, unverified) would bear on choice (a) above.
- No published formal mapping of a 4/4 pattern to its 12/8 or 6/8 counterpart beyond the informal shuffle/triplet equivalence was found.

## 5. Speech side: stress timing, nPVI, and predicting sung rhythm from the words alone

### Takeaway
English speech has high durational contrast between neighbouring syllables (nPVI about 67 against French 49), and that contrast shows up, diluted, in English composers' instrumental themes (47 against 41) regardless of binary or ternary metre. The best published words-to-rhythm model (Hayes's constraint ranking for English folk verse) predicts singers' settings for about 71% of lines from binary stress and syllable count alone, but it is built on a duple, four-beat grid and has not been published for triple metre.

### Cited Findings
- nPVI formula: nPVI = 100/(m-1) x sum for k = 1 to m-1 of |(d_k - d_(k+1)) / ((d_k + d_(k+1))/2)|, where d_k is the duration of the k-th vowel interval (speech) or note (music) and m is the count. (The fetched text rendered the denominator imperfectly; the mean-of-pair form is the standard one.) — [Patel & Daniele 2003, Cognition 87](https://ccc.inaoep.mx/~villasen/bib/ritmo%20lenguage%20y%20musical%20-Patel_Daniele_Cognition.pdf)
- Speech: British English 66.99 (SE 2.89, n = 20 utterances), French 49.27 (SE 2.78, n = 20); p < 0.001. Music (instrumental themes): English composers 46.91 (SE 1.81, 137 themes, 6 composers), French 40.90 (SE 1.97, 181 themes, 10 composers); p < 0.01. — [Patel & Daniele 2003](https://ccc.inaoep.mx/~villasen/bib/ritmo%20lenguage%20y%20musical%20-Patel_Daniele_Cognition.pdf)
- Metre does not drive nPVI: binary-metre themes 42.98 versus ternary 45.25, p = 0.28; the two nations did not differ in their mix of binary and ternary (p = 0.59). Song-derived pieces, dances and marches were excluded. — [Patel & Daniele 2003](https://ccc.inaoep.mx/~villasen/bib/ritmo%20lenguage%20y%20musical%20-Patel_Daniele_Cognition.pdf)
- Caution on carrying speech-rhythm findings into melody: Temperley (2017) is cited as questioning whether they apply to instrumental melody. — [White, Pater & Breen 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10356498)
- Hayes, "Textsetting as constraint conflict": four competing goals: (a) match stress to strong positions, (b) avoid long stretches with no syllable starting, (c) avoid squeezing syllables too tightly, (d) align phrase ends with line ends. — [Hayes, landing page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)
- The implemented grammar: a four-beat line with 16 slots at three strengths, alternating S, W, M, W (S = 4 strong beats, M = 4 mid slots, W = 8 weak slots). Inputs: binary stress per syllable and syllable count; phrasing is not used in the implementation. Ten constraints in strict rank order, highest first: (1) no syllable in an extra-weak slot; (2) no overflow of the line; (3) no three empty slots in a row; (4) no empty S; (5) no stressless syllable in S; (6) no stressed syllable in W; (7) no stressless syllable in W; (8) "strong is long" (after each S, count the W slots before the next S and subtract from 3; sum); (9) no stressed syllable in M; (10) no empty M. Ranking found by greedy hill-climbing; no numeric weights. — [Hayes, paper PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Accuracy on 364 four-beat folk-verse lines where at least 4 of 9 native speakers agreed on a setting: Hayes's grammar matched the most common setting on 70.6% of lines, plus 6.9% ties for first; the Halle & Lerdahl syllable-distribution algorithm (as implemented by Halle 1999) matched 23.1% (84 lines). The paper does not discuss triple metre. — [Hayes, paper PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Data availability: Hayes posts the 346/364-line stress and setting data and the longer 640-line Hayes & Kaun (1996) set (with syllable weight and phrasing) on his site. — [Hayes, landing page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)
- In sung rock, the dictionary stress of a word does not fix its beat strength: common function words land on a quarter beat 12% to 56% of the time, word by word (see section 3 table). — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)

### Inferences
- A metre-independent "speech layer" is defensible: Patel & Daniele's result that nPVI does not differ between binary and ternary themes says the long-short contrast inherited from English is a property of the language, carried into either metre. So the engine can hold one prosody-derived preference (stressed syllables longer and on stronger slots; no long gaps; no crowding) and let only the grid change.
- Hayes's constraints are stated in terms of slot strength (S, M, W), gaps and crowding, not slot number, so they can be re-pointed at a triple grid by relabelling: in 6/8 the two beats are S (or S and M), the other eighths W; in 3/4 beat 1 is S, beats 2 and 3 are M, off-beat eighths W. Two constraints need re-deriving for threes: "no three empty slots in a row" (a lapse threshold tuned to a duple grid; in a triple division two empty slots between beats is normal) and "strong is long" (its "subtract from 3" constant is tied to four slots per beat). That re-derivation can be fitted on the 3/4 and 6/8 songbook data the engine has.
- Hayes's ranking describes the "plain" (folk/songbook) placement of section 4's step 1. The pop shift then corresponds to demoting constraint 4 (no empty strong slot) and constraint 6 (no stressed syllable in a weak slot) in the specific case where the weak slot immediately precedes the empty strong one. That gives a concrete, testable way to express "pop" as a re-ranking rather than a new grammar.
- 71% line-level agreement is a realistic ceiling for stress-only prediction of plain settings; expect lower for pop, where function-word placement is word-specific.

### Gaps
- Isochrony evidence (whether English stresses really recur at equal intervals) was not searched in this pass. From memory only (unverified): instrumental studies since the 1980s (Roach, Dauer) found no strict isochrony in English inter-stress intervals, and the stress-timed versus syllable-timed distinction is now usually recast as a gradient of durational variability, which is what nPVI measures.
- No model was found that predicts sung rhythm from spoken prosody alone and is validated in triple or compound metre. Halle & Lerdahl (1993) and Hayes & Kaun (1996) were not opened directly.
- Other related work seen in results but not read: a Cambridge "English Language and Linguistics" paper on syllable weight and natural duration in English popular-music textsetting; Dell & Halle comparing French and English textsetting; Temperley & Temperley on the Scotch snap. These may hold further numbers.
- Licence of Hayes's data files and of the CMU dictionary not verified.
