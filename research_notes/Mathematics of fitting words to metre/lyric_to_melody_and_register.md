# Lyric-to-melody / lyric-to-rhythm formalisations, and measured statistics of pitch register and pitch concentration in popular song

Reading note. Every number below was read from the cited page in this session unless it is in a Gaps list. Where a paper prints a value only in a figure, that is said. No data from TheSession.org was used or downloaded; one cited paper (melodic diversity, arXiv 2408.12635) names it among its corpora, and only that paper's published aggregate findings are reported.

## 1. Lyric-to-melody and lyric-to-rhythm systems (2018 to 2026): what explicit alignment rules, templates and statistics do they give?

### Takeaway
Only three of the systems read give rules that can be lifted straight into a rule engine: ReLyMe (additive reward terms for strong/weak match, pause placement, sentence contour and structural repetition, with published weights), TeleMelody (a four-part template: tonality, chord, beat-level onset position 0 to 3, cadence class from punctuation, plus numeric cadence-extraction thresholds), and Tian et al.'s LYRA (note is "stressed" if longer than the average note; real songs obey that rule on only 73.3% of lines). The rest (SongMASS, LSTM-GAN, SongGLM, CSL-L2M, Songs Across Borders) are learned models whose reusable content is their feature definitions and dataset statistics, not rules.

### Cited Findings

**ReLyMe (Zhang et al., ACM MM 2022): explicit rules and weights**
- Decoding rule: ordinary beam score s_i = log P(y_i | y_0:i-1, x) is replaced by s'_i = log P(y_i | y_0:i-1, x) + R_cons(y_0:i, x), with R_cons = R_t + R_r + R_s (tone, rhythm, structure). — [ReLyMe, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.05688)
- Tone rules. (a) Pitch shape of a single tone, used when one syllable carries more than one note: R = 1 if the note group's pitch movement matches the tone's shape, else 0. (b) Pitch transition between adjacent syllables: Δp_j = p_j − p_(j−1) is graded excellent / good / fair / bad for the tone pair, reward 3 / 2 / 1 / 0 (grading table is in the paper's Figure 4, from Mandarin sources, not reproduced in the text). (c) Sentence pitch contour: R_pc = 1 if the melody's pitch direction matches the sentence's intonation (e.g. rising for a question; punctuation is kept to signal this), else 0. For English and other stress-accent languages only rule (c) is applied; (a) and (b) are for tonal languages. — [ReLyMe, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.05688)
- Rhythm rules. R_r = R_sw + pause reward. Strong/weak: R_sw = 1 if the lyric's strong/weak positions match the melody's, else 0; melody beat strength is read from a predefined time signature; keywords belong on strong positions, auxiliary (function) words on weak ones. Pauses: a pause is a REST or a long note; three dissonance cases are named: a REST inside a prosodic phrase; a long note on a non-final syllable of a phrase; no REST or long note at the end of a sentence. Pause reward = 1 when pauses sit at word or sentence split positions, 0 when a pause is not at a split or when there is no pause between two lyric sentences. — [ReLyMe, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.05688)
- Structure rule. Build a matrix M with M_ij = 1 if note i and note j sit at corresponding positions in phrases with the same lyric structure. Reward R_s = 2 when the pitch-difference sequence matches (Δp_i = Δp_j for some earlier j with M_ij = 1, i.e. the same contour allowing an overall shift), R_s = 1 when Δp_i − Δp_j is a multiple of 12, else 0. The page text states these two conditions without fully explaining how they relate; check the repository before copying. — [ReLyMe, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.05688)
- Published weights. In TeleMelody: lyric-to-template stage uses log P + λ_r R_r; template-to-melody stage uses log P + λ_t R_t + λ_s R_s, with λ_t = 1.2, λ_r = 1.5, λ_s = 1. In SongMASS (one stage): log P + λ_t R_t + λ_r R_r + λ_s R_s with λ_t = 1.5, λ_r = 1, λ_s = 1. Sampling: top-k 2 and 10 in TeleMelody's two modules, 5 in SongMASS; temperature 0.5 for evaluation. Evaluation used 100 English and 100 Chinese held-out samples. — [ReLyMe, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.05688)
- Code: github.com/microsoft/muzic (licence not stated on the paper page). — [ReLyMe, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.05688)

**TeleMelody (Ju et al., EMNLP 2022): template method**
- Template = four musical elements. Tonality: C major or A minor only (all else transposed), one token at sequence start. Chord: root (12) × quality (7: major, minor, diminished, augmented, major7, minor7, half-diminished) = 84 chords, one chord token per note. Rhythm pattern: four tokens 0 to 3 giving the beat-level onset position of the note within the bar. Cadence: three classes, none / half / authentic. Each note carries three template tokens (chord, rhythm, cadence). — [TeleMelody, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09617)
- Alignment basis: one note per syllable (English) or per character (Chinese). — [TeleMelody, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09617)
- Cadence from lyrics at inference is a fixed mapping: full stop → authentic cadence, comma → half cadence, every other syllable → no cadence. — [TeleMelody, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09617)
- Cadence extraction from melodies (for training) is rule-based: "no cadence" if the note is short or the next onset is under 1.5 beats away; "authentic" for tonic-chord root notes, and with probability p (e.g. 0.3) for other tonic-chord notes followed by an onset interval over 2 beats; everything else that qualifies is "half". — [TeleMelody, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09617)
- Cadence statistics in the 157,702-sample template-melody set (units of a sixteenth note): no-cadence notes average 24.57 per sample, duration 1.74, onset interval 2.29; half-cadence notes 2.53 per sample, duration 8.54, onset interval 9.66; authentic-cadence notes 1.66 per sample, duration 10.34, onset interval 11.70. No-cadence notes are 5.86 times as common as the two cadence types together. — [TeleMelody, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09617)
- Melody encoding: per note four tokens: bar (256 values), position (16 values, sixteenth-note grid), pitch (128), duration (16 values, sixteenth to whole note). — [TeleMelody, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09617)
- Lyric-to-rhythm is a learned Transformer (4+4 layers, hidden 256), not a rule. Training pairs were made by crawling sung audio, separating vocals (Spleeter), forced alignment (Montreal Forced Aligner) and beat tracking (librosa): 9,761 English and 74,328 Chinese samples. English samples average 26.02 words, 31.15 syllables and 3.79 punctuation marks. Template-melody data: 157,702 samples averaging 28.76 notes and 5.93 bars, from about 45,129 LMD-matched MIDI files. — [TeleMelody, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09617)
- A pure hand-rule fallback exists and is measurably worse: with "hand-crafted rules (no paired data)" for the lyric-to-rhythm step, English scores were PD 35.60 / DD 47.93 / MD 2.55 against 43.86 / 52.06 / 2.40 with the learned rhythm model (PD, DD = pitch and duration distribution similarity to ground truth, higher better; MD = melody distance, lower better). — [TeleMelody, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09617)
- Controllability: the generated melody honours the rhythm template 99.88% and the cadence template 99.94% of the time; tonality 75.64%; chord accuracy 64.11% against 62.10% in ground truth (i.e. real melodies only agree with their own inferred chord about 62% of the time). — [TeleMelody, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09617)
- SongMASS and the Transformer baseline appear only as comparison rows (English PD 34.32 / DD 47.88 / MD 2.61 for SongMASS); SongMASS used 8,000 English paired lyric-melody samples plus unpaired pre-training data. No rule is extracted from it. — [TeleMelody, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09617)

**LYRA, "Unsupervised Melody-to-Lyric Generation" (Tian et al., ACL 2023): the stress-duration rule**
- Rule: a note is labelled stressed (1) if its duration is longer than the average note duration, else unstressed (0). Syllable stress comes from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary. Beat position is not used. — [Tian et al., arXiv 2305.19228](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.19228v2)
- Scoring during decoding: p'(w) = p(w) if the candidate word's stress pattern matches the note pattern, α·p(w) otherwise; hard constraint α = 0, soft constraint 0 < α < 1, and the soft setting used α = 0.01. — [Tian et al., arXiv 2305.19228](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.19228v2)
- Measured share of lines on which the stress-duration rule holds: human ground truth 73.3%; SongMASS 58.8%; GPT-2 baselines 53.6% and 54.2%; LYRA with no rhythm control 63.1%, soft 68.4%, hard 89.5%. The authors note that humans do not follow the rule consistently, so higher is not automatically better. — [Tian et al., arXiv 2305.19228](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.19228v2)
- Data: 38,000 English song lyrics for training; test melodies from the Yu et al. aligned dataset; licences not stated. — [Tian et al., arXiv 2305.19228](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.19228v2)

**SongGLM (Yu et al., AAAI 2025): feature definitions worth reusing**
- Syllable stress: 0 unstressed, 1 primary, 2 secondary (CMU dictionary). Melodic peak: a note higher than both neighbours, excluding first and last notes. Rhythm skeleton: notes that are metrical accents, agogic (length) accents on metrical accents, or agogic accents on syncopations. Two relationships are asserted: stress–peak (stressed syllables tend to sit on melodic peaks) and stress–skeleton (stressed syllables tend to sit on rhythm-skeleton notes). — [SongGLM, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2412.18107)
- Joint lyric-melody n-grams for n = 2 to 12 are scored s = s_m + s_lm, where s_m is a t-statistic for the melodic n-gram and s_lm = C × (mean lyric-side t-statistic over the m lyric n-grams attached to that melodic n-gram), C = 1 if m = 1 and otherwise C = 1 − H′, H′ being the entropy of the lyric n-gram probabilities divided by log m. The top 25% are kept. Dataset: over 200,000 English lyric-melody pieces built from MelodyNet; licence not stated. The portion of the page read contained no measured alignment percentages. — [SongGLM, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2412.18107)

**CSL-L2M (2024, Chinese pop): attribute list and syllable-to-note ratio**
- Twelve sentence-level statistical controls: pitch mean, pitch variance, pitch range, direction of melodic motion, amount of arpeggiation, chromatic motion, duration mean, duration variance, duration range, prevalence of the most common note duration, note density, and syllable-to-note alignment ratio; each binned into 64 equal-population classes. Formulas are not in the paper. — [CSL-L2M, arXiv 2412.09887](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.09887)
- Dataset: 10,170 Chinese pop songs, all 4/4, with section labels verse / chorus / insertion / bridge / outro. More than 80% of syllables are set one note to one syllable; nearly 20% are one syllable to several notes. Most pitches lie between MIDI 50 and 80; most notes are eighths or sixteenths; most tempi 60 to 120 BPM. Licence not stated. No verse-versus-chorus numbers are given. — [CSL-L2M, arXiv 2412.09887](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.09887)

**Yu et al., Conditional LSTM-GAN (2019/2021) and its parallel dataset**
- 12,197 MIDI files (7,998 from LMD-full, 4,199 from a Reddit MIDI set), 789.34 hours. Each syllable is paired with a triple {MIDI pitch, note duration, rest duration}. Duration alphabet (in beats): 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 16, 32. Rest alphabet: 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. Training used 13,251 sequences of 20 notes (265,020 syllable-note pairs). Only one-syllable-to-one-note pairs are used; melisma is left to future work. Most pitches fall between MIDI 60 and 80; the quarter note is the commonest duration; rest = 0 is the commonest rest. No stress rule is reported. Licence not stated on the page (dataset said to be on GitHub). — [Yu et al., ar5iv 1908.05551](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1908.05551)

**"Songs Across Borders" (Ou et al., ACL 2023): singability as three hard constraints**
- Length: output syllable count must equal a length prompt len_i (i = 1 to 20). Rhyme: end word must fall in a requested rhyme class; text is generated in reverse so the rhyme word comes first. Word boundary: a per-syllable flag sequence, bdr_1 = a word boundary must follow this syllable, bdr_0 = don't care; the melody's boundary positions are musical pauses and the positions just before highlighted notes such as downbeats or high notes (so that a new word begins on the highlighted note). — [Songs Across Borders, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.16816)
- Achieved control: length accuracy 99.85%, rhyme accuracy 99.00%, boundary recall 95.52% (baseline 20.54 / 37.49 / 62.28). The paper gives no formula for deriving boundaries from note durations and says users need musical knowledge to write that prompt. Target language is Chinese, so no stress rule. One of its lyric corpora is MIT-licensed; others unstated. — [Songs Across Borders, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.16816)

### Inferences
- A rule engine can adopt ReLyMe's reward shape directly: score(candidate) = base + 1.5·R_rhythm + 1.0·R_structure (+ contour term), where R_rhythm counts strong/weak matches and correctly placed pauses. Published λ values of 1 to 1.5 against a log-probability give the relative scale: rhythm fit was weighted at or above structural repetition.
- TeleMelody's punctuation-to-cadence map plus its measured cadence note lengths give target bands: line-internal notes about 1.7 sixteenths long on average (so mostly eighths), comma-ending notes about 8.5 sixteenths (a half note), full-stop-ending notes about 10.3 sixteenths (a half note tied to an eighth, or longer), with the next onset roughly a sixteenth to an eighth later than the note's end.
- The 73.3% human figure from LYRA is a sensible target band for "stressed syllable on a longer-than-average note": aim near 70 to 75% of lines, not 100%; 89.5% is what a hard constraint produces and exceeds human practice.
- None of the papers read says how to handle verses whose syllable counts differ. The implicit devices they supply are: one-to-many syllable-to-note setting (about 20% of syllables in CSL-L2M), and ReLyMe's structure reward, which asks corresponding positions of parallel phrases to share contour rather than requiring identical note counts.
- Songs Across Borders' boundary rule transfers to English as: a word should begin on a downbeat or on a peak note, and a word should end before a rest or after a long note.

### Gaps
- Not read this session, so nothing is asserted from them: "Re-creation of Creations" (ROC), SongComposer, "Controllable lyrics-to-melody generation" (Zhang, Yu et al.), AI-Lyricist, DeepRapper, and SongMASS's own paper. From general knowledge only (unverified here): ROC retrieves and re-assembles existing melody segments indexed by chord and rhythm features rather than applying stress rules; SongMASS learns sentence- and token-level alignment by attention with no explicit rule; DeepRapper models rhyme and a beat token for rap, not pitched melody. These would need checking before use.
- ReLyMe's appendix (the exact strong/weak beat table and the tone-pair harmony table) was cut off on the page read; the muzic repository holds the definitive values. Its licence was not confirmed on the pages read.
- No system read reports "share of stressed syllables on strong beats in the training data". The nearest measured figures come from the corpus studies in section 2.
- No system read treats 3/4 or 6/8 explicitly; TeleMelody's rhythm token is positions 0 to 3 in a bar, and CSL-L2M's data is 4/4 only.

## 2. Measured relations between linguistic stress and musical features (beat position, duration, pitch height, peaks)

### Takeaway
Stress-to-metre alignment is strong but far from absolute: in pop lead sheets stressed syllables favour downbeats and half-bar positions, about 55% of primary-stress syllables fall on longer-than-local-average notes, melodic peaks are more likely on stressed syllables and content words, and in rock about one third of stressed syllables are syncopated (mostly anticipating the next strong beat). One small study that separates metres finds the fit is looser in 3/4 than in 4/4.

### Cited Findings

**Nichols, Morris, Basu and Raphael (ISMIR 2009)**
- Corpus: 679 popular lead sheets in MusicXML (229 private, 450 from Wikifonia); only 4/4 bars analysed. — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Base rates: P(primary stress) = 0.46, P(unstressed) = 0.48 (remainder secondary). Stressed syllables are strongly favoured on downbeat and half-bar positions; unstressed syllables are very unlikely on downbeats and very likely on eighth-note off-beat positions; chi-square p < 0.0001. Exact per-position values are in figures only. — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Duration: with relative duration split at 1 (short ≤ 1, long > 1), 55% of notes carrying unstressed syllables are short and 55% of notes carrying primary-stress syllables are long; p < 0.0001. — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Peaks: the probability that a note is a melodic peak rises with stress level (figure axis 0 to 0.2; exact values not in text); p < 0.0001. — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Function words: overall P(stopword) = 0.59; stopwords become less likely at stronger metric positions and at melodic peaks; p < 0.0001. Vowel type (short, long, diphthong) also correlates with note duration, mean relative durations spanning roughly 0.4 to 1.2 across vowel classes. — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)

**Keywords and strong beats, with a 3/4 versus 4/4 split (arXiv 2412.04202, 2024)**
- Corpus: 56 songs from piano books, 26 in 3/4 and 30 in 4/4; 2,713 words, 3,516 syllables (3/4: 1,123 words, 1,492 syllables; 4/4: 1,590 words, 2,024 syllables). Stress from CMU dictionary, primary only; keywords from YAKE. — [Keywords and Strong Beats](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04202v1)
- Overall: 80.8% of keywords fall on strong beats; 59.9% of stressed syllables fall on strong beats (40.1% on weak); 84% of unstressed syllables fall on weak beats; 91.3% of syllables on strong beats are stressed; keywords make up 66.4% of strong-beat words and 20.1% of weak-beat words. Approximate conditionals: P(strong | keyword) ≈ 0.8, P(downbeat | keyword) ≈ 0.7, P(strong | stressed) ≈ 0.6, P(downbeat | stressed) ≈ 0.5, P(stressed | strong) ≈ 0.9. — [Keywords and Strong Beats](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04202v1)
- By metre (accuracy / sensitivity / negative predictive value / combined score). Word level: 3/4 = 0.718 / 0.711 / 0.684 / 0.698; 4/4 = 0.749 / 0.855 / 0.787 / 0.820. Syllable level: 3/4 = 0.608 / 0.508 / 0.394 / 0.444; 4/4 = 0.688 / 0.657 / 0.452 / 0.536. So the lyric-to-beat match is weaker in 3/4 on every measure. — [Keywords and Strong Beats](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04202v1)
- Caveat: the paper's conclusion quotes different figures (76.8% of keywords on strong beats, 72% of non-keywords on weak beats, 83% of strong beats being keywords) from its results section; small sample, piano-book arrangements, and this corpus counts 73.3% of syllables as stressed, far above the 46% in Nichols et al. — [Keywords and Strong Beats](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04202v1)

**Tan, Lustig and Temperley, "Anticipatory Syncopation in Rock" (Music Perception 2019)**
- Corpus: 80 songs from the Rolling Stone corpus, 1955 to 1997, 23,129 notes; 17 non-4/4 songs and 3 rap songs were excluded. Syllable stress: primary 43.5%, secondary 1.8%, unstressed 50.3%, non-words 4.4%. Monosyllabic function words are 31% of word tokens. Tempo 58 to 234 BPM, mean 117.2, median 114. — [Tan, Lustig and Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Definition: a syncopation at the n-th-note level is a stressed syllable on a weak n-th-note beat with no syllable on or before the next n-th-note beat. Syncopation quotient SQ = syncopations / all stressed syllables. Anticipatory share ASQ = syncopations on the positions just before strong beats / all weak-beat syncopations. — [Tan, Lustig and Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Results: eighth-level SQ = 0.228 (2,382 of 10,433 stressed syllables); sixteenth-level SQ = 0.105; combined 0.333. ASQ = 0.772 (eighth), 0.740 (sixteenth), 0.762 combined. A comparison set of ten 19th-century English songs (1,397 notes, 602 stressed syllables) has SQ = 0 and only 18 stressed syllables on weak eighth-note beats. — [Tan, Lustig and Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Which beats get anticipated (eighth-note positions 1 to 8 in a 4/4 bar; "standard" = stressed syllable on the strong beat, "syncopation" = on the eighth before): beat 1 anticipated on position 8: 1,046 syncopations vs 1,028 standards (tendency 0.504); beat 3 (position 5) anticipated on position 4: 794 vs 639 (0.554); position 3 anticipated on 2: 305 vs 389 (0.439); position 7 anticipated on 6: 237 vs 438 (0.351). — [Tan, Lustig and Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- A typical anticipatory shift is about 250 ms. Eighth-level and sixteenth-level SQ are negatively correlated across songs (r = −0.52); tempo and year correlate r = −0.54. — [Tan, Lustig and Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)

**Palmer and Kelly (1992)**
- In composed song settings, lexical and phrasal stress predict metrical placement. Gilbert and Sullivan: compound nouns took the stronger metrical accent on the first syllable 43 times and on the second 5 times; adjective-noun phrases on the first word 16 times and the second 79 times (chi-square(1) = 66.6, p < .01). Art-song settings of English poetry: compounds 96 first vs 9 second; phrases 91 first vs 67 second (chi-square(1) = 33.5, p < .01). In composition, note duration and melodic contour did not distinguish the syllables; metrical position did. — [Palmer and Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- In performance (10 and 12 singers), syllables are sung longer when on a metrically accented note (F(1,9) = 23.5; F(1,11) = 13.4) and when linguistically accented (F(1,9) = 18.0; F(1,11) = 86.7), with no interaction: the two effects add. Determiner-led phrases put the adjective on the metrical accent 66 times to the noun's 25; clause-initial phrases put the noun there 31 times to the adjective's 25. — [Palmer and Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)

**Rap (Condit-Schultz, MCFlow, Empirical Musicology Review)**
- Corpus at publication: 124 songs, 47 artists, 374 verses, 5,803 bars, 62,466 syllables; described as freely shareable, no named licence. Typical delivery about 4.5 syllables per second; about 85% of durations in a verse alternate between two values in a 2:1 ratio; commonest phrases are about five syllables over two beats or ten over four beats; stressed syllables tend to fall on stronger beats and rhymed syllables even more so, clustering near beat four; about a quarter of stressed syllables are rhymed. Exact per-position percentages are not printed. — [Condit-Schultz, MCFlow](https://www.osu.tests.sfulib4.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/EMR/article/view/4961/4496)

### Inferences
- Target bands a rule engine can use for 4/4 pop: stressed syllables on a strong beat or its anticipation: about 60% directly on strong beats (keyword paper) plus up to a third displaced early by an eighth or sixteenth (rock SQ 0.333, of which about three quarters are anticipations). Unstressed syllables on weak positions: about 84%. Syllables landing on strong beats should be stressed about 90% of the time.
- Asymmetry worth encoding: "what sits on a strong beat should be stressed" (≈ 0.9) is a much tighter constraint than "every stressed syllable must reach a strong beat" (≈ 0.5 to 0.6).
- Content words outrank lexical stress: keywords reach strong beats about 80% of the time against 60% for stressed syllables generally, and function words (59% of sung tokens in Nichols) retreat from strong beats and from peaks.
- Duration is a weak cue (55/45), beat position a strong one; Palmer and Kelly's composition data agree (position distinguishes, duration and contour do not). A cost function should weight beat-strength mismatch well above duration mismatch.
- For 3/4 the only split data found suggests relaxing the alignment target by roughly 10 to 15 points relative to 4/4 (word-level sensitivity 0.711 vs 0.855), though the sample is 26 songs.
- Anticipation preference by position gives a usable rule: anticipating beat 3 and beat 1 by an eighth is as common as landing on them (tendencies 0.55 and 0.50); anticipating beats 2 and 4 is less common (0.44 and 0.35).

### Gaps
- Nichols et al. give the stress-by-position and peak probabilities only as bar charts; exact values would need reading from the figures.
- No study found quantifies "stressed syllables are higher in pitch" in semitones for pop; only the peak-probability relation (Nichols) and SongGLM's unquantified assertion.
- Temperley's own stress-by-metrical-position percentages for the Rolling Stone corpus (book chapter 4 material) were not accessible online. The 2021 rhythm paper (Arthur and Condit-Schultz line of work) annotated accents but did not use them.
- No stress-alignment study found for 6/8 or 12/8 popular song.

## 3. Verse-chorus register: how much higher, louder, denser or more repetitive is the chorus?

### Takeaway
I found no published corpus figure for the verse-to-chorus pitch lift in semitones. The quantitative studies that exist show the direction (a higher pitch centroid raises the odds a section is a chorus; chorus intervals are slightly larger) and show clearly that choruses have fewer, slower notes than verses, but the "chorus is N semitones higher" number is a gap in the literature reachable here; the claim rests on theorists' feature lists and case analyses.

### Cited Findings

**Van Balen, Burgoyne, Wiering and Veltkamp (ISMIR 2013), audio features on the Billboard dataset**
- Data: Billboard dataset v1.2, 649 songs, 7,762 sections (6,462 after cleaning); verse 34% and chorus 24% of annotated time. — [Van Balen et al. 2013](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000180.pdf)
- Regression of "chorusness" (a log-odds measure) on the features in its Markov blanket, coefficient [95% CI]: sharpness 0.11 [0.10, 0.13]; MFCC (timbre) variance 0.12 [0.09, 0.15]; roughness 0.12 [0.08, 0.16]; pitch salience (×10) 0.04 [0.03, 0.05]; loudness 0.03 [−0.01, 0.06]; loudness interquartile range −0.33 [−0.48, −0.18]; pitch centroid 0.10 [0.07, 0.12]. All p < 10⁻¹⁵ except loudness. So choruses are brighter, rougher, more timbrally varied, have a higher and more salient pitch centre, and a narrower dynamic range. — [Van Balen et al. 2013](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000180.pdf)
- The paper does not convert pitch centroid to semitones, does not report raw section means, and does not use the metre annotations. Classification from these features is modest: F = 0.52 against a 0.36 random baseline. — [Van Balen et al. 2013](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000180.pdf)

**BiMMuDa, Billboard Melodic Music Dataset (TISMIR 2024), symbolic melodies with section labels**
- Data: top five Billboard year-end singles for each year 1950 to 2022: 371 songs, 1,133 section-level melody MIDI files, 55,258 notes; licence CC BY 4.0. 380 verse melodies and 325 chorus melodies. — [BiMMuDa, TISMIR](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/10.5334/tismir.168)
- Verse vs chorus means (t-test p): length 24.69 s vs 22.28 s (< 0.01); note events 56.36 vs 46.90 (< 0.01); onset density 2.38 vs 2.15 notes per second (< 0.01); mean interval size 2.02 vs 2.15 semitones (0.011); tonality 0.75 vs 0.73 (0.061); pitch standard deviation 2.97 vs 2.96 semitones (0.90); rhythmic variability nPVI 39.68 vs 40.86 (0.41); melodic information content 3.48 vs 3.52 and rhythmic information content 2.21 vs 2.24 (not significant). — [BiMMuDa, TISMIR](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/10.5334/tismir.168)
- All-melody means: 22.68 s, 48.74 notes, 2.25 notes per second, mean interval 2.11 semitones, pitch standard deviation 2.99 semitones, tonality 0.73. The article does not report mean pitch, pitch range, or any time-signature breakdown. — [BiMMuDa, TISMIR](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/10.5334/tismir.168)

**Theory literature (direction and feature lists, no measurements)**
- Nobile (Intégral 2023) lists the usual chorus markers: more singers, hi-hat to ride cymbal, more guitar distortion, more loudness; cites Temperley 2018 (pp. 158 to 166), whose list of twelve chorus features is called the most detailed, plus Stephan-Robinson 2009 ("higher energy"), Everett 2009, de Clercq 2012, Covach 2010. In its case studies: "Penny Lane" chorus has "higher melodic pitch than the verses" and long held notes against the verse's staccato quarters and triplets; "Lovely Rita" verse is "speech-like, with several repeated notes and a small range" while the chorus has "a larger range and more varied contour", and is noted as an exception with no textural or energy lift. No numbers are given. — [Nobile, Intégral 36](https://theory.esm.rochester.edu/integral/36-2023/nobile/)
- The 2021 comparative rhythm paper cites Temperley 2007, Nobile 2015, Temperley 2018 and Arthur and Condit-Schultz 2021 for verses being denser, less aligned with the harmony and less rhythmically regular than choruses, without supplying figures of its own. — [Comparative analysis of melodic rhythm](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10356498)
- Heetderks's review of Temperley's book reports no verse-versus-chorus register numbers; it notes the book's tension-curve model allows a high-energy but low-tension chorus. — [Heetderks, MTO 29.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.23.29.1/mto.23.29.1.heetderks.html)

### Inferences
- Defensible rule from the evidence: the chorus should have a higher pitch centre than the verse, with fewer notes per second (about 10% fewer: 2.15 vs 2.38) and slightly larger steps (2.15 vs 2.02 semitones on average), while the spread of pitches stays the same (standard deviation about 3 semitones in both). The lift is therefore a shift of the whole band, not a widening.
- Because pitch spread is about 3 semitones within a section, a lift of the section mean by even 2 to 4 semitones would be on the order of one standard deviation and clearly audible; but that range is my estimate, not a measured statistic. BiMMuDa is CC BY 4.0 and section-labelled, so the true distribution of (chorus mean pitch − verse mean pitch) per song could be computed directly from it; that is the cleanest way to obtain the target band and its spread, and to split by time signature if metre can be read from the MIDI.
- "Lovely Rita" and Nobile's remarks show the lift is a tendency with real exceptions; a soft target with tolerance is more faithful than a hard rule.

### Gaps
- No source found gives the verse-to-chorus lift in semitones or scale degrees, its spread, or a split by triple metre or ballads. Temperley's book chapter on form (pp. 158 to 166) may contain a count from the Rolling Stone corpus, but the text was not accessible; Summach's dissertation and Nobile's "Form as Harmony in Rock Music" / voice-leading work were not reachable either.
- Van Balen's later catchiness work and Burgoyne's "Hooked on Music" were not retrieved this session; no numbers from them are reported.
- The CoCoPops / Arthur and Condit-Schultz 2021 verse-chorus melodic study was located only as a citation.
- No study found separates 3/4, 6/8 or ballads when comparing sections; the Billboard metre annotations exist but were unused in Van Balen et al.

## 4. Pitch concentration: pitch-class and pitch distributions, share of the commonest pitch, entropy

### Takeaway
The published pop-melody figures are rankings and entropies rather than "share of the commonest pitch": in the Rolling Stone corpus scale degree 1 is commonest and 5 second, with flat 7 commoner than 7; across song corpora generally, scale-degree entropy sits at about 1.7 to 2.8 bits with tonic and fifth commonest and longest. No source found states the tonic's percentage share in pop, nor how concentration differs between verses and choruses.

### Cited Findings
- Rolling Stone corpus (Temperley and de Clercq 2013): 200 songs, 194 with melody transcriptions. Commonest melodic scale degree is 1, then 5; flat 2 and sharp 4 are the rarest, flat 6 only slightly commoner; flat 7 is commoner than 7 (the reverse of classical practice). Exact proportions are in the paper's Figure 7 only. Key can be found from the melodic scale-degree distribution alone 76% of the time, 82% when notes are weighted by duration, 86% combined with harmony. Inter-transcriber agreement on melodies: F = 0.893 exact, 0.976 allowing one semitone and an eighth of a bar. Corpus is public at the Rochester site; licence not stated in the article. — [Temperley and de Clercq 2013](https://www.midside.com/publications/temperley_declercq_2013.pdf)
- Review of Temperley's book: in major-mode rock songs flat 7 outnumbers 7; minor-mode songs avoid flat 6 and favour the "pentatonic union" scale; the overall "supermode" runs from flat 6 to 7 on the line of fifths, leaving out flat 2 and sharp 4. — [Heetderks, MTO 29.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.23.29.1/mto.23.29.1.heetderks.html)
- Cross-cultural melody study (113 corpora, about 36,000 melodies; folk, art and children's song, not pop): scale-degree (chroma) entropy of folk melodies has a 95% range of about 1.7 to 2.8 bits; tonic and fifth are the commonest degrees and tend to have longer durations; pitch entropy exceeds rhythm entropy in about 78% of corpora; pitch sequences are about 51% repeated material and rhythm sequences about 71%; melody length after removing repetition spans about 13 to 45 notes; scalar-motion melodies with more than 8 scale degrees have under about 1% chance of entropy below 2.8 bits. — [Information and motor constraints shape melodic diversity](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.12635v2)
- Formulas from the same paper: entropy H = −Σ p_i log p_i; maximum H = log A for A distinct degrees; lower bound for a melody of L notes using A degrees, H_lower = (1 − A/L)·log(L/(L − A + 1)) + ((A − 1)/L)·log L. — [Information and motor constraints shape melodic diversity](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.12635v2)
- Pop pitch spread: section melodies in BiMMuDa have a pitch standard deviation of 2.99 semitones overall (verse 2.97, chorus 2.96) and a mean interval of 2.11 semitones. — [BiMMuDa, TISMIR](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/10.5334/tismir.168)
- Section character (qualitative): a verse can be "speech-like, with several repeated notes and a small range" against a chorus with larger range and more varied contour ("Lovely Rita"). — [Nobile, Intégral 36](https://theory.esm.rochester.edu/integral/36-2023/nobile/)
- A Hooktheory-based study (Hooktheory lead-sheet dataset plus a Chinese pop set) reports only plots: in its example the chorus has a flatter rhythm but more melodic movement than the verse; no numeric pitch-distribution or section statistics are printed. Paper is CC BY 3.0; dataset licence unstated. — [Exploring Inherent Properties of the Monophonic Melody of Songs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.09287)

### Inferences
- Converting the entropy band to concentration: 2.8 bits is the entropy of a uniform spread over 7 pitch classes (log2 7 = 2.81), and 1.7 bits is roughly a uniform spread over 3 to 4 (log2 3 = 1.58, log2 4 = 2). A melody whose commonest pitch class takes half of all notes, with the rest spread evenly over four others, has H = 0.5·1 + 4·0.125·3 = 2.0 bits, inside the band; one at 70% with three others at 10% has H ≈ 0.36 + 3·0.332 ≈ 1.36 bits, below it. So by this yardstick a commonest-pitch share above roughly 55 to 60% takes a full melody outside the typical folk-song range. This is arithmetic on a non-pop corpus, so treat it as a provisional ceiling, not a pop statistic.
- With pitch standard deviation near 3 semitones and a roughly bell-shaped spread, the central pitch would be expected to hold on the order of a quarter to a third of notes (duration-weighted somewhat more, since tonic and fifth are held longer); again an estimate, not a reported figure.
- Since duration weighting improves key-finding from 76% to 82%, concentration should be measured duration-weighted as well as by note count: important pitches are long as well as frequent.
- The equal pitch spread in verse and chorus (2.97 vs 2.96) argues against assuming verses are generically more "one-note" than choruses across the charts; recitation-style verses are a recognised type (Nobile) but not the average.
- BiMMuDa (CC BY 4.0) and the Rolling Stone corpus transcriptions would let the share of the commonest pitch, per section, be computed directly; that is the route to a real target band.

### Gaps
- No source found states the percentage share of the tonic, fifth, or commonest pitch in pop melodies, nor pitch-class entropy for a pop corpus in bits, nor how concentration varies with range. Figure 7 of Temperley and de Clercq holds the scale-degree proportions but was not machine-readable.
- No statistics found on "one-note" choruses or reciting verses as a category.
- "Is Melody Dead?" (large-scale Billboard melody analysis, 1960 to 2019) surfaced in search but was not read; Hooktheory-based scale-degree tables were not found.

## 5. Waltz-time and 6/8 pop and country ballads: rhythm templates and how the voice sits on the three beats

### Takeaway
Almost nothing quantitative exists on vocal rhythm in triple or compound-metre popular song: the main corpora drop non-4/4 songs before analysis. What can be cited is (a) stress alignment is measurably looser in 3/4 than 4/4 in one small study, and (b) de Clercq's absolute-time finding that a bar lasts about two seconds whatever the metre, which fixes tempo bands for 3/4 versus 6/8.

### Cited Findings
- Corpus studies exclude these metres: Nichols et al. analysed only 4/4 bars; Tan, Lustig and Temperley removed 17 of 100 Rolling Stone songs for not being in 4/4 or 2/4; the 2021 rhythm study dropped its one non-quadruple song; CSL-L2M's 10,170 songs are all 4/4. — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf); [Tan, Lustig and Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf); [Comparative analysis of melodic rhythm](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10356498); [CSL-L2M](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.09887)
- That exclusion count is itself a base rate: 17 of the 100 top-ranked Rolling Stone songs sampled (20 per decade, 1950s to 1990s) were not in 4/4 or 2/4. — [Tan, Lustig and Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- 3/4 versus 4/4 lyric alignment (26 vs 30 piano-book songs): word-level match score 0.698 in 3/4 against 0.820 in 4/4; syllable-level 0.444 against 0.536. — [Keywords and Strong Beats](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04202v1)
- de Clercq, "Measuring a Measure": the experienced bar in pop/rock tends to about 2 seconds. 4/4 songs average about 120 BPM (bar ≈ 2 s). 6/8 songs average about 60 BPM counted in dotted quarters, bars again ≈ 2 s, and no 6/8 song in the corpora exceeds 80 BPM; 12/8 averages 120 BPM in the Covach and Temperley/de Clercq corpora. The choice between competing readings is made by which bar length is nearest 2 seconds, overridden where form (e.g. 12-bar blues), harmonic rhythm or drum feel point elsewhere. Examples: Buck Owens, "Cryin' Time" (1964), 6/8 at about 76 BPM, bars ≈ 1.58 s (a 12/8 reading would give 3.15 s and is rejected); "Norwegian Wood" read in 6/8 gives bars of almost exactly 2 s; Travis Tritt, "Here's a Quarter" (1991), 9/8 at about 112 BPM, bars ≈ 1.61 s; Hank Williams, "Hey, Good Lookin'" (1951), 12/8 at about 140 BPM, bars ≈ 1.71 s; Led Zeppelin, "You Shook Me", about 55 BPM, kept in 12/8 for the blues form. Sample sizes for non-4/4 metres are small. — [de Clercq, MTO 22.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.declercq.php)
- de Clercq's metre classification for pop/rock uses six base signatures (2/4, 3/4, 4/4, 6/8, 9/8, 12/8, with 2/4, 9/8 and 12/8 deprecated) crossed with swing (straight, swung eighths, swung sixteenths) and drum feel (normal, half-time, double-time): 27 combinations, 22 viable. No corpus counts are given. — [de Clercq 2017 poster](https://www.midside.com/presentations/declercq_2017_pip_poster.pdf)
- One case description: "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds" has verses in a drumbeat-less compound metre and a chorus on a basic 4/4 rock beat; "Penny Lane" verses use lilting triplet rhythms against held notes in the chorus. — [Nobile, Intégral 36](https://theory.esm.rochester.edu/integral/36-2023/nobile/)

### Inferences
- Tempo bands from the two-second bar: a 6/8 ballad at 60 to 80 dotted-quarter BPM gives bars of 2.0 to 1.5 s with six eighth-note slots; a 3/4 waltz with a 2 s bar would be 90 quarter-note BPM, and fast waltzes heard "in one" pair up into two-bar units that behave like a 6/8 bar. The engine can therefore treat slow 6/8 as a two-beat bar (strong on eighths 1 and 4) and 3/4 as a one-strong-beat bar, with the lyric-line length in seconds held roughly constant.
- With only one strong beat in three (3/4) rather than two in four (4/4), fewer strong slots are available per stressed syllable; that is consistent with the lower alignment scores in 3/4 and suggests the engine should accept stressed syllables on beat 3 as a pick-up or on beat 2 with length (agogic accent) rather than penalise them at the 4/4 rate.
- Anticipatory syncopation figures (SQ 0.333) are from 4/4 rock only and should not be assumed for waltz-time ballads; the 19th-century English songs with SQ = 0 are a nearer model for unsyncopated triple-metre text setting until data says otherwise.

### Gaps
- No corpus study found of vocal rhythm templates, onset distributions, stress placement or syncopation in 3/4, 6/8 or 12/8 pop or country songs; no published analyses with numbers of well-known 3/4 or 6/8 pop songs were retrieved.
- No proportion of country songs in triple or compound metre was found (de Clercq's country corpus work surfaced only as a harmony study and was not read).
- TeleMelody's 0-to-3 beat tokens and ReLyMe's "predefined time signature" strength table would both need a new table for 3/4 and 6/8; neither paper supplies one.
