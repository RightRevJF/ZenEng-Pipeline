# Methods for building and validating a selector that picks the best few of ~1,000 generated candidates from ~150 to 750 positive examples

Scope note. The earlier report ("Judging generated melodies without listeners") already covers: classifier two-sample tests and why real-vs-generated AUC measures detectability; the paired conditional test (real song's own lyric/chords fed to the engine); covariate control; corruption-trained critics (Guan & Huang; ReaLchords); likelihood-ratio against a background model (Ren et al.); Fréchet-distance instability at n=100; expectation and memorability terms; originality guard. These notes do not repeat those. They cover what that report lacks: the statistics of selection itself (argmax bias, overoptimisation, how many candidates), positives-only conformity scoring and its small-sample behaviour, weak-label ranking and its sample-size limits, reranking evidence from other fields with numbers, metric meta-evaluation practice from machine translation, and equal-weight versus fitted combination.

## 1. Ranking from positives only: one-class, density-ratio, typicality vs likelihood, optimal distinctiveness, multivariate conformity

### Takeaway
The literature gives a direct reason the current judge is "spent" and mis-aimed: the most central point of a distribution is not a typical member of it, so "zero charges, nearest the medians" selects for a kind of object real songs almost never are; scoring should instead ask whether a candidate's overall distance from centre falls where real songs' distances fall (a two-sided typicality or conformal test on a joint score), and the one large-scale study of song typicality and chart outcome finds an inverted U, not "most typical wins". With n≈150 and 15 to 30 features, simple joint scores (shrunk-covariance Mahalanobis, Isolation Forest) are feasible, but positives-only methods can only certify "resembles the kept corpus"; they cannot rank quality among conforming candidates.

### Cited Findings
- Typicality vs likelihood (abstract only): deep generative models can assign higher likelihood to out-of-distribution data than to their own training data; the cause is "a mismatch between the model's typical set and its areas of high probability density"; in-distribution inputs lie in the typical set, not necessarily in high-density regions. The proposed remedy is a statistical test on whether a batch's likelihood falls in the range of the training data's likelihoods (two-sided), not a threshold on high likelihood. — [Nalisnick et al. 2019, arXiv 1906.02994](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02994v2)
- Stated limitation of that test: it "relies on the distribution of model likelihoods being well separated". — [Nalisnick et al. poster, NeurIPS BDL 2019](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~balaji/BDL-NeurIPS2019-typicality-poster.pdf)
- The mode is unrepresentative in sequence generation: in neural translation the most probable output "often does not represent any significant amount of probability mass"; beam-search (near-mode) outputs agree least with training-data statistics while unbiased samples agree more; beam outputs are systematically shorter than real data; for 41.5% to 87.3% of test-domain inputs all 1,000 samples were unique (flat distribution). The authors' recommendation is a decision rule that uses the whole sample (MBR), not the mode. — [Eikema & Aziz 2020, arXiv 2005.10283](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.10283)
- "Likelihood trap" with human ratings: crowd-rated quality of generated sentences rises with model log-likelihood up to an inflection point (log p = −58.09 in their setup) and then falls; highest-likelihood text was repetitive, lowest was nonsense. Evidence base: 146 crowdworkers, 100 sentences for the curve; main study ~10,000 samples, >38,000 paired ratings. Negative finding: their "selective sampling" fix (reject sequences above the likelihood cutoff) "consistently underperformed" other decoders despite removing <0.5% of mass, so a likelihood cap alone is not a sufficient selector. — [Zhang et al. 2021, arXiv 2004.10450](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.10450)
- Local typicality: Meister et al. define a string as locally typical if each token's surprisal stays within ε of the conditional entropy at that step, motivated by evidence that human text avoids both very predictable and very surprising continuations (per-token deviation from entropy clusters near zero in human text). Human ratings (1 to 5): stories, typical sampling 4.15 vs nucleus 4.13/4.09, reference 4.12 (standard errors ≈ ±0.02, so differences are small); summarisation, typical 4.32 vs nucleus 4.26, beam search 4.35. Stated limitations: hypothesis supported, not proven; no general relation between the typical set and the locally typical set; hyperparameter dependent. — [Meister et al. 2023, TACL / arXiv 2202.00666](https://arxiv.org/html/2202.00666v6)
- Optimal distinctiveness with a chart outcome: on ~25,100 Billboard Hot 100 songs (1958 to 2016), typicality = genre-weighted cosine similarity of 11 audio features to songs charting in the previous 52 weeks (mean .81, SD .06). Peak position (ordered logit): linear term +7.672, squared term −6.805 (SE 2.004, p<.01), an inverted U; songs in the middle of the typicality range are most likely to reach the top; both very typical and very atypical songs mostly stay outside the top 40. Weeks on chart (negative binomial): squared term −1.570 (p<.05); a song 1 SD below mean typicality stays about half a week longer (11.5 vs 11 weeks). Stated limits: 11 coarse audio features; the sample contains only songs that already charted (selection); mechanism untested. — [Askin & Mauskapf 2017, American Sociological Review](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/culture/2017-askin.pdf)
- Conformal p-values from positives only: fit any one-class score on part of the clean set, score the n held-out calibration positives, and give a new item p = (1 + number of calibration scores ≤ its score)/(n+1). Guarantee: marginally valid for exchangeable inliers with any score function and any dimension. Small-sample facts: the smallest attainable p-value is 1/(n+1); p-values for many test items share one calibration set and are positively dependent (PRDS), so Benjamini-Hochberg still controls false discovery rate but Fisher's combination is invalid without adjustment; the realised false-positive rate varies with calibration size, with coefficient of variation ≈ (n·α)^(−1/2): with 100 calibration points the false-positive rate at nominal 0.10 can exceed 0.15, with 1,600 points exceeding 0.12 is unlikely. — [Bates, Candès, Lei, Romano & Sesia 2023, Annals of Statistics / arXiv 2104.08279](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.08279)
- Which one-class detector at small scale: across 25,685 benchmark datasets built from 19 UCI source sets (8 algorithms), Isolation Forest had the best mean logit(AUC) (1.0893) and is recommended for general use; density methods (robust KDE, ensemble GMM) were best with no irrelevant features (rkde 1.4256) but fell sharply when irrelevant features were added (rkde 0.5477, egmm 0.4561 vs Isolation Forest 0.8117); one-class SVM and SVDD were weakest (SVDD below a trivial mean-distance baseline), probably through parameterisation difficulty; differences among the other algorithms "were not large". — [Emmott et al. 2015, arXiv 1503.01158](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.01158)
- Covariance estimation with few samples: the empirical covariance "is not a good estimator of the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix, so the precision matrix obtained from its inversion is not accurate"; shrinkage toward a scaled identity (Ledoit-Wolf 2004; OAS, Chen et al. 2010) fixes the eigenvalue spread; when n is of the order of p or smaller, sparse inverse-covariance estimators tend to beat shrinkage; the Minimum Covariance Determinant gives a robust location/scatter for Mahalanobis outlier screening. — [scikit-learn covariance documentation](https://scikit-learn.org/1.4/modules/covariance.html)
- Positive-unlabelled (PU) learning, the formal frame for "accepted human works vs an unlabelled candidate pool": under the SCAR assumption (labelled positives are a uniform random subset of all positives) Pr(labelled | x) = c·Pr(positive | x), so a classifier trained labelled-vs-unlabelled preserves the true ranking without knowing c (Elkan & Noto result). Limits: SCAR "often doesn't hold"; the class prior is not identifiable from PU data alone; accuracy, precision and F1 cannot be computed from PU data, only recall under SCAR; several methods become unreliable with few labelled examples. — [Bekker & Davis 2020 survey, arXiv 1811.04820](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.04820)

### Inferences
- The quartile-charge judge is a product-of-marginals, mode-seeking rule. Researcher's arithmetic: if p readings were independent, a genuine corpus song would have every reading inside the middle half with probability 0.5^p, i.e. about 3 in 100,000 at p=15, 1 in a million at p=20, 1 in a billion at p=30; the average real song would be "charged" on half its readings. A candidate with zero charges is therefore unlike essentially every real song. (Positive correlation among readings raises these probabilities but not to anything near "typical".) This is the typical-set argument in the engine's own units, and it predicts both observed symptoms: survivors pile up at zero (spent) and the tie-break "nearest the medians" then pushes further toward the mode.
- Researcher's arithmetic under a Gaussian model: squared Mahalanobis distance of a real item is chi-square with p degrees of freedom, mean p, SD √(2p). At p=20 real items sit at distance about 3.3 to 5.6 from the centre (5th to 95th percentile), and the probability of a real item having squared distance below p/4 is about 0.0003. A typicality-style judge would charge a candidate for a joint distance that is too small as well as too large.
- Researcher's simulation (Gaussian, equicorrelated features, 200 replications; not a literature result): (a) candidates with exactly the right marginals but no correlation among features are invisible to quartile charges (AUC 0.51 to 0.56 at p=20 to 30) and visible to Mahalanobis distance (AUC 0.73 at correlation 0.3, 0.96 at 0.6, p=20, n=150). (b) A 1 SD shift on 3 of 20 features: quartile-charge AUC 0.54 to 0.56, Mahalanobis 0.64 to 0.72. (c) Sample size matters little for ranking at these dimensions: AUCs at n=150 are within 0.01 to 0.02 of those at n=750. (d) It matters for calibration: with n=150 and p=20 held-out real items' squared distance is inflated by about 18% with the raw covariance and 11% with Ledoit-Wolf (28% and 17 to 20% at p=30), against 3% at n=750; so thresholds must come from held-out or cross-fitted positives (the conformal recipe), never from the chi-square table or the training items' own distances.
- Consequence for power at n≈150: conformal p-values bottom out at 1/151 ≈ 0.0066 (1/751 ≈ 0.0013 at n=750), and part of the corpus must be held out for calibration. A positives-only conformity test can therefore reject clearly foreign candidates and can form a "conforming set", but it has no resolution to order the top few dozen of 1,000 conforming candidates. It is a gate, not a ranker. This matches the earlier report's "typical, never good".
- Askin & Mauskapf's inverted U suggests a concrete, checkable selection criterion to replace "nearest the medians": target the middle of the real-song distance distribution, or slightly on the atypical side, and test on the builders' own outcome corpora whether the outcome signal is quadratic in conformity. The evidence is audio-feature typicality on charting songs, so transfer to symbolic melody features is an assumption.
- The Emmott result argues for Isolation Forest as a second, differently-built joint conformity score next to Mahalanobis: it degrades least when many readings are irrelevant, which is the likely condition for a hand-built list of 15 to 30 readings. One-class SVM is the poorest choice at this scale.
- PU framing clarifies what the existing real-vs-generated classifier estimates. If "unlabelled" is the candidate pool, its score ranks by Pr(real | x) only under SCAR, which fails here because the kept corpus is not a random draw from "good songs the engine could write". This is a second, independent reason (beyond the earlier report's detectability argument) not to read that classifier's score as quality.

### Gaps
- No source was found that reports power of one-class or density-ratio methods specifically at n≈150 and p≈15 to 30 on music or comparable creative-artefact data; the small-sample statements above rest on general theory, the Bates et al. calibration result and the researcher's simulation.
- Density-ratio estimators (KLIEP, uLSIF, Sugiyama and co-authors), copula-based conformity (vine or Gaussian copula with empirical margins), Hotelling T² small-sample distribution, and Lee et al. 2018 Mahalanobis out-of-distribution scoring were not re-read in this round; they are known methods but no numbers are given here. A Gaussian copula with rank-transformed margins is the natural robust variant of Mahalanobis for skewed readings; that is the researcher's suggestion, not a cited finding.
- The classical "novelty-typicality" literature beyond Askin & Mauskapf (Uzzi et al. 2013 on atypical combinations in science; Berger & Packard 2018 on lyric atypicality and chart success) was not fetched here; the sibling notes on creativity science and cultural evolution may hold it.
- Nalisnick et al.'s batch-size results (the test is weak for a single item and strong for batches) could not be read; only the abstract and poster were accessible. This matters because the judge scores single candidates.

## 2. Learning to rank with weak graded labels: sample size, multiple outcome signals, overfitting control

### Takeaway
With a few hundred labelled songs the binding constraint is the number of parameters estimated, not the choice of ranking loss: prediction-modelling guidance implies only a handful of freely fitted weights are supportable at n≈150 to 750, validation that tunes anything outside the cross-validation loop produces above-chance results on pure noise, and reported accuracy in small-sample studies falls as sample size rises. Count and survival outcomes (weeks on chart, variant counts) have a worked precedent in the chart-typicality study, and permutation tests give a listener-free check on whether any learned ranking has found real structure.

### Cited Findings
- Sample size for fitting a predictive model: Riley et al. recommend the largest n meeting several criteria, chiefly a global shrinkage factor ≥ 0.9 and optimism ≤ 0.05 in apparent R². Worked examples: a continuous outcome with 20 candidate parameters and high R² (0.90) needs ≥ 254 subjects (≈12.7 per parameter; the rule "234 + P" comes from estimating the residual SD within 10%); a time-to-event outcome with 30 parameters and low R² (Cox-Snell 0.051) needs 5,143 participants and 692 events (23.1 events per parameter), 3,429 with 20 parameters; a binary outcome with 30 parameters, 5% prevalence and R² 0.05 needs 5,249. The "10 events per variable" rule is called too simplistic: required events per parameter depend on the signal strength and range from <10 to 50. Estimating just an overall proportion of 0.5 to ±0.05 needs 385. — [Riley et al. 2020, BMJ](https://keele-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/464599)
- Dawes's older rule of thumb agrees in order of magnitude: fitted regression weights need roughly 15 to 20 observations per predictor before they beat unit weights on cross-validation. — [Dawes 1979, American Psychologist](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/dawes/the-robust-beauty-of-improper-linear-models-in-decision-making.pdf)
- Validation bias at small n: on pure Gaussian noise (true accuracy 50%), K-fold cross-validation with feature selection or tuning done on the pooled data gave accuracies well above chance, largest at small N and still detectable at N=1,000; nested cross-validation and a clean train/test split were indistinguishable from chance at about 96.5% of sample-size points. Feature selection on pooled data was the largest contributor (SVM and logistic regression above chance at every sample size), more than hyperparameter tuning; bias grew with the feature-to-sample ratio and the number of candidate features. In a survey of 55 studies, sample size and reported accuracy correlated r = −0.70 (R² = 0.49). Recommendation: nest everything, report intervals. Caveat: simulations on synthetic data. — [Vabalas et al. 2019, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6837442/)
- Over-fitting the selection criterion: any model-selection criterion evaluated on a finite sample has variance, and tuning against it over-fits; the resulting bias is "often of comparable magnitude to differences in performance between learning algorithms"; the argument applies to any criterion optimised over a finite sample, not only cross-validation. — [Cawley & Talbot 2010, JMLR](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v11/cawley10a.html)
- Permutation tests for a learned scorer: (1) permute the labels and refit to get the null distribution of the performance statistic, testing whether any real label-feature structure was found; (2) permute features within class, which preserves each class's marginals and destroys dependence among features, testing whether the model uses joint structure. — [Ojala & Garriga 2010, JMLR](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v11/ojala10a.html)
- Worked precedent for graded outcome models on songs: Askin & Mauskapf model peak position with ordered logit, weeks on chart with a zero-truncated negative binomial, and week-to-week chart movement with song fixed effects, on ~25,000 songs; effects are statistically clear but small in size (half a week per SD of typicality). — [Askin & Mauskapf 2017](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/culture/2017-askin.pdf)
- Amount of comparison data a learned reward model needs before it carries signal: in a controlled study of learned reward models for text, below roughly 2,000 pairwise comparisons the reward model showed "very little improvement over near-chance" validation loss, and this carried through to post-selection quality; larger models did not reach the threshold earlier. (Neural reward models on text; the threshold is not transferable as a number, but the existence of a floor is.) — [Gao, Schulman & Hilton 2023, ICML / arXiv 2210.10760](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.10760)
- Signal ceiling for hit labels from intrinsic features (from the earlier report, restated for the sample-size argument): ~150 melody features separated 122 hits from 144 non-hits at about 52% under honest cross-validation. — [Müllensiefen et al.](http://www.doc.gold.ac.uk/~mas03dm/papers/HitSongScience_Weimar.pdf)

### Inferences
- Applying Riley's shrinkage logic to the builders' case: outcome signals from notes are weak (the project's own earlier finding is 1% to 25% of variance), and weak signal is exactly the regime where the required n per parameter explodes (the low-R² examples need ~170 subjects per parameter). With 150 to 750 songs and weak signal, a freely fitted model with 15 to 30 weights will have a shrinkage factor far below 0.9, meaning its fitted ranking is mostly noise. Defensible designs are: (a) no fitted weights (unit weights with pre-set signs, see section 6); (b) one to three fitted parameters (for example a single quadratic in a joint conformity score, as in Askin & Mauskapf); (c) heavily penalised models whose penalty is chosen inside nested cross-validation.
- Pairwise transformation helps count, not information: 150 songs give ~11,000 pairs but only 150 independent units; pairs sharing a song are dependent, so cross-validation must split by song (and by tune family, composer or hymnal lineage where near-duplicates exist) or it leaks. The Gao et al. floor of ~2,000 comparisons was for independent human comparisons and should not be read as "150 songs give enough pairs".
- For the outcome types named: weeks on chart and variant counts are over-dispersed counts or durations with censoring (songs still charting, hymnals not yet published); negative binomial or Cox-type models with one or two predictors, or simply rank correlation (Kendall's tau or a concordance index) between a fixed score and the outcome, are the forms the sample supports. A concordance index with a permutation p-value is a complete, listener-free test of "does this pre-specified score order the outcome better than chance".
- Two-channel (audience vs musician) modelling at this n is best done as replication, not as a jointly fitted multi-task model: fix the score on one corpus, then test sign and rank correlation on the others. Agreement of sign across chart hits, covered songs, surviving hymn tunes and many-variant folk tunes is a stronger, cheaper result than any multi-task fit, and disagreement of sign is itself the finding that the channels differ.
- Pre-registration, in this setting, means writing down before looking: the score definition, the direction of each reading, the outcome, the test statistic, and the split. Cawley & Talbot's argument implies that every time the builders revise the judge after seeing a validation number, that number stops being a validation; a small untouched "vault" corpus per outcome signal, consulted rarely and logged, is the practical defence.
- Ojala & Garriga's second test maps directly to the engine: if a critic's performance does not drop when features are permuted within class, it uses only marginals, and the quartile judge already has those.

### Gaps
- No paper was found giving sample-size requirements specifically for pairwise or listwise learning-to-rank with n in the hundreds; the constraint is inferred from prediction-model guidance.
- Ordinal regression, RankNet/LambdaMART/RankSVM, random survival forests, and multi-task learning with heterogeneous outcome signals were not individually reviewed; no small-n evidence for or against them was retrieved.
- Varma & Simon (2006) on nested cross-validation bias and the literature on pre-registration in machine learning were not fetched; Vabalas and Cawley & Talbot carry the argument here.
- No study was found that fits one model to audience and musician outcomes jointly for songs and reports whether a shared representation helps.

## 3. Selection under a learned critic: Goodhart, optimiser's curse over 1,000 candidates, and remedies

### Takeaway
Taking the top-scoring 1 of 1,000 under a noisy score guarantees the winner's score overstates its quality, and when candidates are close in true quality the pick is close to random; controlled experiments show true quality first rises and then falls as selection pressure on an imperfect critic increases, and that the critic's own score keeps rising throughout. The remedies with quantitative support are ensembles of differently built critics (averaging ranks, or taking the worst case), shrinkage of scores toward the pool mean, selecting a top set instead of a single argmax, and judging the selected outputs with a critic that was not used for selection.

### Cited Findings
- Optimiser's curse, base case: with all alternatives equal in true value and independent, unbiased, normal estimation errors of SD 1, the expected disappointment (estimated minus true value of the chosen alternative) is 0.85 SD for 3 alternatives, 1.03 for 4 and 1.54 for 10 (values for 2 to 10: 0.56, 0.85, 1.03, 1.16, 1.27, 1.35, 1.43, 1.48, 1.54; intermediate values were read from a figure and should be checked against the original). — [Smith & Winkler 2006, Management Science 52(3)](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2006-smith.pdf)
- When true values differ the curse shrinks: 3 alternatives separated by Δ (in error-SD units) give disappointment 0.85 at Δ=0, 0.66 at 0.2, 0.51 at 0.4, 0.00 at 3.0, with probability of choosing correctly rising from 0.33 to 0.98. — [Smith & Winkler 2006](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2006-smith.pdf)
- Correlation: positive correlation among the estimation errors reduces disappointment (4 alternatives: 0.73 at error correlation 0, 0.41 at 0.5, 0.09 at 0.9), while correlation among true values increases it (0.73 to 0.98 as true-value correlation goes 0 to 0.9). — [Smith & Winkler 2006](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2006-smith.pdf)
- Real-portfolio illustration: with independent errors of SD 5%, 10% and 25% of value, claimed value added was overstated by 14%, 39% and 128%; with 10% error and pairwise correlation 0.5, by 24%. — [Smith & Winkler 2006](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2006-smith.pdf)
- The remedy is Bayesian shrinkage before ranking: revised estimate = α·score + (1−α)·prior mean, with α = 1/(1 + error variance / prior variance of true values); the hierarchical version shrinks toward the observed pool mean. With shrunk estimates the expected disappointment is zero (their Proposition 2). Shrinkage changes the ranking only when items have different error variances. — [Smith & Winkler 2006](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2006-smith.pdf)
- Overoptimisation measured directly with best-of-n selection: using a learned reward model as proxy and a much larger "gold" model as truth, gold score as a function of d = √KL follows R(d) = d(α − β·d), i.e. rises then falls, where for best-of-n KL = log n − (n−1)/n. Fitted on n up to 1,000 and confirmed as an advance prediction out to n = 60,000. Larger reward models have smaller β (more robust); the proxy's own score has much lower β, i.e. it keeps rising while gold falls. Ensembles were not studied in this paper. — [Gao, Schulman & Hilton 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.10760)
- Ensembles of critics as the fix (abstract only): repeating that setup with 25% label noise, conservative selection with an ensemble of reward models (worst-case optimisation, or mean penalised by ensemble disagreement) improved best-of-n performance by up to 70% and "practically eliminates overoptimization" for best-of-n. — [Coste, Anwar, Kirk & Krueger 2024, ICLR](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/dda7f9378a210c25e470e19304cce85d-Abstract-Conference.html)
- Goodhart in reranking with human raters: reranking 200 translation candidates by a reference-free quality estimator (COMET-QE) raised the related automatic metric in every setting (COMET 0.5795 to 0.6012 EN→DE; 0.5361 to 0.6207 EN→RU) while human adequacy fell (4.28 to 4.19; 3.62 to 3.25), MQM fell (95.66 to 93.47; 79.78 to 76.25), major errors rose from 139 to 204, and BLEU dropped from 36.01 to 29.83. The authors call this a "metric gap" and attribute it to over-fitting the metric. A reranker with tuned weights over several features plus MBR did improve human scores (EN→RU MQM 79.78 to 86.78). — [Fernandes et al. 2022, NAACL / arXiv 2205.00978](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00978)
- Self-preference of the selecting metric: outputs chosen by MBR or quality-estimate reranking with one neural metric score far better on that metric (MetricX 0.656 vs 1.58 for greedy; lower is better) with no matching gain from human raters; several single-metric systems were rated significantly worse than greedy decoding. MBR with an ensemble of utility metrics scored 1.27 and 1.28 MQM error against 1.52 for greedy (p<0.001). Recommendations: ensemble the utilities; never evaluate selected outputs with the same or a similar metric. — [Kovacs et al. 2024, WMT / arXiv 2411.03524](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2411.03524)
- Selection exposes a critic's blind spots: MBR over 100 samples with COMET as utility often picked translations with wrong numbers or names even when most samples were right; number-match F1 was ~95.8 for beam search but 82.4 and 77.1 for MBR with one COMET version; manual number-error rates were 4.4% (BLEU utility), 4.6% (chrF++), 7.2% and 16.6% (two COMET versions). Retraining the metric on ~61,000 synthetic number/name perturbations improved sensitivity but did not close the gap, and correlation with human judgement stayed within 0.006, so the standard meta-evaluation statistic did not reveal the defect or its repair. Recommendation: use a metric as a selection utility during its development specifically to find its biases. — [Amrhein & Sennrich 2022, AACL / arXiv 2202.05148](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.05148)
- "Verifier hacking" in images: searching over diffusion noise with a single verifier improves that verifier's score while degrading others (selecting by CLIPScore: CLIP 0.71 to 0.82, Aesthetic 5.79 to 5.68; selecting by Aesthetic: 5.79 to 6.38, CLIP 0.71 to 0.69); classifier-type verifiers caused mode collapse as compute grew. An ensemble that averages the ranks (not raw scores) of three verifiers improved every metric and gave the best independent grader score (88.18 vs 84.29 baseline). Caveats: the independent grader is an LLM, not people; on a compositional benchmark the single ImageReward verifier beat the ensemble because one ensemble member (Aesthetic) was harmful for that task. — [Ma et al. 2025, arXiv 2501.09732](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.09732v1)
- Imperfect verifiers cap the benefit of resampling: when the verifier (unit tests) passes some wrong solutions, resampling until a pass cannot lift a weaker generator to a stronger one's accuracy; false-positive rate correlates strongly with single-sample accuracy; with a cost for false positives the optimal number of attempts is small (K ≤ 5 at cost-benefit ratio 4; "can be less than 10 under realistic assumptions"); false positives were also worse on secondary quality measures than true positives. — [Stroebl, Kapoor & Narayanan 2024, arXiv 2411.17501](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17501v1)
- Quantilisers: instead of the argmax, choose at random from the top q fraction of a trusted base distribution ranked by the score; the expected cost from errors in the score is at most 1/q times the base distribution's (10 times for the top 10%, 100 times for the top 1%). Stated limits: depends heavily on the base distribution; a theoretical proposal without empirical quality results. — [Taylor 2015/2016, MIRI](https://intelligence.org/2015/11/29/new-paper-quantilizers/)

### Inferences
- Researcher's computation, extending Smith & Winkler's base case to the engine's pool size (exact integral for the expected maximum of N independent standard normals): 0.85 at N=3 and 1.54 at N=10 (matching the paper), 2.04 at N=30, 2.51 at N=100, 2.88 at N=300, **3.24 at N=1,000**, 3.85 at N=10,000. If the 1,000 candidates were equal in true quality, the winner's score would sit 3.24 noise-SDs above its true value and the pick would be uniformly random.

  | 0.20 | 1.44 | 3.25 | 5.81 | 16.7% | 1.08 |
  | 0.06 | 0.79 | 3.25 | 12.5 | 5.6% | 0.59 |

  Three readings. First, even a weak critic helps on average (reliability 0.2 still delivers +1.4 SD), so a blunt judge is better than none provided its errors are unbiased noise. Second, the winner's own score is useless as evidence of how good it is. Third, the gap to the best available candidate is large at realistic reliabilities, which bounds what any amount of extra generation can buy (section 5).
- The simulation assumes unbiased noise. The cited experiments (Gao; Fernandes; Kovacs; Amrhein; Ma) show the realistic case is worse: a critic's errors are systematic, the argmax searches for them, and true quality can fall. The practical signature to monitor without listeners is divergence: as selection pressure rises (best of 10, 100, 1,000), the selecting critic's score rises while held-out critics' scores flatten or fall. Plotting held-out critic score of the winner against log N is the engine-sized version of Gao et al.'s curve and requires no human ratings.
- Smith & Winkler's correlation table implies a design rule for ensembles: critics whose errors are correlated with each other do not reduce the curse when averaged; the gain comes from critics built on different information (different features, different corpora, different model families). The four outcome corpora (charts, covers, hymnals, folk variants) are a natural source of independently trained critics.
- Rank-averaging across critics (Ma et al.) avoids the scale problem that one critic with a wide score range dominates a sum; worst-case-over-critics (Coste et al.) is the conservative variant: a candidate is only as good as its weakest critic's opinion. Both are implementable with no labels.
- The current two-stage judge (zero charges, then nearest medians) is an argmax on a single critic applied at maximum pressure; replacing the final step with "random or diversity-spread choice among the top q% that every critic accepts" is the quantiliser idea, and it costs nothing if the critics cannot genuinely order the top set anyway (which the reliability table suggests).
- Shrinkage matters most when candidates' scores have unequal noise: short melodies, or readings computed from few notes, have noisier scores and will be over-represented among extreme values. Shrinking each candidate's score toward the pool mean in proportion to its noise variance (Smith & Winkler's α) or selecting on a lower confidence bound (score minus k times its standard error, estimable by bootstrap over bars or phrases) removes that artefact.

### Gaps
- Smith & Winkler report nothing beyond 10 alternatives; the N=1,000 figures are the researcher's computation under the paper's assumptions.
- Coste et al. was read as abstract only; the size of gains per ensemble type and the number of ensemble members were not verified.
- Thrun & Schwartz 1993 and van Hasselt 2010 (maximisation bias, double estimators), Andrews, Kitagawa & McCloskey 2024 ("Inference on winners"), Bailey & López de Prado (deflated Sharpe ratio for multiple trials), Manheim & Garrabrant 2018 (Goodhart taxonomy) and Skalse et al. 2022 (reward hacking definition) are relevant known work that was not re-read; no numbers from them are used.
- No study was found that measures the optimiser's curse or overoptimisation in symbolic music generate-then-rank systems.
- Lower-confidence-bound selection has support from the ensemble/conservative-optimisation literature above, but no source was read that tests bootstrap-based lower bounds for reranking generated artefacts.

## 4. Validating a judge without listeners

### Takeaway
Machine-translation metric research has a mature kit for testing a judge without new human ratings: controlled perturbation suites with a known order, minimal pairs, synthetic-degradation training, and using the metric as a selection utility to see what it picks; its central documented lesson is that a judge can keep a good average correlation with human judgement while having blind spots that selection will find, and that a judge's rating of its own selections is biased upward. For this engine the listener-free tests that follow are: held-out real works ranked among candidates with conformal calibration, graded corruptions of real melodies with a monotone-response requirement, permutation and noise baselines, and held-out critics.

### Cited Findings
- Perturbation suites with known order: DEMETR contains ~31,000 examples over 35 perturbation types (semantic, syntactic, morphological), each a minimal pair differing from the original in one respect; a metric passes if it prefers the original. Findings: learned metrics beat string metrics overall but with uneven sensitivity, e.g. one metric is sensitive to untranslated words but relatively insensitive to gender changes; another is far more sensitive to word repetition than to aspect changes (abstract-level detail). — [Karpinska et al. 2022, EMNLP / arXiv 2210.13746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13746)
- Synthetic degradations as training signal and robustness evidence: BLEURT pre-trains on ~6.5 million synthetic pairs made by perturbing 1.8 million sentences (mask-filling, back-translation, random word dropping in ~30% of examples). Pre-training improved agreement with human ratings by up to 7.4 Kendall-tau points on one benchmark year and up to 2.1 on later years; under training-set skew, the non-pre-trained metric fell below simple baselines at mild skew while the pre-trained one held until the most extreme skew; competitive with as few as 836 rated records in a new domain. Limits: agreement with humans falls for all metrics as test distribution shifts; two of the pre-training signals hurt in ablation. — [Sellam, Das & Parikh 2020, ACL / arXiv 2004.04696](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.04696)
- Selection as a probe (adversarial use of the judge): running MBR with the metric as utility and inspecting what it chooses revealed number and named-entity blindness that the metric's correlation with human scores had not shown; a targeted sensitivity analysis (alter numbers, names, or random nouns in the reference and measure the score drop) quantified it: average drop −0.037 for numbers, −0.068 for names, −0.232 for nouns. — [Amrhein & Sennrich 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.05148)
- Pitfall, average correlation hides defects: after retraining to fix the blind spot, correlation with human judgement changed by at most 0.006, so the headline meta-evaluation number neither detected the defect nor the repair. — [Amrhein & Sennrich 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.05148)
- Pitfall, a judge over-rates its own selections: neural metrics "overestimate the quality of MBR decoding when the same metric is used as the utility metric"; evaluation of selected outputs must use a different metric family or people. — [Kovacs et al. 2024](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2411.03524)
- Pitfall, metric gain with human loss: see the COMET-QE reranking result in section 3 (metric up, human adequacy and MQM down). — [Fernandes et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00978)
- Held-out positives ranked among candidates, with a guarantee: for a held-out real item exchangeable with the calibration positives, its conformal p-value is uniform; so across many held-out real works the judge's percentile for the real work must be uniform on (0,1) if the judge's notion of conformity is calibrated, and departures are testable. Dependence caveat: p-values sharing a calibration set are positively dependent; Fisher's combination is invalid without correction, Benjamini-Hochberg remains valid. — [Bates et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.08279)
- Noise and permutation baselines: a validation pipeline should return chance on label-permuted or pure-noise data; pipelines with any selection or tuning outside the loop do not. — [Vabalas et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6837442/); [Ojala & Garriga 2010](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v11/ojala10a.html)
- Music-specific precedents already in the earlier report (not re-researched): rank agreement of scorers against human-derived chorale judgements, where hand-built categorical features reached 0.899/0.881 and generic summary features were at chance ([Ens & Pasquier 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.06226)); likelihood-type scores assigning better values to corrupted music, correlation below −0.85 between injected-noise length and loss change ([Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02738)).

### Inferences
- A listener-free validation battery, assembled from the cited practice, in order of how much each can prove:
  1. **Sanity floor.** Run the entire judge-building and judge-testing pipeline on label-permuted outcomes and on feature-permuted corpora. It must return chance. (Vabalas; Ojala & Garriga.)
  2. **Graded corruption, monotone response.** Take held-out real melodies; apply each named corruption at increasing dose (e.g. 5%, 10%, 20%, 40% of notes pitch-shifted; phrases reordered; rhythm jittered; repetition scrambled; chords mis-paired). Requirements: the judge prefers original to corrupted in a high share of minimal pairs per corruption type, and mean score is monotone in dose (test with a rank correlation or isotonic fit per melody). Report per corruption type, DEMETR-style, never as one pooled number, because the documented failure pattern is uneven sensitivity. The current quartile judge can be run through this today as a baseline.
  3. **Sensitivity ratios.** Following Amrhein & Sennrich, compare the score drop per unit dose across corruption types; a judge that drops ten times less for one type has a blind spot there, and argmax over 1,000 will exploit it.
  4. **Real-among-generated rank test.** For each held-out real song, generate the pool for its lyric and chords, score with a judge that never saw that song (leave-one-song-out, and leave-one-tune-family-out), and record the real melody's percentile. A calibrated conformity judge puts real melodies uniformly; a quality judge should put them high. The distribution of percentiles is the statistic (Kolmogorov-Smirnov against uniform for calibration; a sign or Wilcoxon test against the median for "real ranks above typical candidate"). With 150 songs a one-sided sign test has good power to detect a real-song median percentile of about 60 or more; this is a researcher's estimate from binomial arithmetic, not a cited figure.
  5. **Selection probe.** Let the judge pick the top few from many pools, then tabulate how the picks differ from real songs on every reading and on critics the judge did not use. Systematic drift of picks (shorter, flatter, more repetitive, narrower range) is the engine's equivalent of the number/name errors and identifies the blind spot directly.
  6. **Held-out critic.** Keep at least one critic (a different feature family or a model trained on a different corpus) out of selection permanently, and report winners' scores on it as pool size grows. Never report the selecting judge's score for its own winners as evidence (Kovacs et al.).
  7. **Outcome replication.** Fix the judge, then compute rank correlation between judge score and each outcome signal on corpora not used in building it, with permutation p-values and pre-registered signs (section 2).
- What each test can and cannot establish. Tests 1 to 3 show the judge responds to known damage; they cannot show it orders undamaged candidates by quality. Test 4 shows real songs are distinguishable or not from the pool by the judge; a pass can be produced by detectability alone (the earlier report's warning), so it must be read with covariates matched and alongside test 2. Test 7 is the only one that touches "good", and its signal ceiling is low. No combination of these replaces a listening test for the claim "the top pick is great"; they can establish "the judge is not broken, not over-fitted, not exploiting a known blind spot, and weakly aligned with independent outcome signals".
- A specific pitfall when using corruptions both to train a critic and to validate it: the validation corruptions must be of types (or at least doses and random seeds, preferably types) not used in training, otherwise the test only shows memorisation of the corruption recipe. BLEURT's result that robustness under distribution shift is where synthetic pre-training pays off, and Amrhein & Sennrich's result that synthetic retraining did not fully remove a blind spot, both argue for holding out whole corruption families.
- Calibration in the conformal sense is checkable without labels and is worth reporting on its own: if held-out real songs do not get uniform p-values, the kept corpus is not exchangeable across the split (period, genre or source effects), and every downstream threshold is suspect.

### Gaps
- The WMT metrics shared-task meta-evaluation protocol (system-level and segment-level correlation, pairwise accuracy, tie handling), COMET's own paper, the ACES challenge set, CheckList (Ribeiro et al. 2020) and the metamorphic-testing literature were not fetched; the practice is represented here by DEMETR, BLEURT, Amrhein & Sennrich and Kovacs et al.
- DEMETR was read at abstract level only; per-perturbation accuracy figures are not reported here.
- No music-generation paper was found in this round that reports a graded-corruption monotonicity test or a perturbation suite for a symbolic-music quality judge with numbers; the earlier report's sources (corruption-trained critics, mis-pairing rewards) are the nearest. This is an absence in what was searched, not a verified absence in the literature (ISMIR proceedings were not searched directly).
- No source was found quantifying the statistical power of the "real original among generated alternatives" rank test at n≈150; the power statement above is the researcher's estimate.

## 5. Reranking in generation: does it improve human-judged quality, by how much, and how many candidates are worth generating

### Takeaway
Reranking reliably helps when the selector is close to a true verifier or an ensemble of utilities, gives modest human-judged gains otherwise (a few tenths of a point on 5-point scales; around 15% fewer weighted errors in the best translation results), and can make outputs worse under a single learned critic; across text, code and images the benefit grows roughly with the logarithm of the number of candidates and, without a real verifier, selection quality stops improving after roughly 30 to 100 candidates even though the pool keeps getting better.

### Cited Findings
- MBR with a neural utility, human evaluation (abstract only): choosing among unbiased samples by expected BLEURT utility gave "significant gains in human evaluations" over beam search; the chosen translations have much lower model likelihood and lower BLEU, and model probability correlates only weakly with human-judged quality. — [Freitag et al. 2022, TACL](https://aclanthology.org/2022.tacl-1.47)
- Size of human-judged gains from reranking in translation: 5-point adequacy, baseline vs best reranker: 3.68 to 3.90 (IWSLT EN→DE), 3.92 to 4.09 (EN→FR), 3.62 to 3.72 (WMT EN→RU), 4.28 to 4.33 (WMT EN→DE, not significant); MQM EN→RU 79.78 to 86.78, EN→DE 95.66 to 96.20 with major errors 139 to 109. Gains were larger for the weaker (small-data) base models. Candidate counts: up to 200 for reranking, 50 to 100 for MBR. Single-critic reranking (COMET-QE alone) lowered human scores. — [Fernandes et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00978)
- Ensemble utility vs single utility, human MQM error (lower is better): greedy 1.52; ensemble MBR 1.27 and 1.28 (about 16% fewer weighted errors, p<0.001); several single-metric MBR systems significantly worse than greedy on some language pairs. — [Kovacs et al. 2024](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2411.03524)
- Consensus selection with small pools: MBR with only 30 samples (candidates double as pseudo-references) and a simple overlap utility matched or beat beam search (34.0 vs 32.6 on low-resource pairs); an oracle needed only 5 to 10 samples to beat beam search on easier settings and 15 to 25 on the hardest. — [Eikema & Aziz 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.10283)
- Self-consistency (majority vote over sampled reasoning paths), accuracy gains over greedy: +17.9 (GSM8K), +12.5 (AQuA), +7.6 to +7.9 (SVAMP, ASDiv), +6.3 (StrategyQA), +1 to +5 on easier sets. Diminishing returns: on one model AQuA accuracy at 1/5/10/20/40 paths was 19.7/24.9/25.3/26.7/26.9, most of the gain by 5 to 10 paths; the authors state performance "saturates quickly". — [Wang et al. 2022, arXiv 2203.11171](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.11171)
- Code, large pools with a partial verifier: AlphaCode's solve rate with 10 submissions rose 21.0% → 26.2% → 31.8% → 34.2% as samples went 1,000 → 10,000 → 100,000 → 1,000,000 (validation), "approximately log-linearly ... bending down slightly at high sample budgets"; filtering on example tests removed ~99% of samples, and for ~10% of problems no sample passed; clustering survivors by behaviour and submitting one per cluster added 16.9% → 21.0% at 1,000 samples. False-positive rates of test suites were 30% to 62% before the authors strengthened them (4% after). — [Li et al. 2022, AlphaCode, arXiv 2203.07814](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.07814)
- Coverage vs selection: the share of problems for which at least one sample is correct keeps growing over four orders of magnitude of samples (e.g. 15.9% → 56% from 1 to 250 samples on SWE-bench Lite; 79.8% → 95.3% from 100 to 10,000 on MATH), following an exponentiated power law. Without a true verifier, majority voting and reward-model selection improved only from 38.7% to 39.8% between 100 and 10,000 samples on MATH while coverage passed 95%: selection plateaus near 100 samples. Conclusion: "building robust verifiers remains an open problem". — [Brown et al. 2024, "Large Language Monkeys", arXiv 2407.21787](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21787v2)
- Imperfect verifier economics: optimal number of resampling attempts under a cost for false positives is small (K ≤ 5 at cost-benefit ratio 4). — [Stroebl et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17501v1)
- Images: DALL-E draws N = 512 samples per caption and keeps the top k by a contrastive image-text model; FID and IS improve clearly with N up to 32 "after which returns diminish"; human raters preferred its (reranked) samples over a prior system 93.3% of the time for caption match and 90.0% for realism (this compares systems, not reranked vs un-reranked). — [Ramesh et al. 2021, arXiv 2102.12092](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.12092)
- Images, search with verifiers: gains continue with compute but are verifier-specific (section 3); a rank-averaged ensemble verifier raised an independent grader score from 84.29 to 88.18; a smaller model with search at about a tenth of the compute outperformed a larger model without search on the reported metrics. — [Ma et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.09732v1)
- Reranking vs scoring inside the search (from the earlier report, for completeness): soft rewards applied during search gave +0.31 against +0.05 for reranking finished outputs. — cited in the earlier report at `/home/claude/reports/Judging generated melodies without listeners.md` (original source not re-fetched here)

### Inferences
- The cross-domain pattern is consistent enough to state as a design expectation: pool quality (the best candidate present) grows about linearly in log N for a long way; realised selection quality tracks it only as far as the selector's reliability allows, then flattens (Brown et al.: by ~100; DALL-E: by ~32; self-consistency: by 5 to 40). The researcher's selection simulation in section 3 gives the same shape from first principles: at reliability 0.2 the argmax of 1,000 achieves +1.4 SD against +3.2 available.
- For this engine: 1,000 candidates per lyric is already past the point where a blunt single judge can use the extra candidates. More candidates will not help until the judge improves; conversely, any improvement in judge reliability is immediately worth more than a tenfold increase in pool size. A cheap empirical check with no listeners: sub-sample pools of 10, 30, 100, 300, 1,000 and plot the held-out critic's score of the winner; the knee of that curve is the number of candidates currently worth generating.
- The largest reranking gains in the record come from (a) true verifiers (code tests), (b) weak base generators, and (c) ensembles of utilities. The engine has no true verifier, so (c) is the available lever, plus hard constraints that act as partial verifiers (singability, range, prosody alignment: pass/fail rules with low false-positive rates behave like AlphaCode's example tests and can remove most of the pool cheaply).
- Consensus selection (MBR) is available with no critic at all: define a similarity between two melodies for the same lyric (e.g. on contour, rhythm and chord-tone alignment), and pick the candidate with the highest average similarity to the rest of the pool. The translation evidence says this beats mode-seeking with as few as 30 samples. Its known cost transfers too: it selects the pool's centre of mass, which is safe and unsurprising; given the inverted-U finding in section 1, it is better used as one ensemble member or as an outlier filter than as the sole selector. AlphaCode's cluster-then-pick-one-per-cluster step is the complementary device for returning a few picks that are different from each other.
- Expected effect size should be set modestly: where human raters were used, good reranking moved 5-point ratings by 0.1 to 0.2 and weighted error counts by about 15%. Nothing in this evidence supports expecting reranking alone to lift output from "plausible" to "great"; this agrees with the earlier report's conclusion that the generator, not the judge, holds most of the gain.

### Gaps
- Freitag et al. 2022's MQM numbers, candidate-list size and its candidate-count ablation could not be read (PDF not accessible); only the abstract's qualitative claims are used. Freitag et al. 2023 on epsilon sampling and candidate-count curves for MBR was not fetched.
- No controlled human evaluation of reranked vs un-reranked output for images was read (Karthik et al. 2023 on selecting among candidates with an automatic scorer could not be accessed); DALL-E's human result compares systems.
- No study of candidate-count curves or reranking gains with human listeners was found for symbolic music or melody generation in this round.
- CodeT, MBR-Exec and Coder-Reviewer reranking for code were not fetched.

## 6. Combining many weak readings: equal weights vs fitted weights, composite vs ensemble

### Takeaway
At the builders' sample sizes the evidence favours unit-weighted sums of standardised readings with signs fixed in advance over fitted weights: equal weights matched or beat cross-validated regression in classic small-sample comparisons, fitted weights need about 15 to 20 cases per predictor before they pull ahead, and when the outcome is weak or absent the expert's job is choosing readings and their directions. For selection specifically, combining critics by averaged ranks or by worst case has direct empirical support, whereas a single learned composite is the configuration in which metric-versus-human divergence was observed.

### Cited Findings
- Dawes's comparison (correlation of prediction with criterion): equal weights vs cross-validated regression: .60 vs .57 (student GPA, 90 students, two judge panels), .48 vs .38 (later faculty ratings, 111 students), .34 vs .46 (neurosis vs psychosis, 861 patients, the large-sample case where regression wins); random weights with correct signs reached .30 to .51 and still beat the human judges (.19 to .37). — [Dawes 1979](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/dawes/the-robust-beauty-of-improper-linear-models-in-decision-making.pdf)
- Conditions Dawes states: standardise predictors first; orient each so it relates positively to the criterion, with signs assigned a priori; relationships should be conditionally monotone; predictor intercorrelations should not be negative; regression weights need roughly 15 to 20 observations per predictor to beat unit weights on cross-validation; "the whole trick is to know what variables to look at and then know how to add". Where no measurable criterion exists, proper models cannot be built at all and unit weights on expert-chosen variables are the fallback. — [Dawes 1979](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/dawes/the-robust-beauty-of-improper-linear-models-in-decision-making.pdf)
- Modern replication: across nine published U.S. election forecasting models (10 elections, 27 variables in total), equal-weight versions had lower error than the regression originals for six of nine, about 5% lower on average; an equal-weight model using all 27 variables missed by 1.3 points on average, 48% lower error than the typical regression model and 29% lower than the best one. — [Graefe 2015, Journal of Business Research](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v68y2015i8p1792-1799.html)
- Theory (abstract only): whether fixed weights beat least squares in mean squared error is determined by sample size, error variance and model predictability; closed-form bounds exist for the share of true coefficient vectors for which fixed weights win. — [Davis-Stober 2011, Psychometrika](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychometrika/article/abs/geometric-analysis-of-when-fixed-weighting-schemes-will-outperform-ordinary-least-squares/EEB9950C5691ED3F7244D6B51605092C)
- Fitted combination can work with enough tuning data: a reranker with tuned weights over several features was the one configuration that improved human scores consistently in translation, while fixed reranking on a single learned metric hurt. — [Fernandes et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00978)
- Rank-averaged ensemble of critics: averaging ranks, to avoid scale mismatch among raw scores, improved all component metrics and an independent grader; but including a critic that is harmful for the task lowered the ensemble below the best single critic on that task. — [Ma et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.09732v1)
- Ensemble of utilities beat any single utility with human raters (1.27/1.28 vs 1.52 MQM error for greedy; single-metric systems sometimes worse than greedy). — [Kovacs et al. 2024](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2411.03524)
- Conservative ensembles (worst-case or disagreement-penalised) nearly eliminated overoptimisation for best-of-n (abstract only). — [Coste et al. 2024](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/dda7f9378a210c25e470e19304cce85d-Abstract-Conference.html)
- Shrinkage is the bridge between the two extremes: scores should be pulled toward the pool mean by α = 1/(1 + noise variance / signal variance) before ranking. — [Smith & Winkler 2006](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2006-smith.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 15 to 20 cases-per-predictor rule, and Riley's stricter numbers for weak signals (section 2), put the builders' corpora (150 to 750) with 15 to 30 readings at or below the threshold where fitted weights pay, and well below it for weak outcomes. Unit weights with pre-registered signs are the default; ridge-type shrinkage toward equal weights (not toward zero) is the principled intermediate when some fitting is wanted, and whether it beats unit weights can be decided inside nested cross-validation on each outcome corpus.
- Dawes's conditions expose a mismatch with the current judge: unit weighting assumes each reading is monotone in quality once oriented. The quartile charges are two-sided ("too high and too low are both bad"), which is a conformity statement, not a monotone quality statement. The two should be separated: a conformity gate built jointly (section 1), and then a small unit-weighted sum of readings for which a direction is known from independent evidence (the memorability and expectation terms of the earlier report). Mixing them in one composite is what leaves most survivors tied at zero.
- A sign that cannot be pre-specified from prior evidence is a reason to leave the reading out of the ranking sum, not to fit its weight: Dawes's random-weights result shows that correct signs carry most of the value, and the hit-prediction ceiling shows that small corpora cannot be trusted to supply the signs.
- Redundancy matters for unit weights: twenty readings that are mostly restatements of note density give density twenty votes. Grouping readings into a few families (rhythm, contour, harmony fit, repetition, prosody), averaging within family and then equal-weighting families is the standard repair, and it also yields the "independent critics" that sections 3 and 5 call for.
- Composite vs ensemble for selection: a single composite maximised by argmax is the configuration in which every cited failure occurred. The supported alternative is several separately built critics combined by rank average or by minimum, with one critic withheld for evaluation. The Ma et al. counter-example (a harmful member drags the ensemble down) means each member should first pass the corruption battery of section 4 before it earns a vote.

### Gaps
- Davis-Stober's quantitative thresholds (the n, R² and predictor-count regions where equal weights dominate) are behind a paywall and were not read; Einhorn & Hogarth 1975 and Wainer 1976 were not fetched.
- No stacking-specific small-sample evidence (stacked generalisation vs simple averaging at n in the hundreds) was retrieved; the forecast-combination literature on why simple averages beat estimated combination weights is relevant and was not read.
- No study was found comparing equal-weight and fitted composites for music-quality scoring.
