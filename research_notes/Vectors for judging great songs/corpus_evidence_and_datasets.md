# Corpus evidence and open datasets for ranking within good music

Scope note. Two earlier reports ("Cultural evolution for song survival", "Measuring musical greatness from notes") already cover Salganik MusicLab, Pachet & Roy 2008, Janssen 2017 phrase survival, Savage 2022, Hymnary rival tunes, Berger & Packard 2018 (sample and design), Candia 2019 decay curves, Ortega 2021 headline numbers, and first-pass descriptions of BiMMuDa, CoCoPops, Hooktheory, POP909, OpenEWLD, MetaMIDI, PDMX, Chordonomicon, SHS100K. These notes concentrate on what those did not have: effect sizes for graded-success studies, critics-versus-charts and musicians-versus-audience evidence, and which datasets actually carry a GRADED outcome on the same songs that have notes. Items marked [COMPUTED] are counts I produced this session by downloading the public files and running a script; they are not published figures. Items marked [EARLIER NOTES] are carried over from the earlier research notes with their original URL.

Legend used throughout: SYM = symbolic features (notes/chords); AUD = audio-derived descriptors (Echo Nest/Spotify/Essentia); LYR = lyric text. HIT-vs-NOT = charting against non-charting; GRADED = differences among songs that all charted.

## 1. Graded-success studies: what predicts peak, weeks on chart, or later popularity

### Takeaway
Only one family of studies uses symbolic features with a graded chart outcome (Miles et al. on McGill Billboard chords), and its effects are small (d about 0.28 to 0.42, one contrast at 0.96) and limited to zeroth-order chord rarity; every other graded study uses audio descriptors or lyrics, and the recurring result is an inverted U around the contemporaneous norm with optimum slightly below or near the mean, worth about half a chart week per standard deviation. Audio features alone reach Spearman rho about 0.42 to 0.47 against peak or weeks, and about 0.60 balanced accuracy on median splits; the one test of chord progressions against Spotify popularity found no effect once artist popularity was in the model.

### Cited Findings

Symbolic, graded within charting songs
- Miles, Rosen & Grzywacz 2017 (Frontiers in Human Neuroscience). SYM, GRADED. McGill Billboard, 732 songs 1958 to 1991, 545 analysed (major key, no modulation, transposed to C; 596 unique chord labels). Surprise = zeroth-order information content of each chord (corpus frequency, no context). Songs split by peak Hot 100 position: Q1 (top) n=151, Q4 (bottom) n=150. Mean song surprise Q1 5.31 bits vs Q4 4.84 bits, t=2.48, p<0.01, Cohen's d=0.289. — [Miles et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00263/full)
- Same paper, section contrast: SD of section-mean surprise Q1 1.07 vs Q4 0.89 bits (d=0.280). Verses carry the effect (Q1 5.26 vs Q4 4.60 bits, d=0.325); choruses and bridges show no quartile difference, and removing verses removes the song-level effect. Verse-to-chorus absolute change Q1 1.70 vs Q4 1.14 bits (d=0.423); where the verse is more surprising than the chorus, Q1 2.15 vs Q4 0.95 bits (d=0.960). Top-quartile songs drop in surprise going into the chorus. — [Miles et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00263/full)
- NULL inside the same paper: first- and second-order (transition) surprise showed no significant quartile differences in informal tests; the section-SD effect was not significant in a three-genre ANOVA; genre-controlled song-level effect is partial eta squared 0.027 (rock, R&B, country) and 0.016 (rock plus R&B). — [Miles et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00263/full)
- Miles et al. 2021 follow-up (Frontiers in Human Neuroscience) is another corpus study, not a listener experiment. McGill (136 top vs 136 bottom) plus 6,051 Hot 100 songs 2000 to 2019 with neural-network chord estimates (24 chord types only). Surprise rises over time in both quartiles and faster in the top quartile (McGill +0.096 vs +0.032 bits per year). No Q1 vs Q4 difference in the 1958 to 1975 bin; Q1 5.46 vs Q4 4.78 bits in later bins (p<0.01). 28.7% of newly introduced chords first appeared in Q1 songs vs 19.8% in Q4. No standardised effect sizes; authors are employees of the company whose proprietary algorithms were used. — [Miles et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8121146)
- A US patent, "Predicting the popularity of a song based on harmonic surprise" (US 11,048,747), exists on this method, which is a commercial interest to weigh when reading the two papers. — [Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11048747)
- Campagna 2017 (Univ. Pisa thesis). SYM chords plus AUD, GRADED (Spotify popularity 0 to 100). About 900 songs with Hooktheory chord progressions joined to Spotify. R² about 0.50 to 0.55, driven by artist popularity; chord progressions "do not meaningfully change predicted popularity"; I–V–vi–IV appears across all popularity deciles. Three-class accuracy about 60%. No naive baseline reported. — [Campagna thesis](https://iris.cnr.it/retrieve/fbf0c0f5-7fee-4112-817e-b8fed5ecf53d/prod_425777-doc_153760.pdf)
- Wang 2014 (Stanford CS229 project, not peer reviewed). SYM (MIDI n-grams of pitch, instrument, inter-onset), HIT-vs-NOT with the middle removed (positive = ever top 10; negative = artist never in top 100), 1,752 MIDIs of undocumented source. Precision 0.56 to 0.63 at a 0.5 cut-off; headline 0.882 precision is at a cut-off tuned on the evaluation data with recall 0.279; top coefficients showed "no clear pattern". — [Wang 2014](https://cs229.stanford.edu/proj2014/Kedao%20Wang,%20Predicting%20Hit%20Songs%20with%20MIDI%20Musical%20Features.pdf)

Audio, graded within charting songs
- Askin & Mauskapf 2017 (American Sociological Review). AUD (11 Echo Nest features), GRADED. 26,846 Hot 100 songs Aug 1958 to Mar 2016, 25,102 with features. Typicality = genre-weighted cosine similarity to songs charting in the prior 52 weeks (mean 0.81, SD 0.06, range 0.26 to 0.92). Inverted U for both peak (ordered logit) and weeks (negative binomial). A song one SD below mean typicality stays about half a week longer (11.5 vs 11 weeks); in weekly fixed-effects models one SD more typical costs more than 0.6 positions per week. Peak and weeks correlate about 0.72. Positive with peak: danceability, liveness, 4/4; negative: energy, speechiness, acousticness. The average number 1 was less typical than other charting songs, with the gap narrowing recently. — [Askin & Mauskapf 2017](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/25509/Mauskapf_productfeatures.pdf)
- Controls from the same paper [EARLIER NOTES]: major label +.145 on peak; ten or more prior hits +.087 on peak but −.168 on weeks. — [Askin & Mauskapf 2017, alternate PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/culture/2017-askin.pdf)
- O'Toole & Horvát 2023 (EPJ Data Science). AUD (13 Echo Nest features) and LYR (Doc2Vec), GRADED (total weeks on Hot 100). 14,248 songs 1974 to 2013. Novelty = z-scored distance from the genre-year mean. Inverted U in both; optimum novelty for being in the top 15%, 10%, 5% by weeks is −0.35, −0.33, −0.27 SD (audio) and −0.14, −0.17, −0.18 SD (lyrics), that is, slightly MORE typical than the genre-year average. Audio and lyric novelty are uncorrelated (r = −0.01) and their penalties add. Weeks on chart did not correlate with later "influence". Kernel-density results only, no regression coefficients; several reported statistics look odd (p = 1.0 ANOVAs). — [O'Toole & Horvát](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07754)
- Conflict to note: Askin & Mauskapf put the optimum on the less-typical side of the mean; O'Toole & Horvát put it slightly on the more-typical side. Different reference sets (prior-year chart vs same genre-year) and outcomes (peak and weeks vs top-percentile weeks). — [Askin & Mauskapf 2017](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/25509/Mauskapf_productfeatures.pdf); [O'Toole & Horvát](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07754)
- Lee & Lee 2018 (IEEE Trans. Multimedia). AUD, GRADED. 16,686 Hot 100 songs 1970 to 2014 for chart metrics; 1,264 songs 2009 to 2014 for prediction. Median-split balanced accuracy with an SVM: Max (peak) 0.60 and Mean rank 0.59 to 0.61 are the most predictable; Length (weeks) 0.51 to 0.53, Debut 0.52 to 0.57 and Kurtosis about 0.50 are near chance. Adding debut position lifts peak accuracy by 9.9 points. Chroma-based complexity features were the strongest single group. — [Lee & Lee 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1812.00551)
- Vötter et al. 2021 (IEEE ISM), HSP datasets. AUD (Essentia via AcousticBrainz). HSP-S: 7,736 songs, half charting, with Hot 100 peak, weeks, Last.fm listeners and plays. Best Spearman rho: peak 0.468, weeks 0.420, Last.fm listeners 0.34 to 0.37, plays 0.36 to 0.37. Chart vs non-chart accuracy 0.715. The peak and weeks correlations are computed on a set that mixes charting and non-charting songs, so they are not purely within-chart. — [Vötter et al. 2021](https://dbis-web1.uibk.ac.at/sites/default/files/2022-06/ISM_2021__Hit_Song_Prediction.pdf)
- Parmer & Ahn 2019 (ISMIR). AUD codewords (pitch, loudness, timbre, rhythm conditional entropy), HIT-vs-POPULATION. 905,896 MSD songs vs 6,661 Hot 100 songs, 1960 to 2010. Hot 100 songs have lower pitch and timbre complexity, higher loudness complexity, and smaller variance in pitch, loudness and rhythm complexity than random samples. No analysis by chart position, no effect sizes; the inverted U is inferred from narrower variance, not fitted. — [Parmer & Ahn 2019](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2019/paper/000019.pdf)
- Interiano et al. 2018 (Royal Society Open Science). AUD, HIT-vs-NOT. More than 500,000 UK releases 1985 to 2015; successful songs were "happier", more "party-like", less "relaxed", more often female-voiced; prediction 75% to 85%, the 85% only with a "superstar" (prior success) variable. — [UCI news release](https://news.uci.edu/2018/05/15/cmon-get-happy-upbeat-songs-by-female-singers-dominate-the-charts-uci-study-finds)
- Mauch et al. 2015 [EARLIER NOTES]: 17,094 Hot 100 songs 1960 to 2010, audio-derived harmonic and timbral topics; a trend study with revolutions near 1964, 1983, 1991 and no within-chart success model. — [Mauch et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4453253)

Instrumentation, lyrics and repetition
- Nunes & Ordanini 2014 (Musicae Scientiae). Instrumentation coded by ear, EXTREME GROUPS (number 1 vs never above 90). Popular configurations always include background vocals and unpopular ones always lack them; an atypically low or high number of instrument types raises the odds of number 1; direction varies by decade (more instruments helped mid-1970s to 1990s, fewer in the 1960s and late 2000s). The record page gives no coefficients. — [Bocconi IRIS record](https://iris.unibocconi.it/handle/11565/3913118)
- Nunes, Ordanini & Valsesia 2015 (J. Consumer Psychology). LYR/form, EXTREME GROUPS. 1,029 number 1s vs 1,451 songs that never rose above 90, 1958 to 2012. Each extra chorus repetition (coded 1 to 16) raises the odds of number 1 over bottom-of-chart by 14.5%; more repetition speeds the climb; excessive single-word repetition slows it. — [Pacific Standard summary](https://psmag.com/?p=30843)
- Varnum et al. 2021 (PLoS ONE). LYR, GRADED. 14,661 Billboard songs 1958 to 2016; lyrically simpler songs reached higher chart positions, more strongly in years with more new songs entering the charts. The abstract gives no effect size. — [Varnum et al. 2021 record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0244576.html)
- Percino, Klimek & Thurner 2014 (PLoS ONE). Instrument credits (Discogs, more than 500,000 albums), STYLE-LEVEL not song-level. Change in a style's instrumentational complexity vs its average sales measure S(s) (built from Amazon SalesRank): rho = −0.69 (p = 0.001), read by the authors as "simplicity sells" (styles with high sales lose complexity); change in complexity vs change in album count rho = 0.54 (p given as 0.014 in the text and 0.0014 in a caption). The sign convention of S(s) was not visible in the part of the page retrieved, so the direction rests on the authors' own reading. Restricted to styles with at least 1,500 albums, album-count correlations are not significant (for example rho = 0.26, p = 0.28). — [Percino et al. 2014](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1405.5057)

Artist-level and long-run outcomes
- Berg 2022 (Administrative Science Quarterly). AUD (11 Spotify features), ARTIST-level. About 3 million songs by about 69,000 artists on hit-producing labels 1959 to 2010; about 25,000 Hot 100 songs; about 4,900 artists with at least one hit; 93% of artists never charted. Low-novelty portfolios were about twice as likely to produce a first hit; artists whose portfolio at first hit was more novel or varied went on to more hits. Novelty = distance from the previous year's hits. — [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/music-one-hit-wonder-pop-songs-lasting-career); [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/what-separates-hitmakers-one-hit-wonders)
- Long-run streaming is weakly tied to original chart rank, on descriptive evidence only. Among 1,176 Hot 100 number 1 hits, Spotify streams in Dec 2024 span more than three orders of magnitude inside a single decade (1960s: 1.36 billion for "Paint It Black" vs 0.36 million for "Wooden Heart"; 1980s: 2.36 billion vs 1.39 million). — [Mush from the Wimp](https://mushfromthewimp.substack.com/p/the-most-and-least-streamed-1-hits)
- The Pudding 2017: among all 1990s Hot 100 songs, the most-played on Spotify peaked at 6 ("Smells Like Teen Spirit", 50.7 million plays in the 2014 snapshot, yearly rank 42 of 385), then 9, 2, 2, 1; "At Last" peaked at 68 in 1961. No correlation reported. — [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/2017/03/timeless/)
- Chartmetric: of songs on Spotify's "All Out" decade playlists (sampled 2016 to 2023), nearly 70% had reached the Hot 100, at least 60% the top 40 and almost half the top 10; in nearly every decade at least half of number 1 hits still appear. No musical features analysed. — [Chartmetric](https://hmc.chartmetric.com/music-legacy-factors-spotify-popularity-playlists/)

### Inferences
- The only replicated note-level separator of top from bottom of the chart is chord rarity in verses followed by a drop into a plainer chorus. It is zeroth-order, so a judge can compute it directly from McGill/CoCoPops chords; the context-sensitive version (transition surprise) was null, which argues against assuming an n-gram or IDyOM-style chord model will rank better than raw rarity for this outcome.
- Peak and weeks are different targets. Peak is the more predictable from content (Lee & Lee); weeks behaves like a typicality optimum (Askin, O'Toole); artist history pushes them in opposite directions (prior hits raise peak and shorten runs). A judge should not merge them into one label.
- Extreme-group designs (Nunes, Wang, Miles's quartiles) inflate apparent separability relative to a continuous ranking; the continuous rank correlations (rho 0.42 to 0.47 with full audio features) are the realistic ceiling, and symbolic-only numbers will be lower.
- Every "optimal differentiation" result is defined relative to a contemporaneous reference set. A feature of the form |x − contemporaneous median| or a signed, slightly-off-centre optimum is what the literature supports; no study supports a monotone "more novel is better" or "more complex is better" term within charting songs.
- No study was found that predicts decades-later streams, covers or canon membership from symbolic features while controlling for original peak and weeks. That regression is unrun, and the data to run it now exist (section 5).

### Gaps
- Frieler, Jakubowski & Müllensiefen 2015, "Is it the song and not the singer? Hit song prediction using structural features of melodies" (Yearbook of the German Society for Music Psychology 24, 41–54) is the most directly relevant symbolic study and could not be read: the repository record has no full text. From memory only, and unverified: a few hundred pop melodies, classification barely above chance. The record is at [Goldsmiths](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/17197).
- Askin & Mauskapf's coefficient tables (Tables 3, 4, A5) were beyond the part of the PDF retrieved; typicality and typicality-squared coefficients remain unquoted.
- Berg 2022 primary text, Nunes & Ordanini 2014 coefficients and sample size, Interiano 2018 primary text (PMC blocked by a browser check) and Varnum 2021 effect sizes were not retrieved; figures above are from summaries or abstracts.
- Serrà et al. 2012 (Scientific Reports, Million Song Dataset, pitch-transition, timbre and loudness trends 1955 to 2010) was not opened this session; it is a trend study with no success comparison as far as I recall.
- Hamilton & Pearce 2024 (Scientific Reports, BiMMuDa) reports declining melodic complexity with breaks in 1975, 1996 and 2000 but compares no success levels; the sample is top-five year-end singles only. — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1049670)
- No peer-reviewed study was found relating symbolic melody features (as opposed to chords) to graded chart position or streams.

## 2. Critics' canons versus popularity: do acclaimed songs differ in the notes?

### Takeaway
No study was found that tests whether critics'-list songs differ from other hits on symbolic features with a significance test; the nearest evidence is the CoCoPops paper's statement that pitch, scale-degree and functional-harmony distributions are "broadly similar" between its Rolling Stone and Billboard halves. What is established is that critic and lay ratings of the same songs are essentially uncorrelated and that critics are insensitive to familiarity, so a critics' label is a genuinely different target from a chart label.

### Cited Findings
- CoCoPops (Arthur & Condit-Schultz, ISMIR 2023): the Rolling Stone subset (200 songs from the "500 Greatest Songs" list) and the Billboard subset share only 15 songs; Figure 4's pitch, scale-degree and functional-harmony distributions are described as "broadly similar" across the two; no numeric comparison is given. Corpus totals: 398 unique songs, 145,822 note onsets (86,215 Billboard), 37,010 chord changes (19,682 Billboard), 63,809 lyric words. — [CoCoPops paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000027.pdf)
- Lundy, Allred & Peebles 2019 (Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts): for 100 randomly selected songs, Rolling Stone critic ratings correlated r = −.0009 on average with 27 undergraduates' ratings; only 54% of 352 undergraduate pairs correlated positively with each other; non-experts rated familiar songs and artists higher, critics' ratings were unrelated to familiarity. Authors conclude at least two taste cultures. — [Lundy et al. 2019 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_2058448832)
- de Clercq 2017 warns that corpus averages "may not represent any broad stylistic trait but rather the generic midpoint of multiple smaller populations", and that McGill's adjudicated single annotation removes ambiguity information whereas RS200 keeps two independent analyses. The paper does not compare the two corpora quantitatively. — [de Clercq 2017](https://www.midside.com/publications/declercq_2017e_euromac9_pitfalls.pdf)
- The top-quartile Billboard songs carried more chord rarity and introduced more of the new chords (28.7% vs 19.8%), which is a popularity result, not a critics result, but it is the only quantified "innovators chart higher" finding in symbolic harmony. — [Miles et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8121146)

### Inferences
- The comparison the reader wants can be run on files already in hand: CoCoPops RS200 (critics) vs CoCoPops Billboard melodic subset (chart sample), same encoders, same format. The 15 shared songs are too few to be a class but allow a sanity check. Era and genre must be matched, because RS200 is rock-heavy and the Billboard sample is stratified across chart ranks and decades.
- Because the critics' list is itself graded (Rolling Stone list rank is a metadata field in every RS200 file, section 5), a within-critics ranking test is possible without any new data.
- Lundy's familiarity result suggests critics' labels are less contaminated by exposure than charts or streams, which is exactly the contamination the earlier report said to control. That makes a critics' rank a useful second axis, not a noisier copy of the first.

### Gaps
- No published test of Rolling Stone 500 or Acclaimed Music membership against symbolic features was found. Searches returned only student projects and blog posts joining Acclaimed Music to Spotify audio features.
- Acclaimed Music publishes no official bulk dataset or licence that I could confirm; its rankings would have to be transcribed or scraped, and its terms were not checked.
- Temperley & de Clercq's own papers (2011, 2013) may contain RS-vs-other comparisons of harmony or melody; they were not opened.

## 3. Two audiences: do musicians value different things from mass listeners?

### Takeaway
Direct evidence on the notes is thin. The solid findings are (a) musicians' liking peaks at higher harmonic complexity than non-musicians' in a controlled chord study, and (b) what musicians choose to play or cover is organised by genre, playability and a shared repertoire, and it diverges visibly from streaming ranks; no study quantifies which melodic or harmonic features separate "much covered" from "much streamed" songs.

### Cited Findings
- Witek et al. 2023 (PLoS ONE): 25 musicians vs 24 non-musicians rating 20 single chords at four complexity levels. Both groups show an inverted U (quadratic estimate −14.80, t = −11.57), but the shape differs (complexity × group interaction estimate 17.16, t = 4.34, p < .001): non-musicians like the plain triad best and liked the bare octave more than musicians did (difference 0.76 on a 5-point scale); musicians liked medium (four-note extended) and high (flat-ninth) chords more (differences 0.47 and 0.48) and rated triads and extended chords about equally. Among musicians the shift toward complexity correlated with active engagement and openness. — [Witek et al. 2023](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/185767621/journal.pone.0281057.pdf)
- Ortega 2021 (PLoS ONE), SecondHandSongs plus AllMusic, more than 106,000 artists and 855,000 covers: most-covered acts are The Beatles, Duke Ellington, Bing Crosby; before the 1960s covering was frequent because bands "compete in the performance of popular hits" from a shared repertoire of standards; after, covers concentrate on a few pop and rock stars; artists mainly cover within their own genre and language. No musical features analysed. — [CultureCase summary of Ortega 2021](https://culturecase.kdl.kcl.ac.uk/research/2025/07/dynamics-determine-cover-versions-recorded-western-music)
- Guitarists' demand: across page views for 3,424 songs on Ultimate Guitar, the top songs are "Hallelujah", "Wonderwall", "I'm Yours", "Perfect", "Can't Help Falling in Love"; top artists Ed Sheeran (229.5 million views), The Beatles (226.1 million), Taylor Swift (167.8 million). The article does not compare with streams. — [MusicRadar](https://www.musicradar.com/guitars/timeless-songs-that-translate-beautifully-to-guitar-ed-sheeran-is-the-king-of-the-guitar-tabs)
- Folk players (The Session, 9,378 tunes): tunebook adds are higher for tunes nearer the median on six of seven complexity measures, but marginal R² is 0.001 to 0.008; popular tunes have more settings (marginal R² 0.256) and more skewed preference among settings (Pielou evenness vs log popularity, coefficient −0.282, marginal R² 0.386). — [Street et al. 2022](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1207558)
- Hooktheory contributors (musically trained hobbyists choosing what to transcribe) over-represent game and library composers and The Beatles: the most-annotated "artists" in the Sheet Sage export are nintendo (456 sections), kevin-macleod (444), zun (404), the-beatles (323), sega (254), taylor-swift (173) [COMPUTED from the public JSON]. — [Sheet Sage data](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chrisdonahue/sheetsage-data/main/hooktheory/Hooktheory.json.gz)
- Cover-version similarity is carried mainly by tempo: in 70 original–cover pairs, tempo difference alone explains R² = .33 of perceived similarity, key .10, timbre .076, all musical features 40%. Relevant only as a reminder that the cover relation preserves melody, harmony and lyrics while changing the surface. — [Cassano-Coleman et al. 2025](https://escholarship.org/content/qt6hd1m02b/qt6hd1m02b.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Hallelujah" topping the guitar-tab list while being a minor streaming and chart item is the pattern the reader suspects: a musician-facing label (tab views, covers, transcription counts, tunebook adds, real-book inclusion) rewards songs that are self-sufficient as melody plus chords on one instrument. That is a hypothesis consistent with the listings above, not a tested result.
- Witek's result gives a direction for a "musicians' axis": harmonic richness tolerated or preferred at a higher level than for lay listeners. It was measured on isolated chords, so the transfer to whole songs is untested.
- Annotation count per song in Hooktheory, number of Ultimate Guitar versions or views per song (Chordonomicon is scraped from such sources), SecondHandSongs cover counts and Discogs-VI clique sizes are all musician-behaviour labels that can be joined to notes; Billboard peak, weeks and Spotify popularity are the mass-audience labels. Fitting the same features to both and comparing coefficient signs is the direct test, and it has not been published.

### Gaps
- No study was found comparing musical features of jazz standards or real-book inclusions with non-standards from the same era. Phillips's "Shaping Jazz" (Princeton, 2013) explains which early recordings were re-recorded by the geography and identity of their origin, not by the notes; it was located but not read. — [Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/isbn/9780691150888)
- No quantified comparison of tab-view rank against stream rank was found.
- Session-musician or songwriter survey data on which songs professionals admire, tied to features, was not found.

## 4. Lyrics–melody and prosody features linked to success

### Takeaway
No corpus study was found that links text-setting features (stress-to-beat alignment, syllable-to-note ratio, melisma, rhyme placement) to graded success. The success literature on lyrics is text-only: repetition (plus 14.5% odds of number 1 per chorus repeat), simplicity, and distinctiveness relative to genre; the data to test setting features against chart outcome exist in CoCoPops but have not been used that way.

### Cited Findings
- Chorus repetition and word repetition: see Nunes, Ordanini & Valsesia in section 1. — [Pacific Standard summary](https://psmag.com/?p=30843)
- Lyric simplicity predicts higher chart position among 14,661 charting songs, more so in crowded years. — [Varnum et al. 2021 record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0244576.html)
- Lyric novelty has an optimum slightly below the genre-year mean (−0.14 to −0.18 SD) for long chart runs, and is independent of audio novelty. — [O'Toole & Horvát](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07754)
- Berger & Packard 2018 [EARLIER NOTES]: 1,879 songs, seven genres, 2014 to 2016; lyrics more differentiated from the genre's topic profile ranked higher, controlling for airplay and artist; weaker in pop and dance; effect size not retrieved. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/to-climb-the-charts-write-lyrics-that-stand-out.html)
- CoCoPops' 214 Billboard melodic files include expert lyric transcriptions aligned to notes and rhyme-scheme annotation, and each file carries Billboard peak and weeks on chart; 100 of them also have continuous valence and arousal ratings. — [CoCoPops paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000027.pdf); [CoCoPops Billboard README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Computational-Cognitive-Musicology-Lab/CoCoPops/HEAD/Billboard/README.md)
- Within-song benchmark [EARLIER NOTES]: in BiMMuDa, choruses are shorter, sparser and slightly wider in interval than verses (380 verses, 325 choruses). — [Hamilton et al. 2024](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/168)

### Inferences
- Berger & Packard (more distinctive lyrics do better) and O'Toole & Horvát (optimum slightly more typical than average) disagree in sign near the centre; both are consistent with a narrow optimum close to the genre norm, and neither supports large departures.
- The first study of prosody against graded success can be done on the reader's own kept corpus: stress alignment, syllable rate, melisma share and rhyme density computed from CoCoPops Billboard files, regressed on peak and weeks with decade as a covariate. With about 200 songs only effects of roughly d ≥ 0.4 between quartiles are detectable, the size of Miles's larger contrasts.

### Gaps
- Searches for stress-mismatch or text-setting features against chart success returned only descriptive linguistics papers on text-setting; none with an outcome variable.
- Packard & Berger's second-person-pronoun result was not re-read; the earlier notes hold only a summary.

## 5. Datasets: which pair symbolic music with an outcome label, and which give a graded outcome on the same songs that have notes

### Takeaway
Three sources already give a graded outcome on songs with melody and chords: CoCoPops Billboard (peak and weeks inside every file), CoCoPops Rolling Stone (critics' list rank inside every file), and Hooktheory joined to a Billboard archive (about 2,200 songs with melody matched in a crude title join). The largest graded-label resources are chords-only (Chordonomicon with Spotify IDs) or MIDI of uncertain fidelity (Lakh-matched with Million Song Dataset "hotttnesss", MetaMIDI with Spotify matches); every pop corpus still samples only successes.

### Cited Findings

A. Graded outcome on the same songs that have notes (confirmed)
- CoCoPops Billboard subset. 739 McGill songs with chords; 214 with expert melody, lyrics and rhyme; humdrum; GitHub. Every file ends with reference records `!!!BillboardChartDate`, `!!!BillboardPeak`, `!!!BillboardWeeksOnChart`, `!!!SampleTargetRank`, `!!!SampleActualRank`, `!!!SampleID#`, and (melodic files) a MusicBrainz ID. The McGill sample was stratified over chart ranks 1 to 100 and decades, so low-peaking songs are present by design. — [CoCoPops Billboard README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Computational-Cognitive-Musicology-Lab/CoCoPops/HEAD/Billboard/README.md); [example file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Computational-Cognitive-Musicology-Lab/CoCoPops/HEAD/Billboard/Data/ABBA_Chiquitita_1979.varms.hum)
- [COMPUTED] I downloaded all 214 melodic Billboard files. All 214 carry BillboardPeak; 198 carry an MBID. For the 170 sampled once (the other 44 have comma-separated repeats that need parsing): peak ranges 1 to 92, median 8, quartiles 2 / 8 / 26; 38 reached number 1, 58 peaked 2 to 10, 47 peaked 11 to 40, 27 peaked 41 to 100; weeks on chart range 2 to 43, median 14, quartiles 11 / 14 / 18; Spearman correlation of peak with weeks = −0.65. Chart dates: 1950s 13, 1960s 58, 1970s 68, 1980s 66, 1990s 9. — [CoCoPops sample table](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Computational-Cognitive-Musicology-Lab/CoCoPops/HEAD/CoCoPops_Sample.tsv)
- CoCoPops licence conflict: the repository README states CC BY-NC 4.0; the ISMIR paper states CC-BY-4.0. — [CoCoPops README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Computational-Cognitive-Musicology-Lab/CoCoPops/HEAD/README.md); [CoCoPops paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000027.pdf)
- CoCoPops Rolling Stone subset (RS200, de Clercq & Temperley). 200 melodic and harmonic transcriptions of songs on Rolling Stone's "500 Greatest Songs" list; each file carries `!!!Rolling Stone List Rank`; a `RS 5x20 subset` column marks the original 100. Graded critics' label on songs with notes. — [CoCoPops RollingStone README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Computational-Cognitive-Musicology-Lab/CoCoPops/HEAD/RollingStone/README.md)

Included whole in CoCoPops. — [CoCoPops Billboard README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Computational-Cognitive-Musicology-Lab/CoCoPops/HEAD/Billboard/README.md)
- Hooktheory / TheoryTab via Sheet Sage. 26,175 annotated sections (23,833 tagged MELODY, 25,856 HARMONY), 15,213 artist–song pairs from 6,079 artists [COMPUTED]; each record has artist and song slugs, Hooktheory URL, YouTube ID, key, metre, melody notes and chords; CC BY-NC-SA 3.0; 20 MB JSON. No outcome field in the file. — [Sheet Sage README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chrisdonahue/sheetsage/HEAD/README.md); [data file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chrisdonahue/sheetsage-data/main/hooktheory/Hooktheory.json.gz)
- [COMPUTED] Joining Hooktheory slugs to a public Hot 100 archive (weekly charts from 1958-08-04, columns chart_week, current_week, title, performer, peak_pos, wks_on_chart) by normalised artist and title matched 2,299 songs, 2,207 of them with melody: 388 number 1s, 694 peaking 2 to 10, 647 peaking 11 to 40, 478 peaking 41 to 100; by first chart decade 1960s 161, 1970s 231, 1980s 301, 1990s 281, 2000s 459, 2010s 719; median 2 transcribed sections per song. The match is crude string matching (misses variant spellings, may include a few false matches) and needs cleaning before use. — [rwd-billboard-data Hot 100 CSV](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/utdata/rwd-billboard-data/main/data-out/hot-100-current.csv); [data file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chrisdonahue/sheetsage-data/main/hooktheory/Hooktheory.json.gz)
- BiMMuDa. 371 melodies of top-five Billboard year-end singles 1950 to 2022 (381 metadata rows), MIDI per section plus MuseScore, CC BY 4.0. Metadata has a year-end `Position` (1 to 5) and a Spotify link per song. Graded only within the top five, so the range is narrow; the Spotify link allows a present-day popularity join. — [BiMMuDa README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/madelinehamilton/BiMMuDa/HEAD/README.md); [per-song metadata](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/madelinehamilton/BiMMuDa/HEAD/metadata/bimmuda_per_song_metadata.csv)
- The Session. 38,151 settings of 18,042 tunes in ABC; tunebook adds (9,782 tunes with at least 10) and set adds as graded musician-popularity. Licence bars LLM processing [EARLIER NOTES]. — [Street et al. 2022](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1207558)
- PDMX. More than 250,000 public-domain MuseScore scores with user ratings, views and favourites; about 13,000 rated, deduplicated; ratings range 2.83 to 4.98 and reflect engraving and arrangement [EARLIER NOTES]. A licence discrepancy affects 12.29% (31,221) of songs; the maintainers recommend the `no_license_conflict` subset. — [PDMX README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pnlong/PDMX/HEAD/README.md); [PDMX on Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/15571083)

B. Symbolic, with identifiers that allow a graded join (join not verified by me)
- Chordonomicon. More than 666,000 song-level chord progressions with section labels, genre and sub-genre, release date and Spotify IDs; CC BY-NC 4.0; Hugging Face `ailsntua/Chordonomicon`. Chords only, user-generated. Spotify IDs make a popularity join direct. — [Chordonomicon dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ailsntua/Chordonomicon/raw/main/README.md)
- Lakh MIDI. LMD-full 176,581 MIDIs; LMD-matched 45,129 files matched to Million Song Dataset entries with match confidence scores; MSD HDF5 metadata for every matched entry is downloadable; CC BY 4.0. - Popular Hooks (ICMEW 2024). 38,694 hooks as MIDI with melody, harmony, section, tonality, genre, region, lyrics, YouTube links with time stamps, and a TheoryTab URL per song; emotion labels are model-predicted; no popularity field; no licence stated on the card. — [Popular Hooks dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/NEXTLab-ZJU/popular-hook/blame/09deb6cde47ea34d8b86924383ebc11d11e2cb9e/README.md)
- OpenEWLD. Public-domain subset of Wikifonia lead sheets (melody, chords, lyrics; 1,513 files per earlier notes), work entries keyed to SecondHandSongs works with first performance date, language, Discogs genres and styles. - EWLD (full). More than 5,000 Wikifonia lead sheets with composer, work, lyrics and feature metadata; about 5.2 GB; restricted access on Zenodo by request with a non-commercial declaration; no open licence. — [EWLD on Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/record/1476555)

### Inferences
- The reader's kept corpus already contains its own graded label. The 159 kept CoCoPops songs are a subset of the 214 files whose footers hold peak and weeks; reading those two records converts the kept set from one class into a ranked set spanning number 1 to the bottom of the chart, with no join needed. That is the cheapest possible step toward ranking within good music.
- With about 160 to 214 songs and a peak distribution that is top-heavy (median peak 8), a top-versus-bottom quartile contrast has about 40 to 55 songs per arm, enough to detect d about 0.55 at conventional power and not the d about 0.3 typical of Miles. Hooktheory joined to Billboard (about 2,200 songs) is what gives the power to see d about 0.12; its costs are section-only transcriptions, hobbyist accuracy, and a 2010s-heavy distribution.
- A three-label design is available on symbolic data today: mass audience at release (Billboard peak and weeks: CoCoPops, Hooktheory join), critics (Rolling Stone rank: RS200), musicians (Hooktheory annotation count, The Session tunebook adds, cover counts via MBID or SecondHandSongs work links). The 198 MusicBrainz IDs in the CoCoPops melodic files are the key that makes present-day streams and cover counts joinable to the kept corpus.
- Chordonomicon is the only resource large enough to fit a typicality optimum on symbolic harmony against Spotify popularity with genre and release-year controls, replicating Askin & Mauskapf and O'Toole & Horvát in chords instead of audio. Campagna's null on about 900 Hooktheory songs is the prior to beat.
- Lakh and MetaMIDI are fan-sequenced MIDI of unknown fidelity with the melody track unlabelled, and the audio matching mis-pairs similar songs; they are better treated as a noisy large-sample check than as the calibration set.
- No dataset supplies released-but-uncharted songs with notes. The nearest proxies for the bottom of the distribution are McGill's deliberately sampled low chart ranks and Hooktheory songs that never charted (about 13,000 artist–song pairs did not match the Hot 100, a mix of non-US, album tracks, game and library music).

### Gaps
- I did not verify the Lakh–MSD field list, the MetaMIDI Zenodo terms, the Chordonomicon column names beyond the dataset card's prose, or the PDMX column names (`rating`, `n_ratings`, views and favourites are described in the paper per the earlier notes).
- Wikifonia's original size and takedown history, the Nottingham dataset's size and the Essen licence were not re-checked this session.
- Whether SecondHandSongs or WhoSampled permit bulk research use is unknown; neither publishes an open dump that I could find.
- The Hooktheory–Billboard match count is from naive string matching and will change with a proper matcher (MusicBrainz or YouTube ID based).
- How the 214 CoCoPops melodic songs were chosen from the 739 is not described in the paper, so their representativeness of the stratified McGill sample is unknown; the peak distribution I computed (median 8) suggests they lean toward bigger hits.

## 6. Oral-tradition survival as a quality signal

### Takeaway
Nothing found this session changes the earlier reports' conclusion: survival and popularity in oral and player-driven traditions favour intermediate, expected, shorter and more repetitive material, with very small explained variance (marginal R² 0.001 to 0.008 for tune popularity, 0.05 for phrase survival), while the size of the variant set tracks popularity far more strongly (R² about 0.26) than any melodic feature does.

### Cited Findings
- Street, Eerola & Kendal 2022 (Humanities and Social Sciences Communications), The Session, 9,378 tunes: popularity is negatively associated with distance from median complexity on bar count, melodic expectancy, tone transitions, pitch entropy, pitch-pair entropy and self-similarity novelty (all pMCMC ≤ 0.007; note count 0.070), marginal R² 0.001 to 0.008. Effects were confined to older tunes for most measures. The inverted U is inferred from deviation-from-median, not fitted with a quadratic. — [Street et al. 2022](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1207558)
- Same study: number of settings rises with tunebook adds (marginal R² 0.256); variation in complexity among settings falls with popularity once richness is controlled, read as convergence; preference among settings is more skewed for popular tunes (marginal R² 0.386). At the setting level, with tune as a random effect, feature effects are weaker (marginal R² ≤ 0.001; conditional R² 0.20 to 0.70), that is, tune identity explains most of the variance. — [Street et al. 2022](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1207558)
- Janssen, Burgoyne & Honing 2017 [EARLIER NOTES]: 9,639 phrases in 147 Dutch tune families; standardised effects on recurrence across variants: phrase length −0.30, surprisal −0.24, phrase position −0.10, pitch proximity +0.10, phrase repetition +0.09, motif repetivity +0.08, pitch reversal +0.05; marginal R² 0.05, conditional 0.22. — [Janssen et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5403935/)

### Inferences
- The oral-tradition and chart literatures agree on shape (closeness to a contemporaneous norm) and on size (single-digit percentages of variance). They disagree in one place: within Billboard, the top quartile has rarer chords in verses, while in folk transmission lower surprisal survives. These are reconcilable if rarity helps a song stand out at release and predictability helps it be retained and reproduced, which is the same initial-versus-sustained split Berg reports at artist level. It would be worth testing on the kept corpus by fitting the same surprise feature to peak and to a present-day persistence measure separately.
- Tune-family size and number of settings behave like exposure measures (they scale with popularity) more than quality measures; using them as a label without a popularity or age control will mostly rediscover fame.

### Gaps
- No study was found that predicts tune-family SIZE (as opposed to phrase recurrence within families) from melodic features in the Meertens collections.
- No new work from 2023 to 2026 on feature predictors of variant stability surfaced in searches beyond what the earlier notes list (Savage 2022; McBride & Fitch 2026 as recorded earlier).
- Tune age is uncontrolled in The Session analysis, so "older tunes show the effect" may be a survivorship artefact.
