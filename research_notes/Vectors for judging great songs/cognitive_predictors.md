## 1. Uncertainty × surprise: what exactly predicts pleasure, how it is computed, and whether it replicates

### Takeaway
On melodies, the robust finding across five independent datasets is that liking falls as mean information content (IC) rises, with at most a small bend at the predictable end; the famous uncertainty × surprise interaction has now been reported in two opposite directions (Cheung 2019 on chords versus Mas-Herrero 2025 on melodies) and was only marginal (p = 0.06) in Gold 2019. Entropy (uncertainty before the note) is worth computing as a separate reading, but its sign in an interaction term should not be fixed in advance.

### Cited Findings

**How the quantities are computed (Gold 2019, the reference melody study)**
- IDyOM trained on 903 melodies (152 Canadian folk songs, 566 German folk songs, 185 Bach chorale melodies); viewpoints were inter-onset-interval ratio and chromatic pitch combined independently, chosen because that configuration best matched 24 listeners' unexpectedness ratings — [Gold et al. 2019, J Neurosci](https://core.ac.uk/download/237088229.pdf)
- Both IC (surprise of the note that occurred) and entropy (uncertainty of the predictive distribution before the note) were weighted by note duration, averaged over each 30-second excerpt, and z-scored ("mDW-IC", "mDW-Ent") — [Gold et al. 2019](https://core.ac.uk/download/237088229.pdf)
- Study 1: 43 listeners, 55 real-repertoire monophonic excerpts, 1,906 unfamiliar trials. Liking on mDW-IC: linear β = −0.21, quadratic β = −0.09, model R² = 26.3% (linear-only model 25.3%, so the quadratic adds about 1 point). Liking on mDW-Ent: linear β = −0.09, quadratic β = −0.06, R² = 19.1% — [Gold et al. 2019](https://core.ac.uk/download/237088229.pdf)
- Study 2: 27 new listeners, 12 excerpts each heard seven times in a row. mDW-IC: no linear term (p = 0.30), quadratic β = −0.18, R² = 41.6%. mDW-Ent: linear β = −0.31, quadratic β = −0.25, R² = 34.9% — [Gold et al. 2019](https://core.ac.uk/download/237088229.pdf)
- The IC × entropy interaction in Gold 2019 was tested by ANOVA on six k-means clusters and was not significant: F(1.71, 70.21) = 3.17, partial η² = 0.07, p = 0.06. Main effect of IC: partial η² = 0.51. Main effect of entropy: p = 0.10 — [Gold et al. 2019](https://core.ac.uk/download/237088229.pdf)
- Direction of the suggested interaction in Gold 2019: at low IC, liking was higher for high-entropy than for low-entropy excerpts (p = 0.001); under high entropy, low-IC excerpts beat medium-IC (p = 0.01); under low entropy, low-IC and medium-IC did not differ (p = 0.35). The authors' wording: "uncertain contexts amplify the pleasure of predictability" — [Gold et al. 2019](https://core.ac.uk/download/237088229.pdf)
- Repetition did not move the curve: the IC-curve × repetition interaction was not significant (p = 0.38); liking simply fell across seven consecutive hearings (repetition model R² = 0.81 with a small negative quadratic term, β = −0.003) — [Gold et al. 2019](https://core.ac.uk/download/237088229.pdf)

**Cheung 2019 (chords, not melody)**
- A machine-learning model quantified uncertainty and surprise for 80,000 chords in US Billboard pop songs; pleasure was high for (a) low uncertainty + high surprise and (b) high uncertainty + low surprise; amygdala, hippocampus and auditory cortex tracked the interaction and nucleus accumbens tracked only uncertainty — [Cheung et al. 2019, Current Biology, abstract page](https://cms.mus.cam.ac.uk/publications/cheung-uncertainty-surprise/)
- Follow-up by the same group on over 1,000 chords sampled from 739 Billboard songs compared four expectancy models: cognitive (long-term statistical) and sensory (local acoustic) expectations each independently predicted expectancy and pleasantness ratings, and cognitive expectations explained more than twice the variance in perceived surprise — [Cheung et al. 2024, Phil Trans R Soc B, abstract page](https://cms.mus.cam.ac.uk/publications/cheung-expectations/)

**Tests on melody since 2019**
- Korkor, Noreika, Luft & Pearce 2025 (three experiments; N = 182, 93, 105; melodies with 20 graded variants each; IDyOM long-term model trained on the same 903 melodies plus a short-term model). Liking declined with IC in every experiment: Exp 1 linear IC −0.131, R² = 0.58; adding a quadratic raised R² to 0.61 but the quadratic coefficient was positive (IC −0.27, IC² +0.0096). Exp 2 linear IC −0.093, R² = 0.51, quadratic n.s. Exp 3 (Charlie Parker bebop segments) linear IC −0.072, R² = 0.265, quadratic n.s. The authors state their results conflict with the Wundt curve reported by Gold 2019 and Cheung 2019 — [Korkor et al. 2025, Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/32249/4/FullText.pdf)
- Same paper: musical style did not change the slope (Style × IC = 0.020, n.s.; combined R² = 0.384), and one repetition of the stimulus, immediate or delayed, did not change liking (−0.025 and +0.006, both n.s.). Entropy was used only as a control variable; no interaction with entropy was tested — [Korkor et al. 2025](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/32249/4/FullText.pdf)
- Mas-Herrero & Marco-Pallarés 2025 (PNAS; two independent large samples choosing between generated melodies varying in surprise and entropy): inverted U between predictability and preference, plus an interaction in which smaller surprises were preferred in low-entropy melodies and larger surprises in high-entropy melodies; models with this interaction predicted individuals' genre preferences and pleasure for real compositions — [Mas-Herrero & Marco-Pallarés 2025, abstract page](https://www.ub.edu/brainvitge/publication/predictive-processes-shape-individual-musical-preferences)
- Bianco, Gold, Johnson & Penhune 2019 (26 non-musicians, 16 composed 13-note isochronous melodies, mean IC 4.48 ± 1.56, range 1.7 to 9.1, IDyOM on the same 903 melodies): quadratic term for liking significant, χ²(1) = 4.51, p = 0.033, but a linear model fitted almost as well (ΔAIC = 0.6); pupil dilation was larger for predictable melodies, t(22) = 3.14, p = 0.005, and for high-liked melodies — [Bianco et al. 2019, Scientific Reports](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10087105/1/s41598-019-53510-w.pdf)
- Albury, Bianco, Gold & Penhune 2023 (N = 235 online; 24 repertoire melodies with mean IC 6.1, range 3.7 to 10.8, and 24 composed melodies with mean IC 4.5, range 2.8 to 8.1): mean IC had no significant linear (p = 0.33) or quadratic (p = 0.24) effect on liking overall; what mattered was context. Heard alone, the two sets were liked about equally (4.35 vs 3.93, n.s.); mixed together, repertoire rose to 4.99 and composed fell to 3.35 (both shifts p < 0.001). Rated predictability, as opposed to model IC, showed a quadratic (inverted-U) relation to liking, χ²(1) = 15.71, p < 0.001 — [Albury et al. 2023, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1175682/full)
- Mean IC predicted listeners' predictability ratings (b = −0.32, χ²(1) = 36.07, p < 0.001), so the model reading is valid as a measure of perceived predictability even where it fails to predict liking — [Albury et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1175682/full)

**Exposure and prediction error in an unfamiliar scale**
- Kathios, Sachs, Zhang, Ou & Loui (Psychological Science 2024; preprint read): eight studies plus fMRI, about 160 to 180 listeners each, 20-second monophonic melodies in the Bohlen-Pierce scale heard 0 to 16 times. Each melody had an original and an altered ending. Original endings were liked more than altered endings in every study (e.g. β = 0.09, p < 0.001; β = 0.10, p < 0.001), and exposure raised liking in every study (smallest β = 0.02, p = 0.04). The exposure × prediction-error interaction was significant only in Study 1 (β = 0.05, p = 0.02) and not in Studies 2 to 4 (p = 0.29, 0.57, 0.38) — [Kathios et al., bioRxiv full text](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.17.496615.full.pdf)
- Pooled over Studies 1 to 4 (N = 667), familiarity grew logarithmically with exposure count and liking followed an inverted U over 0 to 16 exposures; the pattern replicated in a Chinese sample (N = 156) — [Kathios et al.](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.17.496615.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- Peak location in Gold Study 1 (my arithmetic, vertex = −b/2c): mDW-IC peak about 1.17 SD below the stimulus mean; mDW-Ent peak about 0.75 SD below the mean. In Study 2 the IC peak is at the mean (no linear term) and the entropy peak about 0.62 SD below. For a judge that already uses a two-sided distance from kept-song quartiles on mean IC, this says the target should sit in the lower part of the reference distribution, not the middle, and the penalty should be asymmetric: steep above, shallow below.
- Korkor Exp 1's quadratic is convex (positive IC² term), so it is a decline that flattens, with its minimum at IC ≈ 14 (my arithmetic: 0.27 / (2 × 0.0096)), outside the usual range. Three experiments, 380 listeners, zero inverted U. Combined with Albury (no IC effect once context varies) and Bianco (quadratic indistinguishable from linear), the "sweet spot" on melodies rests mainly on Gold 2019 and Mas-Herrero 2025.
- The interaction is unsafe to hard-code. Cheung (chords): high surprise is good when uncertainty is low. Mas-Herrero (melodies): large surprise is good when entropy is high, small surprise when entropy is low. Gold (melodies, p = 0.06): predictability is most pleasing when entropy is high. These three are not the same pattern; Gold's and Mas-Herrero's point in opposite directions on the high-entropy side. The earlier report's advice (include the product as a feature, let data set the sign) stands and is strengthened.
- Entropy can be computed from the same corpus model the reader already has (Shannon entropy of the next-note distribution, duration-weighted). In Gold Study 2 mean entropy alone carried R² = 34.9% with a linear β of −0.31, larger than IC's linear term in that study. It is the cheapest new reading on this list.
- Albury's context result is a warning about calibration: the same melodies moved by about 0.6 scale points depending on what they were heard next to. A judge's absolute scale is meaningless; only rank within a comparable set is.
- Duration-weighting matters as a design choice: Gold weights each note's IC by its length, so a surprising long note counts more than a surprising passing note. If the reader's mean IC is unweighted, this is a concrete variant to test.

### Gaps
- Cheung 2019's exact design numbers (listener N, number of progressions, the exact regression table) could not be re-read here; the full-text PDF was blocked and PubMed returned a captcha. The earlier report has the interaction coefficient (0.124, marginal R² = 0.476) from the full text.
- Mas-Herrero 2025 was read only as an abstract in this pass (PMC blocked); sample sizes and betas are in the earlier report (240 melodies, about 400 listeners, IC linear −0.25, quadratic −0.25, interaction +0.10 and +0.12).
- No study found that tests the uncertainty × surprise interaction on melody WITH chords together, which is the reader's actual object. All melody studies are monophonic; Cheung's are chords with melody removed.
- No direct, published, peer-reviewed failed replication of Cheung 2019 on chords was found; the only report of non-replication is the journalism cited in the earlier report.

## 2. Memorability and earworms: which features predict recognition or recall

### Takeaway
Nothing found since the earlier reports raises the ceiling: the newer memorability and "catchiness" work has moved to audio features and reports single-feature R² values of about 0.01, with listener-side factors (recognisability, style liking) dominating. The symbolic evidence still rests on FANTASTIC-style features (Müllensiefen & Halpern 2014, Jakubowski 2017, Van Balen), already covered.

### Cited Findings
- Hooked on Music (Korsmit, Burgoyne & Honing 2017): about 140,000 plays from 4,082 participants on 184 fragments of UK top-20 songs from the 1940s onward. Audio-derived features (CATCHY toolbox) explained 11%, 11%, 12% and 4% of variance in the discrimination parameters of four recognition factors and 2 to 3% for verification — [Korsmit et al. 2017](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/16325081/Korsmit_et_al.pdf)
- In the same analysis, melodic complexity (−0.21, SE 0.06) and harmonic complexity (−0.11, SE 0.05, p = 0.08) were negative predictors for one recognition factor; rhythmic conventionality, tonal conventionality and recurrence appeared with mixed signs across factors (recurrence +0.15 for factor 1). The coefficient table was garbled in the source text, so the mapping should be checked against the PDF before quoting — [Korsmit et al. 2017](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/16325081/Korsmit_et_al.pdf)
- Bechtold, Curry & Witek 2025: 92 listeners rated 54 AI-generated songs (Suno v3.5, nine styles, 90 and 120 BPM) for catchiness and urge to move. No symbolic features were available. Best single audio features for catchiness: harmonic change detection function B = 0.099 (R²m = 0.010), spectral entropy B = 0.093 (R²m = 0.009), percussiveness B = 0.081 (R²m = 0.007); key strength was negative, B = −0.061 (R²m = 0.004). Self-rated recognisability was far stronger: B = 0.291, R²m = 0.094 — [Bechtold et al. 2025, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1642561/full)
- A 2023 ISMIR dataset paper built a music-piece memorability dataset with an interactive memory procedure and deep-learning baselines; its headline finding is that higher valence, higher arousal and faster tempo go with more memorable music, using interpretable features and mel-spectrograms (audio) — [arXiv 2405.12847, abstract](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2405.12847)
- Complexity hurts short-term motor memory for melody only weakly: in Bianco 2019, predictable (low-IC) melodies were reproduced with better timing during training (χ²(1) = 11.68, p < 0.001), but at test there was no effect of predictability or liking on accuracy or timing (all p > 0.389) — [Bianco et al. 2019](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10087105/1/s41598-019-53510-w.pdf)
- Familiarity (the memory trace itself) grows logarithmically with number of hearings, not linearly (AIC 31,575 vs 33,986 for linear; N = 667), replicated in a second culture (AIC 8,376.9 vs 8,958.3; N = 156) — [Kathios et al.](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.17.496615.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Harmonic change" being the top audio correlate of rated catchiness (Bechtold) and "melodic/harmonic complexity" being negative for recognition (Korsmit) point the same way as the earlier report: simple material with clear harmonic motion is recognised; complexity costs memory. Both effects are tiny (R² ≈ 0.01 per feature).
- Harmonic change rate is computable from a chord chart (chord changes per bar, or tonal-centroid distance between successive chords). It is the only new candidate reading in this section, and its support is one study on AI-generated audio.
- The logarithmic familiarity curve gives a principled weight for within-song repetition counts: the second and third statement of a hook buy most of the familiarity; the eighth buys little.

### Gaps
- "MemoMusic" as named in the brief was not located as a note-level memorability study; the nearest hit is the ISMIR 2023 memorability dataset, read only as an abstract (dataset size, reliability and model scores not retrieved).
- No 2015 to 2026 study was found that predicts recognition or recall of pop melodies from symbolic features with a cross-validated R² above the Müllensiefen & Halpern 2014 figures already in the earlier report (0.09 explicit, 0.25 implicit).
- Hooked on Music's original feature paper (Burgoyne et al. 2013) and Van Balen's 2015 corpus-tool paper were not re-read; Van Balen is covered in the earlier report.

## 3. Repetition, mere exposure and the inverted U: how much and what kind

### Takeaway
The inverted U between liking and familiarity/complexity is the best-supported general shape in the field (50 of 57 studies compatible), and inserting literal repetition into unfamiliar music raises enjoyment; but no study supplies a numeric optimum for within-piece repetition, and correlational work finds perceived repetition and preference to be nearly independent.

### Cited Findings
- Chmiel & Schubert 2017 reviewed 115 years of research: of 57 studies, 50 (87.7%) were compatible with a segmented inverted-U model of preference against familiarity or complexity, 5 (8.8%) were mixed and 2 (3.5%) incompatible — [Chmiel & Schubert 2017, Psychology of Music, abstract](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/back-to-the-inverted-u-for-music-preference-a-review-of-the-liter/)
- Margulis 2013: one-minute excerpts of Berio and Carter were presented unaltered, with segments repeated immediately, or with segments repeated after a delay; listeners without contemporary-music expertise rated both repetition versions more enjoyable, more interesting and more artistic (7-point scales) than the originals — [Margulis 2013, Empirical Studies of the Arts, abstract](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/aesthetic-responses-to-repetition-in-unfamiliar-music/)
- Touizrar, Knoll & Siedenburg 2021 (60 listeners, 14 three-minute orchestral excerpts, listeners tapped whenever something seemed to repeat): a two-factor solution explained 92% of variance; liking and interest loaded on a preference factor (0.45, 0.47) and essentially zero (−0.03, −0.02) on the repetition factor. Synchronised repetition detection correlated r = 0.83 with the repetition factor and only r = 0.51 (p = 0.065) with the preference factor. Tonal pieces were both more repetitive and far more liked than modernist pieces (β = −1.85 for modernist vs tonal preference) — [Touizrar et al. 2021, Frontiers in Psychology](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.673706/full)
- Touizrar et al. also summarise Tan et al. 2006: repeated hearings raised liking for patchworked excerpts but lowered it for intact ones — [Touizrar et al. 2021](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.673706/full)
- Across-hearing exposure: liking over 0 to 16 exposures follows an inverted U (quadratic beats linear, χ²(13) = 127.03, p < 0.001, N = 667) while familiarity keeps rising logarithmically. Split by music-reward sensitivity, only the low-reward ("anhedonic") tertile showed a significant inverted U (β = −0.04, p = 0.002) — [Kathios et al.](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.17.496615.full.pdf)
- Seven back-to-back hearings of the same 30-second excerpt lowered liking steadily (boredom), without shifting the preferred level of predictability — [Gold et al. 2019](https://core.ac.uk/download/237088229.pdf)
- A single repeat of a short melody, immediate or delayed, did not change liking (coefficients −0.025 and +0.006, n.s., N = 105) — [Korkor et al. 2025](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/32249/4/FullText.pdf)
- Pattern-level evidence from groove: two-bar drum patterns looped eight times are the standard stimulus, and the inverted U there is over syncopation within the loop, with repetition held constant — [Céspedes-Guevara & Witek 2023](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/189045021/Rhythm_syncopation_movement_and_pleasantness.pdf)

### Inferences
- The inverted U is about a listener's position on a familiarity/complexity axis, and the "segmented" wording in Chmiel & Schubert means individual studies usually see only one limb. That matches section 1: real, tonal, short melodies sit on the descending limb for surprise.
- Margulis (experimental, repetition inserted) and Touizrar (correlational, repetition detected) disagree in emphasis. The reconciliation that fits both: repetition helps when the material is otherwise hard to parse (atonal, unfamiliar) and adds little once the material is already tonal and predictable. For machine-written pop, which is already tonal, repetition should be a gate (enough of it to be parseable) and not a term that keeps paying.
- Because familiarity is logarithmic in exposures and liking peaks then falls, a within-song repetition reading could use log2(number of statements of a phrase) and expect diminishing returns after roughly 3 to 4 statements. This is an inference from between-hearing data applied to within-song repeats; no study tests it directly.
- Compressibility and self-similarity remain without listener evidence of an optimum (earlier report: LZ77 did not separate chart ranks). Nothing new found.

### Gaps
- Margulis 2013's N, means and effect sizes were not retrievable (abstract only). Her 2014 book and the Margulis & Simchy-Gross 2016 "repetition makes random tones sound musical" paper were not read in this pass.
- No study found gives a preferred proportion of repeated material, a preferred self-similarity value, or a preferred compression ratio for melodies.
- Chmiel & Schubert's review mixes familiarity (number of hearings) and complexity; it does not give a common metric for "optimal complexity" that could be computed from notes.

## 4. Chills and peak-pleasure triggers identifiable in a score

### Takeaway
Controlled (manipulation) evidence for score-identifiable chill triggers is thin: one small removal study (N = 24) found chills less frequent when the chill passage was cut, significant only when three pieces were pooled, and the passages were defined by texture and dynamics, not by notes. Appoggiaturas, leaps-then-steps and harmonic changes as chill triggers still have no controlled test.

### Cited Findings
- Bannister & Eerola 2018 ("Suppressing the Chills"): 24 chill-prone listeners, three pieces (Sigur Rós "Glósóli", Holst "Jupiter", Steven Wilson "Ancestral"); the chill section in each (a crescendo/dynamic climax of guitars and drums; a slower thematic string statement; a guitar solo) was removed and the audio spliced. Chill reports original vs manipulated: 10 vs 7, 10 vs 8, 10 vs 6; none significant alone (φ = 0.14 to 0.23), pooled χ² = 3.85, p = 0.049, φ = 0.23 — [Bannister & Eerola 2018, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full)
- In the same study, continuous chill-intensity ratings were higher in the chill sections than in control sections of the same piece (z = 4.18 to 5.52, all p < 0.0001), and phasic skin conductance was higher in chill sections than in musical control sections (z = 2.81 to 3.71) but did not differ significantly from the sections that replaced them (p = 0.075 to 0.46) — [Bannister & Eerola 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full)
- Build-up and release is measurable as a tension shape: in 60 EDM "break routine" clips (N = 34), rated tension rose almost monotonically through the build-up (r_s = 0.998 with time) and fell after the drop (r_s = −0.853), with the fall beginning roughly 2 to 3 seconds after the drop (described, not tested). Clip-level tension correlated +0.92 with arousal, +0.62 with emotional strength and −0.69 with valence. None of eight audio features (loudness, flux, brightness, roughness, beat clarity, mode etc.) correlated significantly with tension — [Turrell et al. 2025, Music Perception](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/106400/2/Building%20the%20anticipation_Javadi_AAM.pdf)
- Predictable, liked melodies produce larger pupil dilation (a physiological arousal marker) than unpredictable ones, t(22) = 3.14, p = 0.005, and the predictability effect appeared only for highly liked melodies (b = 4.85, p = 0.021) — [Bianco et al. 2019](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10087105/1/s41598-019-53510-w.pdf)

### Inferences
- The only manipulation study removes whole passages, so it shows that "the climactic section" matters, not which notated device inside it does. The three passages are a crescendo, a broad new theme, and a solo entrance: all are texture/dynamics/entry events, which a lead sheet does not carry. For a notes-and-chords judge, the transferable part is structural: is there a section that stands out as an arrival (register peak, longer notes, slower harmonic rhythm, new melodic material)?
- Turrell's negative correlation between tension and valence (−0.69) is a caution: more tension is more arousing but not more pleasant. This agrees with the earlier report's "score the trajectory, not the mean".
- Combining the earlier oChiM result (IC rises and entropy falls at chill onsets, AUC 0.597) with these: the one note-level chill reading with any evidence is a local IC spike under low entropy, which is the Cheung "low uncertainty, high surprise" cell applied to a moment. Evidence is observational and weak.

### Gaps
- No controlled experiment found, 2015 to 2026, that inserts or removes an appoggiatura, a leap followed by a step, a specific chord change, or a new voice entry and measures chills or pleasure. Sloboda's 1991 survey remains the origin of those claims (covered in the earlier report).
- Crescendo and voice entry need audio or an arrangement; they are not computable from melody plus chords.

## 5. Tension models with listener validation; does any tension profile predict liking?

### Takeaway
Automated tension models now reach a correlation of about 0.6 to 0.7 with continuous listener ratings on held-out pieces, but loudness and roughness do most of the work and the symbolic tonal-tension part (Herremans & Chew's spiral array) contributes little; no study was found showing that any tension profile shape predicts liking.

### Cited Findings
- TenseMusic (Barchet, Rimmele & Pelofi 2024): open-source, audio-in model extending Farbood 2012 with six features (loudness, pitch height, tonal tension, roughness, tempo, onset frequency). Tested on 38 classical and modern pieces of 60 to 90 seconds with 24 raters (inter-rater ICC = 0.80). Fit: Spearman r = 0.62 (weighted model, marginal R² = 0.46) and 0.68 (time-scale model, R² = 0.52); leave-one-out r = 0.59 and 0.61; 26 to 27 of 38 pieces exceeded r = 0.5 — [Barchet et al. 2024, PLOS ONE](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3565372_1/component/file_3565373/kog-24-rim-01-tenseMusic.pdf)
- Feature weights: loudness highest; pitch height, roughness and tonal tension medium positive; tempo small positive; onset frequency small negative. Tonal tension was computed as spiral-array tensile strain (Herremans & Chew) on pitches transcribed from audio, and the authors report it had small predictive power with limited overlap with ratings — [Barchet et al. 2024](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3565372_1/component/file_3565373/kog-24-rim-01-tenseMusic.pdf)
- Integration windows in the best model: attention windows of 3 s for loudness, roughness, tempo and onset rate, 5 s for pitch height, 7 s for tonal tension; memory windows of 3 s (loudness), 4 s (roughness), 12 s (pitch and tonal tension), 20 s (tempo and onset rate) — [Barchet et al. 2024](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3565372_1/component/file_3565373/kog-24-rim-01-tenseMusic.pdf)
- Farbood's 2012 model, on which this builds, was validated on ten pieces with hand-extracted features and no timbre term — [Barchet et al. 2024](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3565372_1/component/file_3565373/kog-24-rim-01-tenseMusic.pdf)
- Listener-rated tension rises with time through a build-up and tracks arousal (+0.92) but goes against valence (−0.69) — [Turrell et al. 2025](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/106400/2/Building%20the%20anticipation_Javadi_AAM.pdf)
- Expectation of closure is a measurable profile: 40 listeners continuously rated "this will end in the next 1 to 2 seconds" on 40 Mozart cadence excerpts. Ratings rose toward the cadence in every category, steepest for perfect authentic cadences (linear trend B = 7.37 for musicians, 4.73 for non-musicians). Peak expectation came about 130 ms before the perfect cadence's arrival (290 ms before, for musicians) and about 340 ms after a half cadence. A cadential six-four, greater event density and longer excerpt predicted higher and earlier peaks (R² = 0.36 to 0.58) — [Sears et al. 2020, Psychology of Music](https://www.music.mcgill.ca/~caplin/sears_2020_expecting-the.pdf)

### Inferences
- For symbolic input, three of TenseMusic's six features survive: pitch height, onset rate/tempo, and tonal tension. The two strongest (loudness, roughness) are lost. A notes-only tension curve should therefore be expected to correlate well under 0.6 with what listeners would rate; the earlier report's rho of 0.68 to 0.75 came from chord-only stimuli with weights fitted to the test set.
- The window sizes are directly usable: smooth pitch-height over about 5 s and harmonic tension over about 7 s, with harmonic tension remembered for about 12 s. At 100 to 120 BPM that is roughly 2 bars and 3 bars of smoothing, and about 5 to 6 bars of memory (my arithmetic).
- Sears 2020 gives a concrete, score-computable "closure expectation" reading: the approach to a cadence should raise predicted ending-likelihood, peaking at or just before the final chord. The earlier report already cites the model-side counterpart (the cadential arrival is the most predictable event of its phrase).
- No paper found tests "tension profile shape → liking". The nearest evidence is Miles 2017 (verse-to-chorus surprise drop, earlier report) and Turrell's tension/valence dissociation.

### Gaps
- Herremans & Chew's original paper offers the three measures (cloud diameter, cloud momentum, tensile strain) without a large listener validation; the only independent listener tests found are TenseMusic (weak contribution) and Navarro-Cáceres 2020 (earlier report). Their original TENOR/JNMR paper was not re-read in this pass.
- Lerdahl & Krumhansl 2007 was not re-read; it is covered in the earlier report.
- Whether a tension curve's shape (single arch, sawtooth, late peak) predicts preference is, as far as this search found, untested.

## 6. Groove and syncopation: numbers usable for a rhythm reading

### Takeaway
The inverted U for syncopation is real for wanting to move in looped drum patterns (quadratic R² about 0.35 to 0.43 on pattern means) and has been re-created with reduced stimulus sets and in melodies, but for pleasantness the newest test found a linear decline with no significant curve, and in realistic full-texture music syncopation's effect is small (0.08 points on a 5-point scale).

### Cited Findings
- Witek et al. 2014 (origin study): 66 listeners, 50 drum breaks (34 transcribed funk, 2 templates, 14 composed), syncopation index adapted from Longuet-Higgins & Lee with instrument weights, range 0 to 81. Quadratic regression on mean ratings: wanting to move R² = 0.347 (β = −0.589), pleasure R² = 0.427 (β = −0.653). Quadratic beat linear for syncopation (adjusted R² difference 0.09, p < 0.001). Audio entropy added nothing beyond syncopation — [Witek et al. 2014, PLoS ONE](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/d9d000f1-421f-4984-8e28-d5dc02f2762e/PubMedCentral/d9d000f1-421f-4984-8e28-d5dc02f2762e.pdf)
- Céspedes-Guevara & Witek 2023 (N = 76; six drum breaks from the 2014 set, two each at index ≈ 4 (low), ≈ 38.5 (medium), ≈ 60 (high); two-bar phrases looped eight times at 120 BPM). Pleasantness: linear effect negative, t = −2.22, p = 0.027; quadratic not significant, p = 0.145; medium beat high (p = 0.047) but low and medium did not differ (p = 0.987). Difficulty staying still: significant inverted U (quadratic β = −0.659, p < 0.001; linear β = −0.305, p = 0.021). Actually moving (tapping) did not change pleasantness — [Céspedes-Guevara & Witek 2023, Psychology of Music](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/189045021/Rhythm_syncopation_movement_and_pleasantness.pdf)
- Stupacher, Wrede & Vuust 2022 reduced the standard set from 54 rhythms to nine and still reproduced the inverted U between rhythmic complexity and groove — [Stupacher et al. 2022, PLOS ONE, abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0266902.html)
- Zalta et al. 2024 (Science Advances; 111 participants across online, MEG and tapping; 12 original melodies each altered to vary syncopation): wanting to move peaked at medium syncopation, and syncopation level tracked neural activity at 2 Hz in a left sensorimotor network — [secondary summary, PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/the-neuroscience-of-groove-why-certain-rhythms-make-us-want-to-dance)
- Duncan & Orgs 2024 (N = 179; 20 fifteen-second house clips at 126 BPM with drums, bass and piano; syncopation manipulated in the bassline only and verified with six SynPy measures). Urge to move: syncopation main effect ηp² = 0.063, mean difference 0.08 on a 5-point scale; low-frequency amplitude main effect ηp² = 0.192, mean difference 0.216; interaction ηp² = 0.024. With bass frequencies cut, syncopation had no detectable effect (difference 0.04, p = 0.604) — [Duncan & Orgs 2024, Music Perception](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10204599/1/Move%20your%20Body.pdf)
- Catchiness and urge to move are distinct: recognisability predicted catchiness (B = 0.291, R²m = 0.094) far more than urge to move (B = 0.049, R²m = 0.002) — [Bechtold et al. 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1642561/full)

### Inferences
- A usable calibration: on Witek's 0 to 81 index, "medium" is about 38 and "high" about 60, i.e. the sweet spot for wanting to move is near the middle of the index's range and pleasantness is flat from 4 to 38 and falls by 60. So for pleasantness the rule is "do not exceed medium", not "aim for medium". This matches the earlier report's reading of Senn 2018 (real repertoire sits at or below the peak).
- Wanting to move and pleasure have dissociated in the newest test (curve for one, decline for the other). If the judge's target is liking, a one-sided penalty above a syncopation ceiling is better supported than a two-sided target.
- The Zalta study is the only one using melodies; it supports applying a syncopation reading to the melody line itself, but it was read only through a news summary and gives no usable optimum value.
- Syncopation matters most when carried by the bass (Duncan & Orgs). In a melody-plus-chords judge without a bass line, the rhythm reading has less to act on than the groove literature implies.

### Gaps
- The vertex of Witek's 2014 curve is in a supplement not retrieved; only the category values (≈ 4, 38.5, 60) are available.
- Zalta 2024's stimulus levels and effect sizes were not retrieved from the primary paper (PMC captcha, HAL blocked).
- No study found tests melodic (vocal-line) syncopation against liking of whole songs; this gap was already noted in the earlier report and still stands.

## 7. Melodic contour, arch, balance and symmetry, and phrase-final lengthening as preference predictors

### Takeaway
There is one direct experimental test of shape properties on liking for melodies: balance (events evenly spread with a central climax) and melodic complexity raise liking modestly (r² 0.06 to 0.07 each), palindromic symmetry lowers it, and smooth-versus-jagged contour has no reliable group effect; individual listeners differ so much that some slopes reverse sign. Phrase-final lengthening has perceptual and production evidence only, with no preference test found.

### Cited Findings
- Clemente, Pearce, Skov & Nadal (melodies arm): 96 four-second monophonic C-major melodies from the MUST set (92 analysed), built to be extreme on balance, contour, symmetry or complexity. Group effects on liking: balance β = −0.246 on an imbalance score (so more balanced is more liked), d = −0.25 [−0.37, −0.12], r² = 0.06; melodic complexity (event density plus pitch entropy) β = 0.333, d = 0.33 [0.19, 0.48], r² = 0.07; asymmetry β = 0.196, d = 0.20 [0.07, 0.33], r² = 0.04 (palindromic melodies were liked less) — [Clemente et al., Brain and Cognition](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/70303251/ana_clemente_et_al_evaluative_judgement_across_domains_publishersversion.pdf)
- Not significant at group level: melodic contour jaggedness (wider, more varied intervals) β = 0.144, p = 0.077; rhythmic contour β = −0.115, p = 0.274; rhythmic complexity (duration entropy) β = −0.078, p = 0.140 — [Clemente et al.](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/70303251/ana_clemente_et_al_evaluative_judgement_across_domains_publishersversion.pdf)
- Definitions used: balanced = homogeneous event distribution with a central climax, unbalanced = events piled at either end; symmetric = second half is a literal retrograde of the first; complexity = number and variety of notes, with melodic complexity combining event density and pitch entropy — [Clemente et al.](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/70303251/ana_clemente_et_al_evaluative_judgement_across_domains_publishersversion.pdf)
- Individual slopes ranged across zero for the main effects: balance −0.769 to +0.029; melodic contour −0.598 to +0.730; melodic complexity −0.709 to +0.775 — [Clemente et al.](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/70303251/ana_clemente_et_al_evaluative_judgement_across_domains_publishersversion.pdf)
- Phrase-final lengthening: when listeners advance through chord sequences at their own pace, they dwell longer on phrase-boundary chords than on other chords, in Bach chorales and in atonal versions; with metrical and contour cues controlled, the effect was larger for tonal than atonal versions, and held for non-musicians — [Kragness & Trainor 2016, abstract](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/articlereference.2016-10-28.9242990309)

### Inferences
- "Balance" as defined (even spread of events, climax in the middle) is the nearest experimental support for the melodic arch as a preference predictor, and it is computable: e.g. the centre of mass of note onsets and of pitch height along the phrase, and the position of the highest note. Effect is small (r² = 0.06) and from four-second melodies.
- The positive complexity effect here (β = 0.333) looks opposite to the IC decline in section 1, but the stimuli are four-second tonal fragments in C major where "simple" means very few notes. Read together: liking rises with note density and pitch variety from a very sparse floor, and falls with statistical surprise. These are separable readings (density/variety versus corpus surprise) and both can be kept.
- The symmetry result is a useful negative: exact retrograde structure, a favourite of mathematical melody generators, is mildly disliked.
- Phrase-final lengthening is something listeners themselves produce and expect, so its absence is plausibly a well-formedness fault, but there is no evidence it raises liking. It belongs with the gates, as the earlier report placed arch and post-skip reversal.

### Gaps
- The participant N for the Clemente melodies arm was not captured in this pass.
- No study found that manipulates arch contour or final-note lengthening in pop-length phrases and measures liking.
- Kragness & Trainor's N and effect sizes were not retrieved (abstract only).

## 8. Prediction-error resolution timing: how soon should a surprise be resolved?

### Takeaway
No study was found that varies the delay between a surprising event and its resolution and measures pleasure; this is a genuine hole in the literature. What exists is indirect: listeners like melodies more when the ending confirms the learned pattern, anticipation of closure peaks at or just before the cadence, and rated tension starts to fall about 2 to 3 seconds after a release point.

### Cited Findings
- Endings that confirm expectation are liked more than altered endings, in every one of seven behavioural studies (β = 0.05 to 0.10), including when the altered ending had itself been heard during exposure (β = 0.05, p = 0.02), which the authors read as schematic (style-level) expectation and not just memory for the specific tune — [Kathios et al.](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.17.496615.full.pdf)
- Listeners' expectation that a phrase is about to end rises continuously toward a cadence and peaks about 130 ms before a perfect authentic cadence's arrival (290 ms before, for musicians), but about 340 ms after a half cadence; deceptive and evaded cadences produced a slower, later rise in musicians (B = 1.30 and 2.21 against 7.37 for perfect cadences) — [Sears et al. 2020](https://www.music.mcgill.ca/~caplin/sears_2020_expecting-the.pdf)
- After a build-up, rated tension peaks at the release point and its decline begins roughly 2 to 3 seconds later (authors' description, not statistically tested) — [Turrell et al. 2025](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/106400/2/Building%20the%20anticipation_Javadi_AAM.pdf)
- Tension-model integration windows give the time scale over which a harmonic event keeps influencing felt tension: about 7 s of attention and 12 s of memory for tonal tension, against 3 s for loudness — [Barchet et al. 2024](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3565372_1/component/file_3565373/kog-24-rim-01-tenseMusic.pdf)
- In Gold 2019, low surprise was liked most specifically when the preceding uncertainty was high (low IC under high entropy beat low IC under low entropy, p = 0.001), i.e. a predictable event is most rewarding when it resolves an uncertain context — [Gold et al. 2019](https://core.ac.uk/download/237088229.pdf)

### Inferences
- A computable stand-in for "resolution" is available without any timing data: for each high-entropy position, check whether the note that follows has low IC (uncertainty resolved by an expected event). Gold's cluster result supports rewarding "high entropy then low IC" pairs. The count or proportion of such pairs per phrase is a candidate reading; its support is one marginal analysis.
- Phrase endings should be the most predictable point of the phrase (Kathios endings result, Sears closure expectation, and Sears' modelling result cited in the earlier report). A reading that compares IC of the final one or two notes of each phrase against the phrase mean, and requires it to be lower, has the best support in this section.
- A time-scale guess, flagged as a guess: harmonic tension is felt over about 7 s and tension starts falling 2 to 3 s after release, so an unresolved harmonic surprise that is still unresolved after roughly 2 to 3 bars at pop tempo has probably stopped being heard as "awaiting resolution". No experiment tests this.

### Gaps
- No experiment found, 2015 to 2026, that manipulates resolution delay (e.g. a suspension held for 1, 2 or 4 beats; a deceptive cadence resolved after 1, 2 or 4 bars) with pleasure or liking as the outcome.
- Egermann et al. 2013 (live-concert physiological responses to high-IC melodic events) could not be re-read; the PDF redirected to a host that was not fetched.
- Huron's contrastive-valence account (pleasure from a negative prediction response followed by positive appraisal) remains theoretical for timing; no quantitative test of its time constants was located.
