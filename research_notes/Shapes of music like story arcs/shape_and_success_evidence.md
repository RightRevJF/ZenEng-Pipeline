# Empirical evidence linking a song's time-course shape to success or liking

Scope note: these notes cover data that link song shape (over time) to outcomes such as charts, streams, ratings, skips and physiological peak response. The theory of tension and contour is covered by another researcher. I worked from primary papers where I could reach them. Where I only had an abstract, a fetched summary or press coverage, I say so. My date-of-check is November 2026. Items I know of from background knowledge but did not verify in this session are listed under Gaps and must not be cited as verified.

---

## Key Question 1: Has anyone found a small set of song shapes from data, the way Reagan et al. did for stories? If so, which shapes, and do they relate to success?

### Takeaway
No one has published a Reagan-style typology of whole-song audio shapes (energy, loudness or valence arcs) that is linked to chart or streaming success. The nearest analogues are: (a) one lyric-sentiment study (UK drill, 550 songs) that clustered 100-point sentiment trajectories into k = 2 groups, where the more positive cluster had about 1.9x the YouTube views; (b) a 2024 lyric-narrative study that finds one average arc (staging early, cognitive tension peaking mid-song) but reports no popularity link in its abstract; (c) descriptive averages of within-song valence/arousal, and album-level U-shapes, with no success outcome. The building block for any future typology exists, though: Spotify skip curves are song-specific, stable across cohorts, and jump right after section boundaries.

### Cited Findings

**Reagan et al. reference point (stories, not songs)**
- Reagan et al. (2016) ran sentiment analysis on 1,327 Project Gutenberg books and found six basic arc shapes. "Man in a hole" made up about 30% of arcs, and arc type correlated significantly with downloads (as summarized by Neto et al. 2025). — [Neto et al., PLOS ONE 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0316963)

**Lyric-sentiment trajectories clustered and linked to popularity (closest Reagan analogue)**
- Kleinberg & McFarlane (UCL), "Examining UK drill music through sentiment trajectory analysis" (arXiv 1911.01324). Corpus: 550 songs by 105 London drill artists. Lyrics came from Genius, and view, like and comment counts from the YouTube API. — [arXiv 1911.01324](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01324)
- Method: lexicon sentiment (standard lexicon of 11,710 terms, plus the SlangSD slang lexicon of 48,277 terms) with a ±3-word valence-shifter window. Each song's sentiment was transformed by discrete cosine transform to a 100-point "narrative time" (1% steps), which is the same approach as Reagan-style syuzhet arcs. — [arXiv 1911.01324](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01324)
- k-means with k = 2 (chosen by elbow and silhouette). The standard lexicon gave a "positive" cluster (53%, n = 289) and a "negative" cluster (47%, n = 261), described as roughly mirror images. The slang lexicon gave two negative clusters (70% and 30%). The two schemes were not associated (χ²(1) = 2.05, p = .152). — [arXiv 1911.01324](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01324)
- Outcome: in a negative binomial regression, positive-cluster songs had about 1.89x the views per 100 days (mean 236,707 vs 140,090, with very large SDs of 592k and 315k) and about 2.01x the engagement. Slang clusters had no significant effect. The authors say the result needs corroboration. — [arXiv 1911.01324](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01324)

**Lyric narrative arc (one average shape, no clustering)**
- Alberhasky & Durkee, "Songs tell a story: The Arc of narrative for music," PLOS ONE 19(5): e0303188 (2024). Two lyric datasets: N = 12,280 and a "greatest hits" set of N = 2,823. Lyrics generally set the stage early, progress the plot steadily, and peak in cognitive tension mid-song. Country and Rap set the scene in more detail, while Pop moves the plot faster and has more internal conflict. **I read only the abstract.** It reports no analysis linking arc to popularity. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/b8e7f4e79e52458589979532040b2095)
- Neto et al. summarize the same paper as finding that staging and cognitive tension tend to decline across a song, with cognitive tension peaking midway. — [Neto et al. 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0316963)

**Audio valence/arousal trajectories within songs (descriptive, no success outcome)**
- Lyu (Communication University of China), arXiv 2609.15675 (2026), "Sectional Structure and Emotional Dynamics in Chinese Pop Songs." Data: 100 songs by top QQ Music artists, 1,046 hand-annotated sections, and frame-level (0.5 s) valence/arousal estimated by models trained on PMEmo, interpolated to 101 time points. Main pattern: "local cycles–global accumulation," meaning local cycling, later-stage accumulation, a **delayed climax** and release-based closure. **No clustering into shape types, and no popularity link.** Chart rank was used only for sampling. VA values are model estimates, and sections were labelled by a single annotator. — [arXiv 2609.15675](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15675)

**Album-level arcs (adjacent evidence)**
- Neto, Hartmann, Luck & Toiviainen, "An album is a story: Feature arcs in sequences of tracks," PLOS ONE (July 2025). An earlier study by the same group covered about 51,000 published albums. In this experiment, 130 musicians and producers sequenced five-track instrumental sets. Loudness, valence and arousal follow a **U-shape** across positions, and tempo follows an inverted U. Openers and closers have the highest valence and loudness. The authors liken this to "Man in a hole" but did not cluster arcs. Effects were significant but small: a quadratic fit improved on linear only modestly (RSS 2534.2 vs 2558.2). Liking was not measured. — [PLOS ONE 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0316963)
- A related arXiv paper, "The algorithmic nature of song-sequencing: statistical regularities in music albums" (2408.04383), turned up in search. **Not read.** — [arXiv 2408.04383](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.04383)

**Behavioural "shape" data at scale: skip curves**
- Montecchio, Roy & Pachet, "The skipping behavior of users of music streaming services and its relation to musical structure," PLOS ONE (2020), arXiv 1903.06008. Spotify data: over 3 billion skip events on 100 popular songs across three months; the top-100 songs as of April 2018 with skip profiles from about 1 billion streams; and an SP20k set of about 20,000 songs totaling about 81 billion streams. — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.06008)
- Each song has its own skip-time distribution, consistent across user cohorts and stable over dates under stationary conditions. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.06008)
- "Listeners are more likely to skip a song right after a change between musical sections." The skip surge lags the boundary by about 3.5 s. A network trained only on skip profiles predicted structural boundaries with weighted F = 0.630 at a ±3 s window. Endings with silence or long fadeouts are skipped often. Changes in a song's skip profile tracked changes in its stream counts, largest in the first two weeks after release. **The paper does not test whether skip-profile shape predicts popularity.** Profiles were normalized for popularity. — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.06008)

### Inferences
- The field has a strong behavioural signal of shape (song-specific skip curves that react to section changes) but no published typology of song shapes tied to success. A Reagan-style clustering of Spotify/Essentia section-level energy or valence curves, run against stream counts, appears to be an **open niche**. This is my inference from what I found, not a confirmed absence.
- The drill result is about cluster *level* (positive vs negative overall), not arc *shape*. The clusters are near mirror images, so it is closer to "happier lyrics get more views" than to "a rising arc wins." It also comes from one genre with very noisy view counts.
- The convergent descriptive pattern is a **delayed or late climax with local cycling**. It appears in Chinese pop valence/arousal (Lyu 2026), in mid-song tension peaks in lyrics (Alberhasky & Durkee 2024), and in EDM build-and-drop. This describes how songs *are* made, not proof that the shape *causes* success.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that clusters whole-song audio energy/loudness/valence curves (Spotify audio-analysis sections or segments, or Essentia) into k shapes and relates them to charts or streams. Searches for "emotional arcs of songs," "song arcs," "shape of a song popularity," and "musical narrative arc clustering" turned up nothing beyond the items above.
- Did Alberhasky & Durkee test arc fit against popularity in the "greatest hits" set? Only the abstract was read.
- A 2024 Anthology of Computers and the Humanities paper, "Measuring stories in contemporary songs," surfaced in search but was **not read**. It may contain lyric-arc data. — [ACH anthology](https://anthology.ach.org/volumes/vol0003/measuring-stories-in-contemporary-songs/)
- Brand, Acerbi & Mesoudi, "Cultural evolution of emotional expression in 50 years of song lyrics," surfaced in search but was **not read**. It is about trends over decades, not within-song arcs. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10427273/)

## Key Question 2: How much of success does shape explain, compared with typicality, artist fame, marketing and timbre? What effect sizes are reported?

### Takeaway
Content-only models predict hits modestly, with typical AUCs of about 0.65 to 0.8 and best "accuracy" around 75% on balanced sets. Almost none of these models use *time-course* features; they use whole-song averages or statistics. Typicality has a real but small effect: it bends chart odds in an inverted U, and one SD of typicality is worth about half a week on the chart. Artist history, label and release year carry large shares of predictive power. Intros have shrunk sharply over 30 years, but a Spotify follow-up by the same author found no stream advantage for songs that grab attention sooner. In lab work, remembered intensity tracks the *average* moment more than the peak and end, and drop sections do produce the biggest physiological responses. Overall, shape's share of the variance in success has **not been estimated by anyone**. Every piece of evidence suggests it is small next to exposure and fame.

### Cited Findings

**Hit-song science and critiques (static features)**
- Pachet & Roy (2008, ISMIR), "Hit song science is not yet a science": could not build an accurate classifier of low, medium or high popularity from acoustic or human-labelled features, and concluded acoustic features are "not informative enough." They also suspected spurious data in Dhanaraj & Logan (2005). (Summarized by Herremans et al.; **primary not read this session**.) — [Herremans et al., arXiv 1905.08076](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.08076)
- Ni et al. (2011), "Hit song science once again a science?": a shifting perceptron on Echo Nest features, top-5 vs positions 30–40 of the UK singles chart, more optimistic results. Herremans et al. criticize it for thin methodological detail. (**Primary not read; no numbers verified.**) — [Herremans et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.08076)
- Herremans, Martens & Sörensen, "Dance hit song prediction," JNMR 2014 (arXiv 1905.08076). Data: UK Official Charts dance top 40 (Oct 2009 – Mar 2013, 697 songs matched to Echo Nest) and Billboard dance top 10 (1985–2013, 2,755 matched). The "temporal" features are *not* arcs: they are statistics (mean, variance, skew, kurtosis, percentiles, range) of 12 timbre vectors and beat-interval differences over the whole track, 139 features in all. Results for top-10 vs 30–40: logistic regression AUC 0.65 in 10-fold CV, and **AUC 0.81 and 80% accuracy on an out-of-time test** (83% of hits but only 32% of non-hits classified correctly). Brightness (timbre 1), timbre 3 and beat-difference range were the most informative. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.08076)
- Zangerle et al. (ISMIR 2019), "Hit song prediction: leveraging low- and high-level audio features." Data: Million Song Dataset, balanced 5,832 Hot 100 hits vs 5,832 non-hits (1954–2018), using Essentia features from 30 s previews. A wide-and-deep network reached 75.04% accuracy vs 73.91% for the best linear model. **Release year was the most important high-level feature (+12–13%).** No structural or time-course features were used, and artist history was explicitly excluded. — [ISMIR 2019 PDF](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2019/paper/000037.pdf)
- Interiano et al. (2018), "Musical trends and predictability of success in contemporary songs in and out of the top charts," Royal Society Open Science. **Full text blocked in this session.** The UCI press release headline is "upbeat songs by female singers dominate the charts." From memory, but unverified: about 500k UK songs from 1985–2015, a random-forest AUC around 0.86, and a "superstar" variable that matters a lot. — [UCI News](https://news.uci.edu/2018/05/15/cmon-get-happy-upbeat-songs-by-female-singers-dominate-the-charts-uci-study-finds); [PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5990848)

**Typicality (Askin & Mauskapf 2017, American Sociological Review; full PDF read)**
- Data: 26,846 Billboard Hot 100 songs (Aug 1958 – Mar 2016), 25,102 with Echo Nest features. Eleven features were used (loudness was dropped as a mastering artifact). Typicality is the genre-weighted cosine similarity to songs charting in the prior 52 weeks, mean .81, SD .06. — [Askin & Mauskapf PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/culture/2017-askin.pdf)
- Inverted U ("optimal differentiation"): moderately typical songs have the highest probability of reaching #1, the top 5, 10 and 20. Ordered-logit coefficients: linear 7.672, squared −6.805. — [PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/culture/2017-askin.pdf)
- Effect sizes are small. A song 1 SD below mean typicality lasts about half a week longer (11.5 vs 11 weeks). In the weekly fixed-effects model, +1 SD typicality means about 0.6 more chart positions lost per week. OLS R² was .047 for peak position and .171 for weeks on chart; the FE model R² was .432. No variance decomposition by source. — [PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/culture/2017-askin.pdf)
- Controls that matter: prior chart count (a sophomore slump; superstars with 10+ hits peak higher but run shorter), major label (higher peaks, longer runs), crossover status, and genre and era dummies. **No time-varying within-song features.** — [PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/culture/2017-askin.pdf)

**Melodic and earworm content (shape of melody, not of the song)**
- Jakubowski, Finkel, Stewart & Müllensiefen (2017), "Dissecting an earworm," Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts 11(2). Data: 3,000 respondents; 100 earworm tunes matched to 100 non-earworms on chart entry, weeks on chart, recency, artist and genre. Earworms have a **common global melodic contour** (relative to a pop corpus) but **unusual intervals** (larger leaps or atypical gradients), and are somewhat faster (124 vs 116 bpm, p = .10). Leave-one-out accuracy was 62.5%. Chart popularity and recency predicted *how often* a song was named. Adding melodic features improved fit significantly (χ²(2) = 55.52, p < .001). — [APA PDF](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/aca-aca0000090.pdf)

**Corpus evolution (no success model)**
- Mauch, MacCallum, Levy & Leroi (2015), Royal Society Open Science. Data: about 17,000 Hot 100 songs (1960–2010), 30 s segments, with 8 harmonic and 8 timbral LDA topics. Revolutions around 1964, 1983 and 1991 (the largest), and no progressive homogenization. **It does not model features against chart success.** The sample is hits only, and the Beatles/Stones "ahead of the curve" point is an interpretation, not a test. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1502.05417)

**Time-course features: intros and time to hook**
- Léveillé Gauvin, "Drawing listener attention in popular music," Musicae Scientiae (online 2017, DOI 10.1177/1029864917698010). Data: US top-10 singles 1986–2015, with tempo measured for 303 songs. Average intro fell from more than 20 s (mid-1980s) to about 5 s, a 78% drop. Once lyrics begin, the title arrives about 18% sooner. Tempo rose about 8%, titles got shorter, and self-focus did not change. **In a Spotify follow-up comparing the most- and least-streamed songs by the same artists, she found no support for the attention-economy hypothesis**, meaning shorter time-to-hook did not predict more streams within an artist. (Read via the phys.org press summary.) — [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2017-04-music-streaming-instrumental-intro.pdf)

**Time-course features: repetition**
- Nunes, Ordanini & Valsesia (2015, Journal of Consumer Psychology), "The power of repetition": repetitive lyrics raise processing fluency and are associated with market success. **I could not retrieve the primary or the NPR article** (robots-blocked). The numbers I recall (about 2,480 Hot 100 songs from 1958–2012, with more repetitive songs more likely to reach #1 and climbing faster) are **unverified**. — [Bocconi IRIS record](https://iris.unibocconi.it/handle/11565/3955321); [NPR (not fetched)](https://www.npr.org/2015/01/11/376496332/the-more-the-better-repetition-propels-songs-up-the-charts)
- Margulis, "Aesthetic responses to repetition in unfamiliar music" (Empirical Studies of the Arts, 2013/2014). **I saw only the record page.** From memory, unverified: adding literal repetition to excerpts by Berio and Carter made them more enjoyable, more interesting and more likely to be judged composed by a human. — [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/aesthetic-responses-to-repetition-in-unfamiliar-music/)

**Time-course in the lab: peak-end and remembered affect**
- Rozin, Rozin & Goldberg (2004), Music Perception 22(1). Remembered intensity correlated with peak (r = .82), peak-end (r = .81) and average (r = .80). Peak-end was the best predictor for only 3 of 20 participants, so the authors called the peak-end rule unsupported. They found a **"slope effect"**: large moment-to-moment increases predicted higher remembered intensity. (Via Schäfer et al.'s summary.) — [Schäfer et al. 2014, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00911/full)
- Schäfer, Zimmermann & Sedlmeier (2014), Frontiers in Psychology 5:911. Design: 54 students, 11 unfamiliar full songs rated continuously (0–100 felt intensity), 507 ratings, analyzed with HLM. Average intensity was the dominant predictor of remembered intensity (r = .805, β ≈ .58). Peak-end was smaller but significant (r = .784, β ≈ .25). R² ≈ .70. Remembered intensity correlated with liking at r = .63. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00911/full)

**Time-course in the lab: EDM build-and-drop**
- Solberg & Dibben (2019), "Peak experiences with electronic dance music," Music Perception 36(4): 371–389. Design: 24 participants; four EDM excerpts with a break routine (breakdown, build-up, drop) and one without. **Drop sections produced significantly higher skin-conductance responses than other sections.** Participants reported intense, energizing, embodied experiences, attributed mainly to the drop. — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/145911/)

**Streaming behaviour tied to time-course**
- Lamere (Music Machinery, 2 May 2014), based on several billion Spotify plays. Share of plays skipped within 5 s: 24.14%; 10 s: 28.97%; 30 s: 35.05%; before the end: 48.6%. Desktop 40.1% vs mobile 51.1%. Teens skip most (over 50%). Artist radio about 55% vs listener-chosen album about 46%. The skip curve drops steeply in the first ~20 s, then declines slowly to about 50% at the end. Caveats: a "skip" includes searches and taps, and free-tier limits apply. **Blog post, not peer-reviewed.** — [Music Machinery](https://musicmachinery.com/2014/05/02/the-skip/)
- Montecchio et al. (2020) confirm about a quarter of streams skipped in the first 5 s and only about half played to the end, at much larger scale. They also find skip surges about 3.5 s after section boundaries. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.06008)

### Inferences
- **Order-of-magnitude ranking of what predicts success, from what I read:** exposure, fame and era (release year +12–13% in Zangerle; superstar, label and prior-hit effects in Askin & Mauskapf) > static timbre and mood features (AUC about 0.65–0.81) ≈ typicality (statistically robust but worth about half a week on the chart) > within-song time-course features. For the last, the only direct within-artist streaming test I found (Léveillé Gauvin's Spotify follow-up) was **null**. Shape's explained variance has never been isolated. Treat any claim that shape "drives" hits as unsupported.
- The evidence is strongest at the *moment-to-moment* level rather than at the level of the whole song. Listeners leave in the first 5–20 s and right after section changes. Drops spike arousal. Remembered intensity tracks the average and rises with steep increases. This supports shape as a driver of **retention and felt intensity**, which plausibly feeds streams (completion counts toward Spotify's 30 s royalty threshold). The link from shape to chart rank has not been shown.
- Most "temporal" features in hit-song science are whole-track statistics (variance and range of timbre), which throw away order. A model given section-ordered curves has not, to my knowledge, been benchmarked against the static ones.

### Gaps
- Interiano et al. 2018: full numbers (sample size, AUC, superstar effect size) were not verified because the PMC page was bot-blocked.
- Nunes et al. 2015 effect sizes (and Nunes & Ordanini 2014 on instrumentation in #1 hits) were not verified.
- Classic structural-intervention experiments that I know from background but did **not verify** in this session: Karno & Konečni (1992), who rearranged Mozart's Symphony No. 40, first movement; Tillmann & Bigand (1996), who reversed segments with little effect on perceived expressiveness; and Gotlieb & Konečni (1985) on Goldberg Variations order. Background recollection is that reordering sections changes liking little in short-term ratings. Verify before citing.
- Salganik, Dodds & Watts (2006, Science), "MusicLab" social-influence unpredictability, is relevant to the "fame vs content" question but **not verified here**.
- Berger & Packard (2018, Psychological Science) on lyrical atypicality and popularity: **not verified**.
- Spotify's Sequential Skip Prediction Challenge (WSDM Cup 2019) dataset and its findings: **not checked**.
- Yadati et al. (ISMIR 2014), drop detection from SoundCloud timed comments: **not checked**. It may give crowd-level evidence that drops attract engagement.
- Van Balen et al. (ISMIR 2013), "An analysis of chorus features in popular song," surfaced in search but was **not read**. It compares choruses with other sections; unclear whether it relates them to success. — [ISMIR 2013](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000180.pdf)
- "On-chart success dynamics of popular songs" (arXiv 1704.08437) concerns trajectories on the chart, not in the music. **Not read.** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.08437)
- No study found that tested "each chorus louder than the last," peak position (e.g. last third vs middle), or time-to-first-chorus against streams with controls for artist.

## Key Question 3: Which findings could serve as targets for a songwriting engine?

### Takeaway
The defensible targets are about *retention and felt intensity*, not proven chart drivers. They are: hook the listener in the first ~5–20 s; make every section boundary earn its place, since skips surge about 3.5 s after changes; avoid silent or long-fade endings; keep overall intensity high on average, with steep rises and a late peak or drop; stay moderately typical in sound for the era; and use repetition generously. "Peak in the last third" and "each chorus louder than the last" fit descriptive corpora and lab physiology, but **no outcome study has validated them**.

### Cited Findings
- **Early hook / short intro.** About 24% of plays are skipped in 5 s, about 35% by 30 s, and most skips happen in the first ~20 s. — [Lamere 2014](https://musicmachinery.com/2014/05/02/the-skip/); [Montecchio et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.06008). Top-10 intros averaged about 5 s by 2015, down from more than 20 s in the mid-1980s. — [phys.org on Léveillé Gauvin](https://phys.org/news/2017-04-music-streaming-instrumental-intro.pdf). Counter-evidence: within the same artist, shorter time-to-hook did not predict more Spotify streams. — [same source](https://phys.org/news/2017-04-music-streaming-instrumental-intro.pdf)
- **Section boundaries are risk points.** Skips rise right after changes between sections, with about a 3.5 s lag. — [Montecchio et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.06008)
- **Endings.** Songs with silent or long-fade endings get many end-skips. — [Montecchio et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.06008)
- **Average intensity, plus steep rises.** Remembered intensity is driven mainly by average intensity (β ≈ .58), with a smaller peak-end contribution (β ≈ .25). Remembered intensity correlates with liking at r = .63. — [Schäfer et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00911/full). Large moment-to-moment increases predicted higher remembered intensity (Rozin et al. 2004). — [via Schäfer et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00911/full)
- **Build-and-drop.** Drops give the largest skin-conductance responses among EDM sections. — [Solberg & Dibben 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/145911/)
- **Late climax with local cycling.** Descriptive only, from 100 Chinese pop songs. — [Lyu, arXiv 2609.15675](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15675). Lyrics peak in cognitive tension mid-song. — [Alberhasky & Durkee 2024](https://doaj.org/article/b8e7f4e79e52458589979532040b2095)
- **Moderate typicality.** Inverted-U odds of reaching #1 and the top 10, with small effect sizes. — [Askin & Mauskapf 2017](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/culture/2017-askin.pdf)
- **Melody.** A conventional overall contour with some unusual leaps is associated with earworm status (62.5% accuracy). — [Jakubowski et al. 2017](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/aca-aca0000090.pdf)
- **Repetition.** Repetitive lyrics are associated with chart success (Nunes et al. 2015; **numbers unverified**). — [Bocconi record](https://iris.unibocconi.it/handle/11565/3955321)
- **Lyric valence.** More positive lyric-sentiment trajectories had about 1.9x the views (UK drill only). — [Kleinberg & McFarlane](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01324)

### Inferences
Suggested engine targets, graded by evidence strength:
- **A (behavioural data at scale):** first vocal or hook within about 5–10 s; no dead air at the start or end; give each section transition a fresh event (new layer, vocal entry, hook) to offset the post-boundary skip bump. Evidence: Lamere; Montecchio et al.
- **B (lab physiology or recall):** keep mean energy high rather than relying on one big peak; build with steep rises; place a peak or drop after a build-up. Evidence: Schäfer et al.; Rozin et al.; Solberg & Dibben.
- **C (descriptive corpora only):** a climax in the later part of the song (last third or final chorus) with earlier local cycles; escalate successive choruses. These are consistent with Lyu 2026 and with EDM practice, but **untested against outcomes**.
- **Context, not shape:** moderate typicality; generous repetition; a conventional contour with distinctive intervals.
- If the engine scores "success," it should report shape as a minor factor and should not promise chart effects. Fame, exposure and era dominate wherever they are measured.

### Gaps
- No outcome-validated threshold exists for "first chorus by X seconds." I found no peer-reviewed number linking time-to-first-chorus to streams with artist controls. Industry claims such as "a 30-second rule" (sites like dynamoi or freebeat) are tied to Spotify's 30 s stream-count threshold, not to evidence about shape.
- No study tested "each chorus louder than the last" or peak position against outcomes.
- A direct test would need: section-level loudness or energy curves (e.g. Spotify audio-analysis sections or segments, now deprecated for new API apps per background knowledge, **unverified**; or Essentia) for many songs, clustered into shapes, and regressed on streams with artist fixed effects. This has not been done, as far as I found.
