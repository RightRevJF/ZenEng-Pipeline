## What the song is about

Two married, middle-aged people fall into something that changes them. They meet in her car on the top deck of a parking garage at a furniture company, black vinyl pressed over every window, twenty years old again at noon. They drive past the county line to a door with no name and pay in dollars for the dark, and laugh like kids in the back row. He goes home and writes her fourteen lines about her laugh.

Her husband reads them. He doesn't shout. He plugs her watch back in by her side of the bed, and that Sunday he shakes the other man's hand at the door and says he liked the words. And then both of them choose the life they already know: the wife in the second row, the coffee poured before sunrise, a car with clear glass. The last chorus is the same tune as the first, and nothing in the song lifts: no key change, no duet, no resolution. He still fogs the window on her street. He never makes the call. Neither does she.

The song never names their work, and it never needs to: "visiting the sick," "the words they pay me for," "the second row," "red pens," "a stack of papers." Let the listener work it out.

**Playing it:**

- **Feel it in two.** Two big beats a bar, each three eighths. The sway is the song's heat. Don't rush it.
- **Bar 4 of every verse line belongs to the piano.** The singer is done by bar 3; the electric piano answers.
- **The ending doesn't lift.** The last choruses are the fullest, but they stay in E♭. The song refuses its own key change the way they refuse each other.

## For the players

- **Electric piano:** the heart of the song. Verses: a low chord on beat 1, held, then the triplet climb on the three eighths of beat 2. In bar 4 of every verse and bridge line, after the voice is done, the answer: a falling line of six eighths from the chord's top. Choruses: chords on both beats; in each line's second bar the second chord comes an eighth early. Intro and outro alone.
- **Bass:** warm and round, fingered. The root on beat 1 (a dotted quarter), the fifth on beat 2, an eighth-note approach into a new chord. Bridge: the root held for the whole bar. In from verse 1; out for the intro and outro.
- **Organ:** drawbar, quiet. Held chords under the choruses and the bridge, and low under verses 4–6. Fullest in the last two choruses.
- **Drums (brushes):** out for the intro, verse 1 and the outro. Verses: hats on the eighths (leaning on 1 and 2), side-stick on 2, a soft kick on 1; verses 5–6 even softer, no kick. Choruses: kick on 1, snare on 2, ride on the eighths, crash at each chorus's top. Bridge: hats and a soft kick only.

## For the singers

**Him: baritone, C3–B♭3, one C4. Her: B♭3–B♭4, one C5. You never sing at the same time.**

- **Sing it like you're talking to one person.** Low and close in the verses. These are confessions, not performances.
- **Leave the fourth bar alone.** Each verse line is done by bar 3. The piano answers; don't fill it.
- **The chorus is warmer, not louder.** Let it open up from the verse, nothing more.
- **"Fog"** is the only high note for each of you, on the downbeat, in your last chorus. Let it ache; don't belt it.
- **The bridge is quiet.** Say what happened plainly. No drama.
- **Her chorus is his tune an octave up.** Same shape, same places to breathe.

## Production brief

**The song.** A slow-burn soul duet about two married, middle-aged people whose affair is the best thing that ever happened to them, and who both go home anyway. Electric piano at the centre, a warm bass, a little organ, brushes. Two singers who never sing together: him low and close, her an octave above him on the same chorus tune. It should feel like a late-night confession, with the heat in the groove and the restraint in the voices, and it should end with nothing resolved: no key change, no duet, the piano alone.

**References**

- **Billy Paul, "Me and Mrs. Jones" (1972):** a Philadelphia soul ballad about an extramarital affair. Paul's voice builds from a near-whisper toward its climaxes. Take the grown-up tone, and that slow build from almost nothing.
- **Luther Ingram, "(If Loving You Is Wrong) I Don't Want to Be Right" (1972):** a Stax-written soul ballad about a married man with two children who is enthralled with his mistress. Take the refusal to judge: the song sides with the feeling.
- **Otis Redding, "I've Been Loving You Too Long" (1965):** a slow, emotional soul ballad with pleading vocals over Steve Cropper's arpeggiated guitar. Take the patience: the accompaniment rolling under a voice that never hurries.

**Non-negotiables**

1. **No key change.** E♭ from the first bar to the last, including the last choruses.
2. **They never sing at the same time.** No harmonies, no overlap, no shared last line.
3. **Bar 4 of every verse and bridge line belongs to the electric piano.** Nobody else fills it.
4. **"Fog" is each singer's only high note:** his C4 (bar 137), her C5 (bar 145).
5. **Restraint.** No belting, no gospel runs, no big drum fills. The heat comes from the sway.
6. **Brushes, not sticks.** No drums in the intro, verse 1 or the outro.

**Range**

- **Him:** baritone, C3–B♭3, one C4 ("fog," bar 137). Verses C3–B♭3; his choruses E♭3–B♭3.
- **Her:** B♭3–B♭4, one C5 ("fog," bar 145). Verses B♭3–G4; her choruses E♭4–B♭4.

**Tracking**

1. **Electric piano and bass together** to the click, so the answers and the bass approaches breathe together. Then the brushes.
2. **Organ** last among the instruments.
3. **The two vocals as separate takes**, each singer alone in the room. They never overlap, so there's no reason to sing to each other's live mic. Track the bridge in order, line by line, so each singer is answering the line before.

**Blend and panning**

- **Him** slightly left of centre, **her** slightly right: close but never in the same place.
- **Electric piano** centre-left and warm; its answers forward in the mix. **Bass** centre. **Organ** wide and low. **Brushes** soft and wide.
- **Vocals dry and close** in the verses and the bridge, a little more room in the choruses.
- **The outro:** the piano alone. Let the last chord ring out completely.
