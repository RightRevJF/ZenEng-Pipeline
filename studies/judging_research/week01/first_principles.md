# Week 01: first principles (written after the run, at Jason's request, 21 November 2026)

## 1. Bedrock
- Fact: a cover is a skilled musician choosing to spend hours learning a song and performing it with a different voice, sound and often style. What carries over from the original is the notes (and the words). The recording, the singer and the production are thrown away.
- Fact: lasting love of the original is people choosing the same recording again for years. That recording includes the sound, which the repo's studies found predicts the yardstick (G) twice, on independent stores.
- Fact: every song tested so far was already a hit (Hot 100, Hooktheory, McGill Billboard). The comparison is great hits against ordinary hits, a narrow band in which most of the differences in the notes have already been filtered out.
- Assumption (untested): that one number, love plus covers, is the right target for a judge that sees only the notes.

## 2. Questioning the requirements
- **G = love + covers.** Set by the project to keep critics out. But love of the original is partly love of the recording, which a notes-only judge cannot see. Covers are the half of greatness that has already survived losing the recording. Flag: mixing the two may dilute the very signal a notes judge could find. Not dropped; proposed as a split (covers alone as the primary target for notes; love kept as the second channel).
- **Notes only.** Jason's brief, kept. First principles support it more strongly for covers than for love, for the reason above.
- **Hits only.** A habit of the available data, not a law. The question of greatness is sharpest between songs that lasted and songs that were written and recorded but went nowhere.
- **Cover count.** Counts all covers alike, including the ones fame produces (card 4) and lounge-band copies of a recent hit.

## 3. Delete
- S nearness to 1 as a test of greatness: failed on all three sets. Keep S as a description of where songs sit (near 1, g about −0.3 to −0.4), not as a judge. Flagged for Jason; not dropped without him.
- The seven tune-shape measures of the first shape run: failed, and the leads did not repeat on fresh songs. Retire them as judges.
- Distance-to-template shapes and S read as an angle: dropped in the debate.

## 4. Simplify
One measure that has already held twice (chords that move within a section) against one outcome made of notes (covers), with nothing fitted.

## 5. Speed the loop
The cheapest kill test is J6a: chords that move against covers made within five years of release. Prediction written: −0.05 or stronger; fails if weaker than −0.03 while later covers still show it.

## 6. The obstacle and the way through
**Obstacle.** The target mixes the notes with what the notes cannot see (the recording, fame, luck), and the songs compared are all hits, so the notes' share of the difference is small and buried.

**Way through (proposal, untested).** Choose the outcome that is made of notes: how far a song travels when everything but the notes is changed. Measure **cross-style cover breadth**, the number of distinct genres or styles a song has been covered in (Discogs release genres and styles in Discogs-VI; SecondHandSongs where available), counting early covers only. A song covered in jazz, country, soul and punk has proved its notes carry it without the original singer or sound. Prediction: note measures (chords that move first) predict cross-style breadth more strongly than raw cover count, and more strongly than love of the original. Fails if the effect on breadth is no larger than on raw count. A second step, later: compare lasting songs with songs that were recorded but never charted (the far tail), where the notes may differ most.
