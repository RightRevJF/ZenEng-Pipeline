# Card 3: Hearing it more makes it liked more, at every level of complexity (Madison & Schiölde 2017)

## Citation and link
Madison, G., & Schiölde, G. (2017). Repeated listening increases the liking for music regardless of its complexity:
implications for the appreciation and aesthetics of music. *Frontiers in Neuroscience* 11, 147.
doi:10.3389/fnins.2017.00147. PMID 28408864. Open access (CC BY).

## The claim, and the data it rests on
Claim: liking rises steadily with repeated hearing, and it rises in the same way for simple and for complex music. There
is no inverted U (Berlyne's "Wundt curve") for exposure or for complexity.

- **Stimuli:** the authors took 197 excerpts from about 800 albums in their own collections, chose 80, cut them to
  38-75 s, and kept 40 for the main study. All instrumental (vocals were excluded; for vocal tracks they used the intro or
  a bridge). Pop, rock, jazz, world music and mixtures; the most complex levels were mostly jazz and progressive rock.
  Hits and widely played tracks were excluded on purpose, so the music was meant to be unknown to listeners.
- **Experiment 1 (picking the levels):** 8 trained musicians (6-12 years of training) rated all 80 excerpts on sliders.
  The 40 kept excerpts formed four levels of 10, picked near the 13th, 37th, 62nd and 87th percentiles of rated
  complexity (mean z = -1.0, -0.4, +0.2, +1.1).
- **Experiment 2 (the exposure study):** 15 adults (10 women, 5 men; aged 28-70, mean 51), a convenience sample of music
  professionals and their acquaintances. Each heard all 40 excerpts once a day at home or in the car, from CDs in seven
  shuffled orders, logged in a diary, for about four weeks. They rated each excerpt at hearings 1, 10, 19 and 28. Ratings
  were 0-10 sliders: "I like this", "odd", "dull", and "I listen to similar music" (style familiarity, not knowing the
  track). Diaries showed small slips: five people ran 1-3 days late, one paused 26 days through illness, two missed one
  hearing.
- **No control group:** everyone got the full exposure. There was no group that heard the music only once, or only at
  the rating sessions.
- **Success:** a rise in mean liking across the four sessions, and whether that rise differed by complexity level.

## The measure: from the notes or not?
Not from the notes. "Complexity" is what 8 musicians said after hearing recorded audio. They rated overall, melodic,
harmonic and rhythmic complexity, tempo, instrumentation, liking and quality. Agreement was good (Cronbach's alpha 0.83
for overall complexity, 0.91 for rhythmic). In a stepwise regression across the 80 excerpts, rated melodic complexity
explained most of the overall rating, then rhythmic. But rated *quality* also came in (beta 0.36), and so did
instrumentation and tempo. Harmonic complexity did not enter. So the levels are raters' impressions of a recording.
They probably lean on melody and rhythm, but they also carry sound, arrangement, tempo and how good the track seemed. No
score, transcription or count of notes or chords appears anywhere. The authors themselves say complexity may be mixed up
with how typical a piece is of its style.

## How big the effect is and how it was checked
- **Exposure:** liking rose with hearings, F(3,42) = 12.35, p < .00001, omega squared about 0.20. The text calls the rise
  steady at all four levels, with no plateau reported. A weak dip at hearing 19 or 28 showed for 4 of the 15 people,
  mostly at the two simplest levels; the largest was about one scale point, and none was significant.
- **Complexity:** no main effect on liking, F(3,42) = 0.81, p = .49. **No exposure x complexity interaction,**
  F(9,126) = 1.33, p = .23. Complexity did show up elsewhere: the simpler excerpts were rated much more "dull"
  (F(3,42) = 11.1, omega squared about 0.35; r = -0.64 with expert complexity), and somewhat more "odd" at higher
  complexity (r = +0.41).
- **Inverted U:** none at any level. The most complex level was liked most at every session, and the next most complex
  tended to come second. The authors say this looks more like a U than an inverted U, but they offer it only as a trend.
- **The individual piece mattered a lot:** the effect of which excerpt it was (within a level) was strong,
  F(9,126) = 7.51, p < .00001. So something about particular pieces drove liking, and the four complexity bands did not
  capture it.
- **Regression on 160 cells** (40 excerpts x 4 sessions): liking = hearings + style familiarity + complexity,
  R squared = 0.57. Standardized betas were 0.55 for style familiarity, 0.46 for hearings and 0.23 for complexity, all
  p < .00005. This treats repeated cells of the same excerpt as independent, so the p-values are too small. It also sits
  awkwardly next to the null complexity effect in the ANOVA. The authors' summary is that
  "familiarity is the single most important variable for explaining differences in liking".
- **Checks:** none on held-out data, no replication, no control group, no correction for the many tests. The listeners
  were small in number and keen on music. Rating sessions were themselves hearings. The complexity range was narrow
  (about 2 SD, all from styles the authors own), and the authors grant that a wider range might show the curve.
  With 15 listeners, a moderate interaction could have been missed: "not significant" is not "absent".

## What it says about the yardstick (lasting love plus covers), not only popularity
- **It is about short-run liking, not lasting love.** It covers 28 hearings in four weeks, by 15 people, of unknown
  instrumental excerpts. A song that is still loved after 20 years has had thousands of hearings across millions of
  people. The study does not reach the range where wear-out would show. The paper's own introduction argues the
  opposite way for the long run: simple music wins listeners fast and loses them sooner, while complex music needs more
  hearings and keeps a smaller group (it cites Serra et al. 2012). The data here do not test that.
- **For the skeptic it is a real point, with limits.** If hearing a song is what makes it liked, then a song that got
  more airplay early will gather more love regardless of its notes. Lasting love would then mostly record past exposure,
  and the notes would add little. That fits the engine's own studies: across about 3,100 songs, no shape of the tune
  told good from great, while the recording's sound did. But the study does not show that structure is irrelevant. The
  individual piece strongly affected liking. The richer pieces were never liked less, and were rated far less dull.
  Complexity was measured only roughly, as four bands from raters.
- **Covers are different.** A cover is a musician choosing to spend weeks learning and playing a song. Exposure plainly
  helps (the paper notes that working musicians live off tunes the audience already knows). But musicians have heard
  everything; for them exposure is close to saturated, so whatever still separates songs should be in the song. The
  covers study fits that: chords that move predict covers in two sources and do not predict views. Madison & Schiölde
  give no reason to expect exposure to erase that signal. Their "dull" result, if anything, says simple material is what
  repeated listeners tire of first.
- **The confound to watch:** chart exposure and lasting love are both made partly by the same liking. "Exposure" cannot
  be taken from charts as if it were assigned at random, the way it was here.

## One test Luthier could run on its kept tables
**Does chart exposure explain lasting love and covers, and does it erase or interact with the notes?**

Songs: the Hooktheory songs of 2005 or earlier that are already joined to G (Spotify love within era plus covers within
era) and to the Hot 100. Exposure: total weeks on the Hot 100, plus peak position. Note measures (fixed beforehand, from
the covers study): chord repetition within a section, and share of beats with no melody note (rests). Controls: year,
note count, source. Split the artists in two halves by name as the lock. Write the prediction to PREDICTION.md before
running.

Prediction (written in advance):
1. Weeks on chart predict love more strongly than either note measure does (partial rho at least twice as large, in both
   halves).
2. **Madison & Schiölde carry over (the skeptic's hypothesis):** the exposure x note-measure interaction on *love* is
   null: |beta| < 0.05 in both halves, pooled p > .05.
3. **Covers are not made by exposure:** chord repetition's negative link to covers survives with weeks on chart and peak
   held. Expected partial rho about -0.05 or stronger, same sign in both halves, pooled p < .05. Its link to love, with
   the same controls, is under 0.03 either way.

What would make it fail:
- (2) fails if songs with moving chords keep *more* love per week of chart exposure (interaction > +0.05, same sign in
  both halves). That would be the long-run wear-out the paper's introduction proposes and its data could not see.
- (3) fails if the chord-repetition link to covers drops below |0.03| once weeks on chart are held. Then the covers
  finding was riding on exposure, and the skeptic wins on covers too.
- The whole test is void if weeks on chart and G correlate above 0.7, because then exposure and outcome cannot be
  separated.

## Leads
1. **Liking and memory for musical stimuli as a function of exposure.** Szpunar, Schellenberg & Pliner, 2004.
   doi:10.1037/0278-7393.30.2.370. It found liking falls with focused hearing but not with listening in the background,
   so when exposure happens is a moderator.
2. **Subjective complexity, familiarity, and liking for popular music.** North & Hargreaves, 1995.
   doi:10.1037/h0094090. It used real pop songs and reported the inverted U this paper failed to find.
3. **Relationship between complexity and liking as a function of expertise.** Orr & Ohlsson, 2005.
   doi:10.1525/mp.2005.22.4.583. It asks whether musicians want more complexity than lay listeners, which bears on covers
   as musicians' choices.
4. **Liking for happy- and sad-sounding music: effects of exposure.** Schellenberg, Peretz & Vieillard, 2007 (published
   2008, vol. 22). doi:10.1080/02699930701350753. It found that overexposure lowers liking, depending on mood. That is a
   direct test of wear-out, and the engine already found that loved songs sound less cheerful.
5. **Measuring the evolution of contemporary Western popular music.** Serra, Corral, Boguña, Haro & Arcos, 2012.
   doi:10.1038/srep00521. It is the source for the paper's claim that simple music wins fast and fades fast, and it
   measures pitch transitions at corpus scale.
