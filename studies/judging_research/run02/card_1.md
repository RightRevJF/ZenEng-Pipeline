## The claim, and the data it rests on
- **Corpus:** SecondHandSongs, scraped in January 2020, plus AllMusic for each artist's genre (21 genres, more than 100 styles) and song count. Medleys (11,112) and covers of traditional or unknown songs (20,428) were removed. When artists performed together, the cover was counted once for each of them.
- **Network:** 106,095 artists joined by 855,650 covers. An arrow runs from the covering artist to the *original performer*: the person who first recorded the song, not the songwriter. Arrows are weighted by how many times one artist covered the other, whatever the song. **There is no song-level analysis at all.** Every result is about artists.
- **What counts as success ("impact"):** three network scores. Weighted in-degree is the total number of covers an artist received. PageRank is covers weighted by how much the covering artist is covered in turn. Weighted betweenness is how far an artist sits on the bridges between groups (crossover).
- **Claims:** (1) Covering is extremely unequal. (2) Covers per artist grew until the 1950s and then fell. The author reads this as two phases: before rock, standards were reworked in competing versions; after rock, covers became tributes. (3) The main reason one artist covers another is that they share a genre, with language and nationality next. (4) Jazz and Pop/Rock are the two hubs of the genre network.

## The measure: from the notes or not?
Not from the notes. Nothing about pitch, rhythm, harmony or form is measured. The inputs are counts of covers between artists and AllMusic genre labels, so this is a study of social and industry structure. Genre is a label attached to the artist, not to the song, and not something read from the score. Its use for Luthier is as a guide to the *outcome* side: how cover counts behave and what biases them.

## How big the effect is and how it was checked
- **Inequality:** only 22% of artists have ever been covered, and the top 100 artists (0.1%) take 41% of all covers. The in-degree and out-degree distributions follow power laws with exponents of 2.94 and 2.88. Density is below 0.01%. There are 9.8 covers per artist against 79 songs per artist.
- **Top artists:** The Beatles 16,825 covers received, Duke Ellington 10,564, Bing Crosby 7,011. By PageRank: The Beatles, Bob Dylan, Bing Crosby. By betweenness: Frank Sinatra first.
- **Rise and fall over time (cumulative network by decade):** weighted average degree goes 1.07 (1910s), 18.18 (1950s), 16.85 (1960s), 12.94 (1990s), 9.68 (2010s). Clustering peaks at 0.093 in the 1940s and falls to 0.066 by the 2010s.
- **Genre stays at home:** 73% of covers by jazz artists are of jazz artists. The figure is 43% for country and 37% for blues. Jazz takes 24% of its covers from Vocal-genre singers. Artists from the 1900s gave 55% of their covers to Vocal and 17% to Classical. Artists from the 2010s gave 39% to Pop/Rock and 11.7% to Vocal. Louvain clustering of the 35-genre network finds five communities (modularity 0.279). English-language artists make up 63% of the network. **The paper gives no overall share of within-genre against cross-genre covers.**
- **Checks:** none in a statistical sense. All of it is description. There is no held-out data, no second source, no significance tests, and no comparison with sales, charts or awards. The author admits that SecondHandSongs coverage is uneven (minority and local styles are likely under-counted), that AllMusic's genre labels are debatable, and that raw counts favour prolific artists. He chose not to normalise by song count because one-hit artists would then score too high.

## What it says about the yardstick (lasting love plus covers), not only popularity
- **Cover counts as a measure:** this is a strong warning that raw cover counts are dominated by a few already-famous performers. The author suggests that musicians, who cover only a few songs each, aim at the most popular and prestigious names, and cites conformity bias in music sampling (Youngblood 2019). PageRank builds the same loop into the score itself. That is the cover-fame feedback loop, though the paper interprets it rather than tests it. For Luthier, the original performer's prior cover count is a confound to hold fixed. If it is not held, "great song" partly means "song by a covered artist".
- **Cross-genre covering:** this is the most useful part for the cross-style breadth idea from last run. Covering within the same genre is the strong default (jazz 73%). A cover across genres therefore goes against the main force in the network, which makes it a more selective signal than a raw count. The paper also implies a baseline problem: songs from jazz or Vocal origins draw covers mostly from their own large, inward-looking communities. Any breadth score must therefore be judged against the original's own genre.
- **Decay and era:** covering practice itself changed. Per-artist covering peaked in the 1950s, and after rock arrived covers became tributes aimed mostly at artists of the 1950s and 1960s. Cover counts are therefore not on the same scale across eras. A song from 1935 and a song from 1985 face different covering cultures, not only different amounts of time. The paper does not measure the lag from original to cover or how covering fades over time, so it says nothing directly about "early covers only".
- **Lasting love:** the paper does not address it.
- **Popularity is not greatness:** the paper does not separate the two. "Impact" here is artist-level influence, mixed in with fame.

## One test Luthier could run on its kept tables
**Does chord motion predict cross-genre covering beyond the cover count and the original artist's cover fame?**

- **Data:** the Discogs-VI songs already matched to Hooktheory (2,360 with note measures). First check whether Discogs-VI's release records carry Discogs genre tags; if they do not, the test cannot run on this source.
- **Breadth:** for each song, count the distinct Discogs genres among cover releases issued within 10 years of the song's first year, leaving out the original's own genre.
- **Prerequisite (Ortega's pattern):** first confirm that most early covers share the original's genre. If they do not, Ortega's homophily does not carry to this source, and breadth means something different here.
- **Model:** partial Spearman of within-section chord repetition against breadth. Hold the log count of early covers, the first year, the original's genre, and the original performer's total covers in Discogs-VI from songs other than this one (the fame confound Ortega points to). Split by artist name into two halves.
- **Prediction, written now:** chord repetition goes with *less* breadth. Partial rho is −0.05 or lower in each half, and below zero pooled at p < .05.
- **Fail:** pooled rho above −0.03, or the halves disagree in sign. A pass that disappears once artist cover fame is added counts as a fail of the note claim, and as evidence for Ortega's fame loop.

## Leads
1. **Conformity bias in the cultural transmission of music sampling traditions.** Youngblood, M., 2019, Royal Society Open Science, doi:10.1098/rsos.191149. Tests whether reuse follows popularity more than merit; this is the mechanism Ortega invokes for covering.
2. **The evolution of popular music: USA 1960–2010.** Mauch, M., MacCallum, R. M., Levy, M., Leroi, A. M., 2015, Royal Society Open Science, doi:10.1098/rsos.150081. Harmonic and timbral topics over 50 years; a basis for era-adjusting note measures before comparing cover outcomes.
3. **Fashion and art cycles are driven by counter-dominance signals of elite competition: quantitative evidence from music styles.** Klimek, P., Kreuzbauer, R., Thurner, S., 2019, Journal of the Royal Society Interface, doi:10.1098/rsif.2018.0731. Style turnover dynamics; could explain why covering moves between genres across eras.
4. **Covers Uncovered: A History of the "Cover Version," from Bing Crosby to the Flaming Lips.** Dineley, S., 2014, doctoral dissertation, Western University, https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3630&context=etd. The history behind Ortega's two phases (competing versions before rock, tributes after), relevant to comparing cover counts across eras.
5. **Song 'adaptations' and the globalisation of French pop, 1960–1970.** Anderson, K., 2015, French Cultural Studies 26(3). The one earlier quantitative cover study Ortega cites; covering across languages, a parallel to covering across genres.
