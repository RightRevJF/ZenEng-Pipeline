# Agent 5: Voice and address (who speaks, to whom, in what register)

Consensus quota ran out after two searches; the rest came from WebSearch and WebFetch. Pronoun counts for our songs (38 songs, hold-outs p69, p60 and p73 not read) and for the round-1 corpus were computed from stored features and simple word counts. No lyric text was stored.

## 1. Summary
The strongest evidence on address comes from the audience side, not from critics. A "you", especially "you" as the object of the verb, raises liking and buying of songs (Packard & Berger 2020: about 4,200 Billboard songs plus experiments, N = 2,921). The proposed mechanism is not that the listener feels addressed. Instead, "you" makes listeners think of a particular person in their own lives. In the round-1 corpus, acclaimed writers use a little less "you" than other hit-makers (4.5 against 5.4 per 100 words), and 26% of acclaimed hits sit at or below our level of 2.0 (20% of all hits). So low "you" is a favoured but minority corner, not a defect.

The bigger finding is that our songs are low on both personal pronouns: "I" 5.2 per 100 words (acclaimed 7.6, all hits 8.4) and "you" 2.0. First plus second person combined is about 7.2 for us against 12.9 for acclaimed hits. Only 18% of acclaimed hits are that impersonal (14.5% of all hits). Our lyrics lean on images and on third person (2.1 per 100 words) rather than on a speaker and an addressee.

Narrative-psychology research says perspective has only small direct effects. First-person narration raises identification by about d = 0.10, and several careful experiments find nothing. Identification depends more on a character the reader can relate to, and on that character being present, than on grammatical person. Theory from popular-music studies (Frith, Moore, Culler, Cole, Watts & Andres Morrissey) agrees on one point the numbers cannot yet test: the listener both is addressed by the song's voice and takes that voice up. Pathos travels through a felt relationship between a speaker and an addressee, and it does not need named emotion.

For Jason's note "failing to catch the pathos", the best-supported lever is address: give the song a present addressee (object "you", apostrophe to a person, God, or the dead), or a speaking "I" whose stake is plain. Adding stated feeling is not the lever, and adding more images is not either. For narrated songs, the evidence favours letting the characters speak (reported dialogue, as ballads do) over a narrator who explains.

**What travels and what is style.** These travel: address used as an act toward a present other; a relatable, specific person; perspective as a small dial; conversational base register. Register switching into archaic or prophetic voices (Dylan, scripture-steeped writers), the withdrawn ballad narrator (folk), and the first-person confessional (singer-songwriter) are genre or writer styles.

## 3. Testable next
1. **Third-person narration and acclaim.** Measure: third-person pronoun rate (he/she/they/him/her/them per 100 words), plus a "story song" flag (third person > first + second person). Data: feat.csv corpus (add one column) and within-writer pairs. Prediction: across writers, story songs are over-represented among acclaimed hits and under-represented among chart peaks; within writer, no difference.
2. **Object "you" versus subject "you".** Measure: dependency-parsed share of "you" as object against as subject or generic. Data: the same corpus with a parser; lines not needed. Prediction: object "you" goes with higher year-end rank (replicating Packard), not with acclaim. Generic "you" goes with acclaim more than addressed "you".
3. **Personal-pronoun layer (I + you).** Measure: first plus second person per 100 words. Data: the corpus, within writer. Prediction: no within-writer effect on acclaim, but low values plus high image density (our corner) predict acclaim across writers more than image density alone. If true, our corner is fine and the pathos gap lies elsewhere.
4. **Address switches.** Measure: number of changes of pronoun mode (I, you, we, third person, quoted speech) across sections. Data: lyrics with line and section breaks (not in round 1). Prediction: acclaimed songs show one or two deliberate switches (for example a turn to direct address at the bridge) more often than other hits by the same writers.
5. **Our drafts against Jason's pathos notes.** Measure: first plus second person rate, presence of an apostrophe or a sustained addressee, per draft. Data: our draft history with Jason's comments (excluding hold-outs). Prediction: drafts he flagged as lacking pathos have lower personal-pronoun rates and no present addressee.
