# Agent 3: Structure and the turn (25 November 2026)

Angle: how a lyric's large-scale shape (arc, turn, refrain, ending, ambiguity) affects how it moves, is remembered and lasts. Consensus ran out of allowance after five searches; the rest came from WebSearch and WebFetch. No lyrics or poems reproduced.

## 1. Summary

The strongest evidence on shape is about **where feeling lands, not which template is used**. Having a standard narrative arc does not predict a story's popularity (Boyd et al. 2020, about 40,000 narratives). Narrativity marks prestige only in country music, where Grammy-nominated songs beat their own album-mates; for rock, R&B, rap and Song of the Year the gap was small and not significant (Bamman et al. 2025). That is the same within-album design as our within-writer test, so "make it a story" is a genre's style, not a rule that travels. What does travel is **end-weighting**. In poems read aloud, chills pile up towards the ends of lines, stanzas and whole poems, and passages of direct social address (one person speaking to another) give more chills than description, even with position controlled (Wassiliwizky et al. 2017). How intense a song is remembered as being follows the **average** of the whole song most, with peak and end adding a smaller share (Schäfer et al. 2014). So a strong ending cannot rescue a weak middle. Removing a story's resolution hurts its sense of being a story far more than it hurts liking (Brewer & Lichtenstein 1981). Repetition helps up to a point (inverted U in about 88% of 57 music studies). Repeated *words* lose appeal unless they turn song-like. Parallelism (recurring sound and pattern) raises both liking and understanding, while deviation lowers both (Menninghaus et al. 2024). On ambiguity, high ambiguity and the felt "insight" raised liking and interest even when the puzzle was not solved (Muth et al. 2015, visual art). That supports "mysterious but not confusing": open meaning carried in fluent, patterned form. For the sonnet volta, the bridge and the chorus that gathers meaning, I found **no reader-response evidence**: they are craft teaching (FOLKLORE), though measurable. Two findings bear directly on Jason's note that we fail to catch the pathos. First, the social-address chill effect. Second, sad endings and the "man in a hole" shape win audiences (votes, box office) but not ratings or critics. This repeats the round-1 split between popularity and acclaim.

## 3. Testable next (rule-first)

1. **Narrativity within writer (null predicted except country).** Measure: Bamman's narrativity composite (Agent/Events/World; their RoBERTa if released, otherwise an annotated proxy). Data: our existing 1,027-song within-writer set (song-level, so no line breaks needed). Prediction: acclaimed minus other ≈ 0 overall; positive for country artists only.
2. **Chorus recolouring.** Measure: semantic distance between the verse texts that come before each chorus repeat (embedding of verse 1 vs verse 2 vs bridge), with the chorus held fixed; plus the share of refrain repeats with a changed word. Data: lyrics with section labels (line-broken corpus). Prediction: higher in acclaimed songs within writer.
3. **End-weighting.** Measure: image-noun and social-address density in the final section and final lines of sections, against the song's mean; also the *weakest* section's score (testing "average dominates"). Data: section-labelled lyrics. Prediction: acclaimed songs have a higher end/mean ratio and a higher minimum section score.
4. **Ending valence.** Measure: sentiment of the last 20% of words minus the song mean (syuzhet-style, word-position chunks, feasible on the current words-only corpus); also the height of the peak. Prediction: falling or bittersweet endings go with acclaim and with contest-style popularity; peak height irrelevant or negative.
5. **Turn location.** Measure: position of the largest section-to-section shift in sentiment or embedding. Prediction: acclaimed songs place it in the final third (bridge or last verse) more often than the same writer's other hits.
