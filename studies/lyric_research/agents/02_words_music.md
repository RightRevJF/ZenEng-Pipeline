# Agent 02: words meeting music (text setting and singability)

Round 2, 25 November 2026. Searches: Consensus (4 searches, then the plan's allowance ran out), then WebSearch and WebFetch. No lyrics are reproduced here.

## 1. Summary
How words meet the tune is the best-evidenced part of lyric craft for **comprehension and memory**, and has almost no evidence for **acclaim or popularity**. Solid: English songs put stressed syllables on strong beats, long notes and melodic peaks, and aligned settings are understood better (about 85% vs 75% of words). The kind of mismatch matters more than the count: a stress wrong inside a word is penalised far more than loose phrase stress, and a stressed syllable sung an eighth early (anticipation) is normal rock idiom, not an error. Long notes favour long vowels and diphthongs; high notes erase vowel distinctions (above roughly A4 to E5 only /i a u/ survive). Archaic words (81% vs 41% recognised), melisma (84% vs 77%) and back-to-back rhymes cost intelligibility. Emotional match between words and music is weak and inconsistent evidence: the major/happy-words link is tiny and flips in some genres, and the best-known lyrics-vs-melody emotion study failed to replicate. Deliberate "prosodic dissonance" is a recognised device, supported by close readings, not data. For us: the mouth tests aim at the right things, but should rank mismatches by kind, pass anticipations, and check vowels on long and high notes and how the title is set. This protects intelligibility and memory; it is a floor, not a route to acclaim.

| Phrase ends meet line ends; enjambment and mismatch are expressive when chosen | Hayes 2009; Ginsborg & Sloboda 2007 (recall breaks at phrase ends). | Keywords land on strong beats more than syllable stress alone predicts | Liao et al. 2024, IEEE BigData pilot: 80.8% of keywords on strong beats. Nichols 2009: peaks favour content words | SUGGESTIVE (pilot) | Corpus | Probably | Put the title and key nouns on beat 1 or 3 and on a peak or long note |
| Catchiness (fast recognition) follows how prominent the voice is and how repetitive and conventional the melody is | Van Balen et al. 2015, ISMIR: 973 players, 1,715 segments. Voice prominence +0.14 SD, melodic repetitiveness +0.12. Marginal R² about 0.10 | STRONG (big n, modest R²) | Memory or recognition (popularity channel) | Yes | Hook setting: repeat the melody under the title. This is the chart channel, not acclaim |
| Emotional match between words and music: weak and inconsistent | Kolchinsky et al. 2017, R. Soc. **Travels:** word stress meeting the beat; long vowels on long notes; vowel loss at high pitch; costs of archaic words and melisma; phrase ends at line ends; repeated melody under the hook. **Style:** amount of syncopation and anticipation; syllable rate; speech-timed delivery (Hough's model 4: Dylan, rap); deliberate dissonance; mode matching mood.

**Gap:** no study found links setting quality to acclaim, canon or covers. Hough 2025 and Smith 2024 suggest acclaimed writers often break alignment on purpose, so "more aligned" need not mean "more acclaimed".

## 3. Testable next (rule-first)
Data: Rolling Stone corpus stress files (Tan et al. 2019; 80 songs, syllable-level onset, pitch, CMU stress; zipped at rockcorpus.midside.com, a host not on our shell's allow-list, untested; section labels unconfirmed), joined to RS-500 status from round 1. Our songs: same measures from their LilyPond scores.

1. **Title prominence.** Share of title stressed syllables on beat 1/3 and on a longer-than-median note or local peak, vs other content words in the song. Predict title > others in the corpus; then place our songs.
2. **Long-note vowels.** Share of longer-than-median notes carrying a tense vowel or diphthong (CMU class). Predict corpus above 55%, lax vowels under-represented; ours at least the corpus median.
3. **Peak words.** Among notes in each song's top 10% of range: share of function words, and of vowels other than /i a u/ or diphthongs. Predict function words below base rate (Nichols); flag ours above the corpus 75th percentile.
4. **Verse vs chorus dissonance** (Smith's untested claim). Rate of unaligned, non-anticipatory stressed syllables. Predict verse > chorus. Needs hand-added section labels.
5. **Acclaim and alignment within writer.** Non-anticipatory mismatch rate and anticipation share, RS-500 songs vs the same artists' other corpus songs. Predict null or slightly more syncopation in acclaimed songs; a null means alignment is a floor, not a lever. Low power at 80 songs.
