# Agent 4: Feeling without naming it (pathos)

Research notes, 25 November 2026. Searches: Consensus (4 searches, then the plan's allowance ran out), then WebSearch and WebFetch. No lyrics or poems reproduced.

## 1. Summary

The evidence says being moved is not a reaction to images. It is a reaction to **people in a relationship at a turning point**. Kama muta / being-moved research across 19 countries finds that the trigger is a sudden strengthening (or a threatened loss) of a bond: death, birth, marriage, farewell, reunion, kindness. The feeling is mixed (sad and warm together) and comes with tears, chills and warmth in the chest. The lyric-specific studies point the same way. The lyric content that predicts tears is farewell (Mori 2022). Lyrics add the most to sad songs and the least to happy ones. People say they cry over songs because of the lyrics, their memories and nostalgia. In poetry, chills gather at the ends of lines, stanzas and whole poems, and passages with direct speech or address to someone get more of them. "Show, don't tell" holds up as a way to get comprehension and vividness: readers build specific feelings from situations without being told, and picturing something produces more emotion than processing the words. But no study we found shows that naming one feeling lowers the impact. The acclaimed hits from round 1 average 1.2 named emotions per 100 words; ours have none. The "specific victim" effect, often given as the reason concreteness moves people, turns out to be fragile (pooled r about .04 to .05). The likely diagnosis for Jason's "missing the pathos" is this. Our drafts are full of things but short on **persons in a predicament** and on **contrast of feeling** (one image family, one tone), and nothing **lands at the end**. Concrete images set the tone; a relational event and a turn near the end supply the pathos.

## 3. Testable next (rule-first)

1. **Persons in relation.** Measure: rate per 100 words of person words (kin terms, names, he/she/you as a person, "we") plus relationship-change verbs (leave, return, die, born, forgive, hold, wait). Data: round-1 corpus (works without lines) plus our songs. Prediction: acclaimed > other hits across writers, and ours < acclaimed. If both hold, this is the gap Jason hears.
2. **Valence contrast within the song.** Measure: standard deviation, and range from first half to second half, of word valence (Warriner norms) over the content words. Data: round-1 corpus + ours. Prediction: acclaimed songs show more spread (mixed feeling), and ours show low spread because one image family dominates.
3. **One named feeling, placed late.** Measure: whether a song has 1 to 3 emotion-label words, and their position (word index / length), against having none. Data: round-1 corpus (positions by word order). Prediction: among acclaimed songs, "few and late" is more common than "none". Our zero sits outside the acclaimed norm.
4. **End-weighting.** Measure: emotional load (valence extremity + person/relation words + address) in the last line of each section and the last section, against the song mean. Data: needs a corpus with lines (Genius/WASABI with line breaks) and acclaim labels. Prediction: higher end-load in acclaimed songs, as with poetry chills at closure.
5. **Separation theme.** Measure: lexicon flag for farewell, parting, death or reunion. Data: round-1 corpus, within writer. Prediction: the acclaimed song of a writer carries a parting or reunion event more often than the same writer's other hits (Mori 2022 and Menninghaus elicitors).
