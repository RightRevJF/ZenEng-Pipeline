# Agent 06: what the masters say (round 2, 25 November 2026)

Method: interviews, books and letters from 25 acclaimed writers and teachers, read on the web (WebSearch, WebFetch). Everything is paraphrased. Counts show how many named masters state a principle in a source I actually read. Counts are not independent votes: these writers learned from each other (Hammerstein taught Sondheim, Pattison taught a generation at Berklee) and they are survivors describing their own habits. Every principle is FOLKLORE unless the evidence column says otherwise.

## 1. Summary
The masters agree most on five things: concrete, specific detail (10 masters); hard revision and cutting (12); emotional honesty, meaning truth rather than autobiography (10); freshness, no stock phrasing (8); and economy, every word placed (8). The first and fourth match what round 1 found across writers (more image nouns and fewer stock phrases mark acclaimed writers), and we already sit past the acclaimed writers on both. The masters' own warnings point at where we may now be failing. Detail is meant to give the listener room to see their own life (Mitchell, Cave, Pattison, Cawley), not to pile up. Sondheim's "less is more" and Pattison's "space" both cap density. Key lines need room to land. Hal David's test is whether the lyric moves the writer, and if it doesn't, it goes. This fits Jason's "failing to catch the pathos" note better than any word-level measure does. The masters disagree on rhyme (Sondheim and Webb want true rhyme; Pattison and Bridgers use slant rhyme for unease or to avoid stiffness), on clarity versus ambiguity, on planning versus discovery, and on autobiography versus character. Where they disagree, the choice belongs to the song's style, and our process should not fix one answer. Two process findings have real evidence. First, quality follows quantity (Simonton's equal-odds rule). Second, creators misjudge their own new ideas, while peer creators judge them best (Berg 2016). Together they back "write many drafts, let someone else choose" more than any single craft rule.

## 3. Where the masters disagree (style choices, not rules)
- **Rhyme.** True rhyme: Sondheim (jokes don't land without it; clarity), Webb (false rhymes bother him; he says Simon and McCartney care too), Dylan (rhyme as a game, writing lines backward from the rhyme pair). Slant or family rhyme: Pattison (to unsettle), Bridgers (rhyme can make a sincere line stiff). Evidence cuts both ways. Rhyming sayings are judged truer (McGlone & Tofighbakhsh 2000, Psychological Science; a related slogan study found nothing). Successive rhymes reduced sung intelligibility (Johnson et al. 2013). Heavy rhyme is a machine tell (Walsh 2024).
- **Clarity vs ambiguity.** Sondheim, Hank Williams and Hal David want instant clarity. Dylan, Cohen and Waits let images arrive from the unconscious and keep the mystery.
- **Plan vs discover.** Sondheim decides what must be said first, and Hal David often starts from a title. Simon refuses to plot (he would rather "discover it"), Dylan and Van Zandt describe songs that arrive whole, and Waits sees songs as living things to chase.
- **Autobiography vs character.** Swift, Bridgers, L. Williams and Isbell draw on their own lives (Isbell also mixes in fiction). Newman and Sondheim write characters. Mitchell rejects the "confessional" label. What they share is emotional truth, not literal fact.
- **Fast vs slow.** Dylan, King and Van Zandt say their best songs came quickly. Cohen took years. Both camps still revise.
- **Timelessness vs the present.** Smokey and Fields want songs to last and not chase trends. Swift and Kendrick use current, dated detail.

## 4. Testable next
- **T1. Image-density ceiling (#2, #7).** Measure: image nouns per 100 words, quadratic term, against acclaim. Data: the existing 4,612-song corpus (testable now). Predict an inverted U: acclaim odds peak at moderate density, below our 13.7.
- **T2. Rhyme type by style.** Measure: share of line-end rhymes that are perfect, slant or none. Data: lyrics with line breaks (WASABI or similar) plus acclaim lists by genre. Predict no overall effect, with perfect rhyme higher in theatre and Tin Pan Alley acclaim, and slant or multisyllabic rhyme higher in rap and indie.
- **T3. Spoken register (#10).** Measure: share of words and word pairs common in spoken English (SUBTLEX-style frequencies), and the archaic-word rate. Data: corpus and ours. Predict acclaimed writers above us on spoken register and below us on archaic words.
- **T4. Stakes and change (#11, #5).** Measure: narrative-arc score (Alberhasky & Durkee's method) and the share of lines with a human agent doing something with a consequence. Data: corpus and ours. Predict ours lower than acclaimed, which would fit the pathos note.
- **T5. Best-of-N with an outside judge (#3, #4).** Measure: Jason's blind ratings of verses picked from 1, 5 or 20 candidates, picked either by the drafting model or by a separate judge. Predict ratings rise with N, and the separate judge beats self-selection.
