# Lyric research: reading notes (25 November 2026)

Sources found with Consensus (Elicit's API is not on this plan) and the web. Each note: what the paper found, how strong, what it suggests for us. Findings are hypotheses for our tests, not rules.

## A. Lyrics and success
- Nunes, Ordanini & Valsesia (2015, J. Consumer Psychology), "The power of repetition": more repetitive lyrics are processed more fluently; Billboard #1s are more repetitive (lab + chart). For us: the audience side (749.22); repetition in a refrain is earned, not a flaw.
- Melvill-Smith et al. (2022, Psychology of Music): processing fluency of lyrics (readability, rhyme, complexity) and UK top-5 peak/longevity, 1999-2014.
- Chehbouni et al. (2025, Psychology of Music): lyrics' typicality (repetition, complexity, themes, emotion) predicts weeks in the UK top five, not peak. Typical lyrics last on the chart.
- Parada-Cabaleiro et al. (2024, Scientific Reports): over five decades pop lyrics got simpler, more repetitive, more negative and more personal; rock fans read older songs' lyrics most.
- Askin & Mauskapf (2017, ASR): optimal differentiation: songs that are a little different from their neighbours do best (musical features; the same idea Berger & Packard test on lyrics' topics).
- Packard & Berger (2020, Psychological Science), "Thinking of you": songs with more second-person pronouns are liked and bought more (4,200 Billboard songs + experiments; strongest for "you" as object, e.g. "I love you"). Meta-analysis (Jin et al. 2025): "you" has a small positive effect in narrative (r about 0.15).
- Alberhasky & Durkee (2024, PLoS ONE), "Songs tell a story": song lyrics follow the narrative arc (staging, plot progression, cognitive tension), tested on 12,280 songs and a smaller set of greatest songs.
- Caution from all of these: success here is charts and sales, the audience's channel; none measures lasting acclaim or covers.

## B. Poetry quality, craft and imagery
- Kao & Jurafsky (2012, NAACL; 2015, LiLT): award-winning poets against amateurs: the strongest single marker of the professional poems was frequent reference to concrete objects; professionals use fewer emotion words and LESS perfect end rhyme (OR 0.12) and less alliteration (OR 0.53) [corrected 25 Nov by round 2's checker; page poetry, free verse]; amateurs resemble 19th-century professionals (abstraction, stated feeling). For us: concreteness up, stated emotion down.
- Dalvean (2013, Digital Scholarship in the Humanities): 98 linguistic variables tell professional from amateur poems at 84.5%.
- Jacobs & Kinder (2018, Metaphor and Symbol): literary metaphors differ from non-literary ones in surprisal, sonority and length; familiarity and ease of interpretation separate them.
- Chaudhuri et al. (2025, Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity and the Arts): readers' creativity judgments of poems follow aesthetic appeal, then surprise; the surprising lines were confirmed by semantic distance.
- Walsh et al. (2024, arXiv), "Does ChatGPT have a poetic style?": machine poems are more uniform than human ones: heavy rhyme, quatrains, iambic metre, "we/us/our", and words such as "heart", "embrace", "echo", "whisper". For us: a list of machine tells to check our drafts against, since our drafts are machine-written.

## C. Memory, and words with tune
- Purnell-Webb & Speelman (2008, Perceptual and Motor Skills): rhythm, with or without melody, aids recall of text: a schematic frame the words attach to. Familiar tune or rhythm helps more.
- Kilgour et al. (2000, Memory & Cognition): sung lyrics beat spoken ones only because singing is slower; equate the rate and the advantage goes.
- Racette & Peretz (2007, Memory & Cognition): learning to sing a new song is a dual task at first; words and tune are held separately early on.
- Peretz et al. (2004): in familiar songs, words and tune prime each other both ways; the stress pattern meeting the metre may be why. Serafine et al. (1982): words and tune of folk songs are remembered together, not apart.
- Peynircioglu et al. (2007, Psychology of Music): lyrics are the best cue for both title and melody.
- Ginsborg & Sloboda (2007): recall falters at phrase ends; the song's form is the frame for recall.
- Napadow et al. (2023): song lyrics are learned better with images than with the text in front of you.
- For us: a steady, sung rhythm and stress that meets the metre are what make words stick (our mouth tests and words' fit already chase this); concrete pictures help memory; the title is the handle for everything else.

## D. Studies of acclaimed craft (Dylan as one case among many, not a template)
- Hampton (2019, Bob Dylan's Poetics; reviewed by Burtzos 2022): the songs read as a run of artistic problems, each style answering the limits of the last; a persona that moves between vernaculars (the "hobo voice", the "archaic voice") lifts the words out of cliché; combinatory rather than pastiche; a collective world where the singer's trouble and ours are the same.
- Lebold (2007, Oral Tradition): the pleasure lies in three things at once: the written text, the voice rewriting its rhythm (oral free verse), and personae (prophet, trickster, man of sorrow) the songs build and undo.
- Garg (2025, arXiv), after Sunstein's "dishabituation": over fifty years the lyrics lean more on metaphor and vary more in which concepts sit at the centre; a refusal to settle.
- Dai et al. (2024, Int. J. Humanities and Arts Computing): Dylan's lyrics are verb-heavy (active texts), closer to written than spoken prose, first-person singular strong.
- Hewett (2017): "Imagine" and "Like a Rolling Stone" use grammar (tense, aspect, address) as an expressive tool, in opposite ways: Lennon invites, Dylan confronts.
- Antosz-Rekucki (2026): "All Along the Watchtower" drawn closely from scripture (prophets, Revelation): borrowing from a deep source and compressing it.
- What travels beyond one writer: (1) a voice or register chosen and kept, or switched on purpose; (2) verbs carrying the action; (3) compression and ambiguity anchored in concrete images; (4) borrowing from deep sources (scripture, folk song) and making it new; (5) direct address used as a dramatic act, not a filler "you".

## E. Text setting (words to tune)
- Gordon et al. (2011, Frontiers in Psychology): stressed syllables on strong beats improve comprehension of sung words (EEG and a lexical task).
- Johnson, Huron & Collister (2013, Empirical Musicology Review): sung words are harder to understand with archaic language, melismas, rhythm against the speech rhythm, and successive rhymes; easier with diphthongs and with a word heard just before.
- Hayes (2009): good setting resolves conflicting aims: stress on strong beats, no long lapses, no extreme compression, phrase ends at line ends. Some stress mismatch is the price of avoiding the others.
- Hough (2025, Gamut): four models in popular song: unified (stress meets beat), lyric dominated by the music, accentuated (stress pushed against the grid on purpose), speech-timed (Dylan's and rap's territory).
- Smith (2024, Music Theory Online): "prosodic dissonance" can be expressive, not only a fault.
- Girardi & Plag (2022): in art song, metrical position matters more than note length, and pitch least, for where stress goes.
- For us: our words' fit and the mouth tests are on solid ground; archaic words are a measurable cost (relevant to our hymns and our "carry", "nave"-type words); a deliberate mismatch can be an effect if chosen, not drawn.

## F. Novelty, cliché and surprise in words
- Beaty & Johnson (2021, Behavior Research Methods; SemDis): semantic distance between words strongly predicts human creativity ratings across tasks. Orwig et al. (2025): creativity rises with semantic distance up to a point (about 0.9 to 1), then flattens: too far reads as nonsense. A sweet spot, not "more is better".
- Bunescu & Uduehi (2022): creative language (humour, metaphor) carries high information content and surprise under a language model. Momen et al. (2026): surprisal correlates only moderately with judged metaphor novelty.
- Saakyan et al. (2026, ICLR): n-gram novelty goes with expert-judged creativity, but about 91% of the most n-gram-novel expressions were not judged creative; novelty needs appropriateness (sense, pragmatics). In open models, more novelty came with less sense.
- Lu et al. (2025, ICLR; Creativity Index): professional authors' text is about 66% less reconstructable from web snippets than machine text; machine text leans on stock phrasing.
- Lu et al. (2026): creativity metrics disagree across domains; perplexity tracks fluency, not novelty.
- Weinstein et al. (2022): creative sentences show rarer words and rarer word pairs, unique syntax, and rhyme and sound; together they explain only 16% of ratings.
- For us: (1) measure cliché as stock-phrase overlap (how much of a line is a common phrase), not raw word rarity; (2) aim for a sweet spot of distance inside an image, with sense kept; (3) treat any single creativity number with suspicion.
- Varnum et al. (2021, PLoS ONE): 14,661 Billboard songs, 1958-2016: simpler lyrics did better on the charts, more so in years crowded with new songs.
- Alberhasky & Durkee (2024): the narrative arc holds in a set of 2,823 greatest hits too; country and rap set the scene in more detail, pop moves the plot faster with more internal conflict.
- Berger & Packard (2018, Psychological Science): songs whose lyrics differ more in topic from their genre were more popular (weaker in dance and pop; topics, not style).
- Yu et al. (2023, ICWSM): in 50,000+ songs every kind of novelty, lyrics included, went with lower popularity. The popularity literature disagrees with itself about novelty.
- Ortega (2021, PLoS ONE): cover versions as a measure of a song's impact among artists (855k covers from SecondHandSongs); genre is the main channel of covering.

## G. What this means for the tests
1. Charts and critics pull opposite ways on simplicity. Chart papers reward fluency, typicality and repetition; poetry and creativity papers reward concrete images, fewer named emotions, sound devices, and surprise kept sensible. This is our two-channel finding (749.22) again, now in words.
2. Jason's target is lasting work across many acclaimed writers. So the outcomes are acclaim and covers (critics' lists, number of recorded versions), not chart peaks.
3. Compare within writer: an acclaimed writer's canonized songs against their own other songs. That removes style, era and fame, and stops the measures turning into "sound like Dylan".
4. Measures with the best prior support, in order: concreteness and imageability of nouns; named-emotion rate; verb share; second-person address; stock-phrase (cliché) rate; sound devices (internal rhyme, alliteration, assonance); within-song semantic distance in a sweet spot; narrative arc; repetition (chorus share); archaic-word rate (costs intelligibility).
5. Store features only, never lyric text.
