# The no-recording laboratory: Irish session tunes (19 Nov 2026). Study only; nothing built.
The Session's public dump: 23,205 tunes with a usable first setting; 11,774 in ten or more players' tunebooks, 15,763 on at least one commercial recording, 5,607 in neither.
Yardstick Gs = mean rank of tunebooks and recordings. Rules in PREDICTION.md, written first. Tune type, mode, length and submission order held (order alone runs -0.625 with Gs).

L1 A ridge reader over twenty plain page measures, fitted on half the tunes, judged on the other half: +0.123 (p 1e-78); halves +0.127, +0.121;
   tunebooks +0.122, recordings +0.113; tunes submitted after 2010 only +0.091; without submission order held +0.132. PASS.
   The first time in these studies that the written tune alone has predicted anything on tunes it had not seen.
   Parts, each alone, same sign in both halves: pentatonic share +0.116; bars repeated +0.057; compressible +0.058; time on the tonic +0.057; repeated notes +0.051;
   interval variety -0.060; pitch variety -0.051; leaps -0.043; ending on the tonic -0.055 (the played tunes more often end off the tonic and lead back round).
L2 Gs against the scaling exponent S (7,332 tunes of 128+ notes; S median 1.04): no inverted U. Squared term positive (z +2.1), Gs flat across fifths of S
   (0.522, 0.509, 0.502, 0.502, 0.504). FAIL. Even with the forgotten included, nearness to S = 1 does not grade a tune.

Reading: where there is no record and the whole range is present, the page carries a real, modest signal, and it is plainness: pentatonic, repetitive, tonic-anchored,
few leaps. That is what makes a tune easy to carry and pass on. Among Hot 100 hits these same measures graded nothing, which fits range restriction: every hit already has them.
Not shown: that this is "greatness" rather than "ease"; whether it transfers to hymn tunes or to song covers.
