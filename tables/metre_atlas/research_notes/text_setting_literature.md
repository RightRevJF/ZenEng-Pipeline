# Text-Setting: Aligning Lyric Syllables and Stress with Musical Metre (Across Metres and Languages)

## Q1. What do Palmer & Kelly (1992), Halle & Lerdahl (1993), Hayes & Kaun (1996), Hayes (2009), Dell & Halle (2009), Temperley & Temperley (2013), Kiparsky and later work report about stress-meter alignment, in numbers?

### Takeaway
English sets stress to strong beats strictly at every position in the line. Measured rates: primary stresses almost never fall on off-beats in pop lead sheets, compound nouns put the stronger beat on the first syllable about 90% of the time, and English stress-meter alignment (SMAV) is about 1.15 against 0.77 for French. French enforces the match strictly only at line ends: 97% of line-final syllables are stressable. Constraint models (Hayes 2009) reproduce consultants' preferred English settings 70.6% of the time, against 23.1% for Halle & Lerdahl's algorithm. Most corpora are small (10 to 80 songs, 9 to 12 singers), so treat the exact figures as approximate.

### Cited Findings

**Palmer & Kelly (1992), English: Gilbert & Sullivan and art songs**
- Compound nouns (e.g. BLACKbird) put the stronger metrical accent on the first syllable in 43/48 cases (about 90%) in themes from all 14 Gilbert & Sullivan operettas. Adjective-noun phrases put it on the second syllable in 79/95 cases (about 83%); χ²(1)=66.6, p<.01 — [Palmer & Kelly 1992, J. Memory & Language](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- In poetry set by nine composers, compound nouns had the stronger accent on syllable 1 in 96/105 cases (about 91%). Adjective-noun phrases had it on the second word in only 67/158 (about 42%), so phrasal stress is set less reliably than lexical/compound stress; χ²(1)=33.5 — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- Clitic phrases: in determiner-initial phrases the adjective took the metrical accent in 66/91 cases (about 73%); in clause-initial phrases the noun took it in 31/56 (about 55%) — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- Melodic contour and notated duration did not separate the syllables in these analyses; metre position did the work — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- Performance: with 10 vocalists ("To Jane"), musical accent (F(1,9)=23.5) and linguistic accent (F(1,9)=18.0) each lengthened sung syllables, additively. With 12 vocalists ("David"), musical accent also raised amplitude. SMALL SAMPLES (10 and 12 singers) — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)
- The paper contrasts binary metres (2/4, 4/4: strong-weak) with ternary metres (3/4, 6/8: strong-weak-weak) but gives no breakdown by metre — [Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)

**Nichols, Morris, Basu & Mitchell (2009, ISMIR), English popular music**
- Corpus: 679 lead sheets in MusicXML (229 private, 450 from Wikifonia) covering pop, rock, R&B, country, Latin and jazz. Only 4/4 measures were analysed; stress came from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Metric positions: downbeat > half-beat > quarter-beat > eighth-beat > other. Downbeat and half-beat positions strongly favour stressed syllables and rarely carry unstressed ones. Unstressed syllables are very likely on eighth-beat positions, and primary stresses rarely fall off the beat. Base rates: P(primary stress)=0.46, P(unstressed)=0.48; χ² p<0.0001 — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Duration: 55% of notes carrying primary stress are long (longer than the song's mean note), and 55% of notes on unstressed syllables are short — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Pitch: melodic peaks (a note higher than both neighbours) become more likely as stress increases. Monosyllabic stopwords (P=0.59 overall) favour weak positions and non-peaks — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Long vowels and diphthongs go on longer notes, short vowels on shorter notes — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Per-position probabilities appear only in figures and were not recoverable as numbers here — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)

**Hayes & Kaun (1996), English folk song chanted in binary rhythm**
- Corpus: 670 English folk-song lines (mostly Cecil Sharp). Ten native speakers chanted every line in binary rhythm, and people who started chanting in ternary were redirected. The data averaged 3.41 settings per line — [Hayes & Kaun 1996, The Linguistic Review](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf). The data file itself has 640 lines ([Hayes metrics page](https://brucehayes.org/Metrics.htm)). The two counts conflict.
- Grid: 16 positions per four-beat line on four levels (strong/medium/weak), grouped into dipods, hemistichs and lines. Three-beat lines leave the fourth strong beat empty — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Stress matching tightens at higher prosodic boundaries. At Intonational Phrase ends (4-stress), 1,102 cases matched and 12 mismatched (about 1.1%). At Phonological Phrase ends, 1,485 matched and 60 mismatched (about 3.9%); p<.0001 — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- "Beginnings free, endings strict": stress inside words is set more strictly than across word boundaries. The original settings contain only one line-initial lexical inversion, and consultants rejected it. Inversions are mostly line-final, and 12 of 17 are in unrhymed lines — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- 10.9% of settings (760/6,998) are "headless", leaving the initial positions empty (anacrusis or rest). No line fills more than 14 of its 16 positions — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Sung syllable duration is predicted by grid position and stress, r²=.532 (Bertheau, 100 lines) — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Inter-transcriber agreement: 99.6% on metrical position, 88.2% on stress values — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)

**Hayes (2009), "Textsetting as Constraint Conflict" (OT)**
- Four competing goals: (a) stress to strong positions; (b) avoid long lapses, i.e. stretches where no syllable begins; (c) avoid extreme syllable compression; (d) align phonological-phrase boundaries with line ends. A good setting often gives up (a) to satisfy (b) — [Hayes, Textsetting page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)
- Ranked constraints, highest first: *FILL EXTRA WEAK >> *RUN-ON >> *LAPSE (three empty grid positions in a row) >> *NULL IN S >> *STRESSLESS IN S >> *STRESSED IN W >> *STRESSLESS IN W >> STRONG IS LONG (for each S position, 3 minus the number of W positions before the next S) >> *STRESS IN M >> *NULL IN M. The ranking was found by greedy hill-climbing, and many rankings give the same outputs — [Hayes 2009 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Unpenalized "natural" cells: stressed syllable in S, stressless in M, empty W — [Hayes 2009 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Grid: per line, 4 S, 8 M and 16 W positions, plus an Extra-Weak row used only when more than three stressless syllables must be squeezed between stresses. That has about 13,000 candidate settings per line — [Hayes 2009 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Accuracy on 364 consensus four-beat lines (9 consultants): the OT grammar matched the favourite setting on 70.6%, plus 6.9% tied. Halle & Lerdahl's Syllabic Distribution Algorithm got 84/364 = 23.1% — [Hayes 2009 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf). The HTML page says 346 lines ([Textsetting page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)), which conflicts with the PDF's 364.
- Consultant settings almost never contain lapses; they appear only in lines too short to avoid one. Example: in "To court young maidens I was bent", the winning setting puts a stressed syllable in an M position to avoid a lapse — [Hayes 2009 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- The paper does not discuss ternary metre, 3/4 or 6/8 — [Hayes 2009 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)

**Halle & Lerdahl (1993)**
- "A generative textsetting model", *Current Musicology* 55:3–23. It formalizes stress-to-strong-beat preference — [Temperley 2022, Annual Review of Linguistics](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/temperley-arl22.pdf). Its algorithm reproduces only 23.1% of consultants' preferred settings (see Hayes above).

**Dell & Halle (2009), French vs English**
- English matches stress to musical beat anywhere in a line. French enforces the match rigidly only at line ends, perhaps because French stress is easy to hear only before major breaks — [Dell & Halle 2009, in Aroui & Arleo (eds.), *Towards a Typology of Poetic Forms*, Benjamins, pp. 63–78](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/lfab.2.03del/html)
- "Positional Parallelism": in French, stanzas sung to the same tune keep the same line count, syllables per line position, and melisma placement. Violations are rare in traditional French song and common in English — [Dell & Halle 2009](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/lfab.2.03del/html)

**Temperley & Temperley (2013), French vocal music (JASA 134(1))**
- Corpus: 54 songs from *Chants de France* (1922); 9,003 syllables; 1,193 lines; mostly 18th–19th century — [Temperley & Temperley 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- Coding: s = last full syllable of a polysyllable (the penult if a schwa follows); c = monosyllabic content word; f = monosyllabic function word; u = other syllables of polysyllables; x = syllabified final schwa — [T&T 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- Metrical levels: L1 = below tactus. L2 = tactus (quarter in 2/4, 3/4, 4/4; dotted quarter in 6/8, 9/8, 12/8). L3 = bar in 2/4, 3/4, 6/8, 9/8 and half-bar in 4/4, 12/8. L4 = bar in 4/4, 12/8 — [T&T 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- Share of each syllable category at each level (L1/L2/L3/L4): s = .16/.19/.46/.20; c = .28/.28/.33/.11; f = .40/.32/.23/.04; u = .58/.28/.11/.03; x = .28/.40/.31/.01 — [T&T 2013, Table I](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- Mean metrical strength: s 2.69, c 2.26, x 2.05, f 1.91, u 1.59. Stressable 2.57 vs unstressable 1.80, giving SMAV (the difference) = 0.77; t(53)=14.7 — [T&T 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- 97% of line-final syllables are stressable, with mean strength 3.14 vs 1.88 for other syllables. Without line-final syllables, French SMAV drops to 0.49 — [T&T 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- English comparison: SMAV 1.15 (stressable 3.08, unstressable 1.93), significantly higher than French (p<.01). For u-s words (unstressed then stressed), the second syllable was stronger in 94/94 English tokens vs a mean of 0.64 in French. SMALL SAMPLE: the English set is only the first 10 songs of *Songs of England* (1900) — [T&T 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- Across verses, a stressable syllable in verse 1 is matched by a stressable one in verse 2 only 54% of the time (331/613). SMAV: verse 1 = 0.51, verse 2 = 0.33. Tunes fit verse 1 best — [T&T 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- French secondary stress falls on the antepenult, not the initial syllable: mean strength 2.40 vs 1.53 over 45 words — [T&T 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- Results are not broken down by duple vs triple metre — [T&T 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)

**Later work**
- Ryan (2022), 2,371 English popular songs: syllables map to the grid according to natural duration even after controlling for final lengthening. The domain of weight is the interval between p-centres, not the syllable or rime. Both categorical weight and duration matter. No numbers in the abstract — [Ryan 2022, English Language & Linguistics 26(3)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-language-and-linguistics/article/abs/syllable-weight-and-natural-duration-in-textsetting-popular-music-in-english/50F8885341914C37D6F1BD22A632A0B6)
- Temperley & Temperley (2011), the "Scotch snap" (short note on the strong beat, long note off it): common in English and Scottish melodies, rare in German and Italian ones. They link it to the many very short stressed syllables in English. Temperley calls this "suggestive but inconclusive" because each nation counts as one data point — [Temperley 2022 ARL review](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/temperley-arl22.pdf)
- Only metrical accents need to align with stress. Phenomenal accents such as duration and loudness may conflict with metre, and that conflict is syncopation — [Temperley 2022](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/temperley-arl22.pdf)
- Kiparsky's "A modular metrics for folk verse" argues that stress and metre are governed by separate constraint systems that must be satisfied jointly. It builds on Hayes & MacEachern's stochastic-OT analysis of English folk quatrains — [PhilArchive record](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/KIPAMM). Hayes (2009) says Kiparsky's word- and phrase-boundary effects also show up in the Hayes & Kaun data — [Hayes 2009 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- Tone languages, for comparison: tone-tune match is 92% parallel in Cantonese, 53% in Shona, and 97% of a Mandarin folk corpus follows head-syllable rules — [McPherson, "Musical adaptation as phonological evidence"](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10166941)

### Inferences
- Encodable English rule set, in priority order: (1) never leave long silent gaps inside a line (*LAPSE); (2) keep stressless syllables off S and stressed syllables off W; (3) hold to (2) most strictly inside polysyllabic words and at phrase and line ends; (4) the line opening may be loose (headless or pickup settings in about 11% of settings); (5) at most three stressless syllables between stresses in a 4-subdivision beat.
- Phrasal (adjective-noun) stress is a soft target in English settings (about 42–83% compliance). Compound and lexical stress is near-hard (about 90%+).
- For French, use a different profile: a hard rule that the line-final full (non-schwa) syllable lands on the strongest available beat, and only a weak preference mid-line (SMAV about 0.5).
- T&T's level scheme gives a ready way to compute beat strength in any common time signature: in 6/8 the dotted quarter is the tactus and the bar is level 3.

### Gaps
- Exact per-position probability tables from Nichols et al. (figures only) and Ryan's (2022) coefficients (behind a paywall) were not available.
- Halle & Lerdahl's algorithm and rules could not be read directly. Only secondary descriptions and Hayes's accuracy comparison are reported here.
- Kiparsky's specific constraints for sung folk verse (e.g. bounding) were not recovered from the full text.
- No quantitative German stress-meter study was found; the Leiden German/Dutch project (Proto) reported no results on its page ([Leiden project](https://www.universiteitleiden.nl/en/research/research-projects/humanities/poetry-rhythm-and-meter-textsetting)).

## Q2. How do lyric metres (iambic, trochaic, anapestic, dactylic; hymn metres CM, LM, SM, 87.87) map to musical time signatures in practice?

### Takeaway
Hymn-metre labels count syllables per line and imply a foot type; they do not fix a time signature. A text can go to any tune with the same syllable counts and the same foot (iambic CM to iambic CM, trochaic 87.87 to trochaic 87.87). No quantitative study of hymn metre against time signature was found.

### Cited Findings
- CM (Common Metre) = 8.6.8.6, alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter. LM = 8.8.8.8, iambic tetrameter. SM = 6.6.8.6 (iambic trimeter, with tetrameter in line 3). 87.87 is mostly trochaic. 8.7.8.7.D may be trochaic or iambic. Doubled forms (DCM, DLM) join two stanzas — [Wikipedia, Metre (hymn)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_(hymn))
- A hymn may be sung to any tune in the same metre provided the poetic foot also matches. Dactylic hymn metres exist, e.g. "Great Is Thy Faithfulness" — [Wikipedia, Metre (hymn)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_(hymn))
- English folk lines are typically four-beat. Three-beat lines (e.g. the 6- and 8-syllable lines of CM/SM-type quatrains) are four-beat frames with the final strong beat left empty, i.e. a rest or held note — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Bulgarian folk songs alternate long lines with lines 1–3 syllables shorter (8~5, 8~6, 10~8, ...). Hayes & Schuh liken this to the 4–3 pattern of English folk song — [Hayes & Schuh, Ling 251 Handout 12](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12_bulgarian_metrics.pdf)

### Inferences
- Engine mapping, derived from the grid models above and not measured: iambic lines = anacrusis plus downbeat stress, so a pickup note. Trochaic lines start on the downbeat. CM's "8.6" = a 4-beat line, then a 3-beat line plus a rest or held final beat, giving 2 + 2 bars in 4/4 or 3/4. The time signature (duple or triple) is free for iambic and trochaic metres, since each foot fills one beat either way: one note per beat or a long-short split.
- Anapestic and dactylic texts carry two unstressed syllables per stress. They fit compound metres (6/8, 9/8, 12/8) or 3/4 at one syllable per eighth or quarter without compression, matching Palmer & Kelly's "ternary = strong-weak-weak" description ([Palmer & Kelly 1992](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Palmer1992.pdf)).

### Gaps
- No corpus study was found that counts how often CM, LM, SM or 87.87 tunes are in 3/4, 4/4 or 6/8 (e.g. from hymnary.org metadata). That count could be built from Hymnary's tune index, which was not fetched.

## Q3. How is text set in triple and compound metres in English folk and popular song?

### Takeaway
Little published quantitative work separates triple from duple text-setting in English. The main corpora (Nichols et al.; Tan, Lustig & Temperley) restrict themselves to 4/4 or 2/4, and Hayes & Kaun forced binary chanting. Treat triple-metre rules as extrapolations from the grid models. The one strong pop-specific finding is anticipatory syncopation: about a third of stressed syllables in rock fall just before the beat.

### Cited Findings
- Hayes & Kaun's consultants were made to chant binary. Volunteers who started ternary settings were corrected by example, which shows ternary chanting comes naturally to some English speakers but leaves it out of the data — [Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)
- Hayes's STRONG IS LONG constraint prefers up to 3 W positions after each S, i.e. a long note or rest after a stressed beat. *FILL EXTRA WEAK forbids more than 3 stressless syllables between stresses within the binary grid — [Hayes 2009](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)
- T&T 2013 treat 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8 with the dotted quarter as tactus (level 2) but report no separate results for compound metre — [T&T 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- Rock (Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019, Music Perception 36(4)): 80 songs from the Rolling Stone 500 corpus, 1955–1997; 23,129 notes; only 4/4 and 2/4 (17 songs in other metres were excluded) — [Tan, Lustig & Temperley 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
  - 2,382 of 10,433 stressed syllables are syncopated at the 8th level (SQ=.228), and another .105 at the 16th level, for a combined SQ of .333. The 19th-century English song corpus has SQ = 0 (0/602) — [same](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
  - Syncopation is anticipatory: 77% of 8th-level syncopations fall on the 8th before beats 3 and 1 (ASQ=.772), and 74% at the 16th level. By shift: 5→4 (stress anticipating beat 3) has tendency .554, 1→8 .504, 3→2 .439, 7→6 .351. SMALL CORPUS (80 songs) — [same](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
  - 8th-level syncopation increases with tempo and year. Among monosyllabic function words, "in" falls on quarter-note beats 56% of the time and "it" 12% — [same](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Stressed syllables in rock occur on the weak beat just before a strong one only in "restricted and quite systematic ways" — [Temperley 2022](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/temperley-arl22.pdf)
- Nichols et al. analysed only 4/4 measures — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)

### Inferences
- For 6/8 and 12/8 in an engine: treat each dotted-quarter beat as an S plus two W slots. Anapests and dactyls then set one syllable per eighth. Iambic and trochaic lines in 6/8 typically set the stressed syllable as a quarter and the unstressed as an eighth (long-short), satisfying STRONG IS LONG. This is an extrapolation, not a measured rate.
- In 3/4, two-syllable feet either fill beats 1–2 and hold or rest on beat 3, or set the stress as a half note plus the unstressed syllable on beat 3, acting as a pickup to the next bar. Three-syllable feet fill the bar one syllable per beat. This is an extrapolation.
- Pop-specific rule: allow a stressed syllable to anticipate beat 1 or 3 by an 8th (or 16th), at roughly up to a third of stresses in rock style and essentially never in 19th-century and hymn style. Never shift a stress to just after the beat as a default; anticipation dominates.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study of English triple or compound metre text-setting was found. The Nichols et al. (2009) syllable-count figures for 3/4 and 6/8 do not exist because 4/4 was filtered. "Hough's structural models" could not be identified or located.
- Nothing measured was found on how often English 6/8 songs set two vs three syllables per beat.

## Q4. How is text set in additive metres (Balkan, Greek, Turkish) and in Finnish Kalevala metre?

### Takeaway
Additive-metre traditions often decouple text metre from musical metre. Bulgarian folk verse counts syllables, ignores stress, and the same text can be sung in several aksak metres. Ottoman art song links usul to classes of aruz quantity metres. Finnic runosong is a quantity-sensitive trochaic tetrameter most often sung in 5/4 or 4/4. The governing rule there is about syllable length on beats, not stress on beats.

### Cited Findings

**Bulgarian (aksak)**
- Folk verse is syllable-counting. Of 89 coded songs, 50 have 8-syllable lines, 17 have 10, 5 have 12 and 2 have 9. Fixed caesura splits: 5+3 for 8-syllable lines (34/50), 5+5 for 10 (13/17), 6+6 for 12 (4/5). Caesurae never fall inside a word or clitic phrase. SMALL SAMPLE (89 songs) — [Hayes & Schuh, Handout 12](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12_bulgarian_metrics.pdf)
- "Stress plays essentially no role in folk song metrics", even though Bulgarian stress is lexically contrastive. Rhyme is not systematic either — [same](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12_bulgarian_metrics.pdf)
- Text metre and musical metre are not correlated: the same text may have traditional settings in several musical metres — [same](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12_bulgarian_metrics.pdf)
- Settings in the corpus: pravo 2/4 (2 bars per line); lesno 7/8, grouped 3+2+2 in the handout's notation (the most common aksak setting, 17 songs); ruchenitsa 7/8 (2+2+3); elenino 7/8 (2+2+1+2 per the handout); pajdushko 5/8 (2+3); dajchovo 9/8 (2+2+2+3); kopanitsa 11/8 (2+2+3+2+2). A lesno song takes 2 bars per line; a dajchovo example takes 3 bars per line including a refrain — [same](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12_bulgarian_metrics.pdf)
- Internal refrains (fixed two-syllable words like "mome", "male") are ignored in verse syllable counts but are sung — [same](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12_bulgarian_metrics.pdf)
- Hristov (1913/1925) claimed that every number from 4 to 14 serves equally as the basis for n-syllable verse, n-pulse metre and n-bar phrasing. He grouped pulses into short (2-unit) and elongated (3-unit) beats — [Goldberg, MTO 31.2 (2025)](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.25.31.2/mto.25.31.2.goldberg.html)
- Source corpus: the Miladinov brothers' collection, 665 songs and 23,559 verses — [Hayes & Schuh, Handout 12](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12_bulgarian_metrics.pdf)

**Turkish / Ottoman (aruz and usul)**
- Aruz is the quantitative prosody of Arabic, Persian and classical Ottoman poetry. Short syllable = cv or v. Long syllable = cV, cVc, cvc, cVcc, cvcc, vc or V. Each bahir (metre family) has sub-metres (vezin), e.g. u u – – | u u – – | u – u –. Licensed modifications: ulama (merging), zihâf (shortening), imâle (lengthening), med (insertion) — [Corpus Musicae Ottomanicae wiki](https://projects.academiccloud.de/projects/corpus-musicae-ottomanicae-cmo-public-wiki/wiki/a-prosodic-system-and-its-elements-aruz)
- Bektaş (2005) analysed 466 beste and found that each usul relates to aruz metre. The concordance is stronger between usul and bahir classes than individual metres, so he frames it as "bahir–usul" rather than "aruz–usul" — [Bektaş 2005, Asian Music 36(1)](https://repository.bilkent.edu.tr/items/744814c8-160c-4047-a98f-16a605d31675/full)

**Finnish / Finnic Kalevala metre (runosong)**
- The line is octosyllabic and trochaic, with four feet. Rule A: "Stressed syllables must be long on the downbeat and short on the upbeat." Unstressed syllables may go anywhere, and monosyllables count as stressed. Stress is word-initial — [Kiparsky 1968](https://www.brucehayes.org/251metrics/papers/Kiparsky1968Kalevala.pdf)
- Strictness rises through the line. Foot 1 is free; foot 2 often breaks Rule A; foot 3 is tighter; foot 4 has no violations in Sadeniemi's sample — [Kiparsky 1968](https://www.brucehayes.org/251metrics/papers/Kiparsky1968Kalevala.pdf)
- Further tendencies: words are arranged in increasing length; no line-final monosyllable (exceptionless); a frequent break between syllables 4 and 5; about two-thirds of lines begin with a long syllable; alliteration is common — [Kiparsky 1968](https://www.brucehayes.org/251metrics/papers/Kiparsky1968Kalevala.pdf)
- Sung practice: "The most common times were 5/4 and 4/4." Some northern group wedding songs use 9/8. The singer chose the basic beat. Melodies span about a pentachord and cover one or two lines. A long stressed syllable must land on a stressed beat and a short one on an unstressed beat — [Laitinen 1985/2017, Finnish Music Quarterly](https://fmq.fi/articles/rune-singing-the-musical-vernacular)
- Archangel Karelian solo singing mostly used a "five-beat, two-line melody"; four-beat melodies were used mainly in children's songs. Lines are typically 8–10 syllables, occasionally 5–12. The long-stressed-on-strong rule is strictest at line end. The corpus is 244 Ingrian and 272 Archangel Karelian recordings, analysed qualitatively — [Kallio 2017, Oral Tradition 31/2](https://journal.oraltradition.org/wp-content/uploads/files/articles/31ii/07_31.2.pdf)

### Inferences
- Engine rule for Kalevala-style 5/4: map 8 syllables to 5 beats by lengthening the final syllable(s), e.g. the final trochee as two quarters and the first six syllables as eighths. Place long stressed syllables on strong positions, especially in feet 3 and 4, and allow a free first foot. This is a plausible reconstruction consistent with "five-beat" melodies and Rule A; the exact rhythmic template was not documented in the sources read.
- Engine rule for Bulgarian aksak: count syllables to the line (8 = 5+3) and map lines to whole bars (2 bars of 7/8, or 3 bars of 9/8 with refrain), with the caesura at a bar or beat boundary. Do not enforce lexical-stress-on-long-beat; the source says stress is not metrical in folk verse.
- Ottoman: map long aruz syllables to longer notes or stronger usul strokes within a bahir-appropriate usul. The specific düm/tek mapping is unverified.

### Gaps
- No source was found on Greek text-setting in additive metres (e.g. 7/8 kalamatianos, 9/8 zeibekiko), or on Greek stress vs beat in such metres.
- How syllables map onto 3-unit vs 2-unit beats in Bulgarian song was not documented quantitatively in the sources reached.
- Bektaş's numeric concordance rates and syllable-to-stroke rules are in the full PDF, which was not read. The CMO wiki page does not describe syllable-to-note duration mapping.
- Mari Sarv's Estonian regilaul quantity-rule studies ([folklore.ee](https://www.folklore.ee/folklore/vol7/maripar.htm)) were found but not read.

## Q5. Which datasets with syllable-aligned lyrics exist for text-setting research, and can they be downloaded?

### Takeaway
Hayes's English folk-song data (640 lines) and the Hayes 2009 consensus-setting file are free plain-text downloads. For pop, DALI (5,358 songs, note-level alignment) and the Yu et al. LMD-derived set (about 265k syllable-note pairs) are on GitHub. The MusicXML lead-sheet set used by Nichols et al. was partly private.

### Cited Findings
- Hayes & Kaun (1996): `FolkLine.txt`, 640 lines of English folk song annotated with 16-position grid setting, stress level, phonological phrasing and stressed-syllable weight. ASCII; view in a Courier font. https://brucehayes.org/papers/FolkLine.txt. Sound examples: https://brucehayes.org/papers/hksound.zip — [Hayes metrics page](https://brucehayes.org/Metrics.htm)
- Hayes (2009): tab-delimited data with stress transcriptions and the consultants' most common setting. https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/DataForHayesTextsettingAsCC.txt. WAV examples are under https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/Sound/ — [Hayes Textsetting page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting)
- Hayes & MacEachern (1998), quatrain form: appendices PDF; constraint violations for 625 candidates (`violatio.txt`); an annotated Appalachian folk-song database (`AppalachianFolkSongsAnnotated.xls`); tableaux at https://brucehayes.org/HayesMacEachern/quattabl.htm. The grid-playing DOS program is lost — [Hayes metrics page](https://brucehayes.org/Metrics.htm)
- DALI v1.0: 5,358 audio tracks with lyrics time-aligned at the note, word, line and paragraph levels, plus aligned vocal-melody notes. https://github.com/gabolsgabs/DALI. The paper states no dataset licence; check the repository — [Meseguer-Brocal et al., arXiv 1906.10606](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.10606)
- Yu, Srivastava & Canales lyrics-melody set: 12,197 MIDI files (7,998 LMD-full with lyrics). They extracted 13,251 20-note sequences = 265,020 one-to-one syllable-note pairs as {MIDI pitch, duration, rest}. https://github.com/yy1lab/Lyrics-Conditioned-Neural-Melody-Generation — [Yu et al., arXiv 1908.05551](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.05551)
- Nichols et al. (2009): 679 MusicXML lead sheets (229 private, 450 from Wikifonia) with syllables attached to notes, so no alignment step was needed. Not packaged as a public release in the paper — [Nichols et al. 2009](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2009/paper/000084.pdf)
- Temperley & Temperley (2013) source: *Chants de France* (Jameson & Heacox 1922), 54 songs, plus *Songs of England* (Hannon & Fanning 1900). The coded data are described but no download is given — [T&T 2013](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/temperley-temperley-jasa.pdf)
- Tan, Lustig & Temperley (2019) used Rolling Stone corpus melodic transcriptions with stress coding (80 songs) — [Tan et al. 2019](https://www.jurispro.com/files/articles/anticipatorysyncopationinrockacorpusstudymusicperception364353370_8745.pdf)
- Ryan (2022): 2,371 English popular songs; data availability was not visible in the abstract — [Ryan 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-language-and-linguistics/article/abs/syllable-weight-and-natural-duration-in-textsetting-popular-music-in-english/50F8885341914C37D6F1BD22A632A0B6)
- Bulgarian: the Miladinov collection (665 songs, 23,559 verses, texts only) is on liternet.bg per Hayes & Schuh — [Handout 12](https://www.brucehayes.org/251HayesSchuhMetrics/handouts/12_bulgarian_metrics.pdf)
- Turkish: Corpus Musicae Ottomanicae encodes aruz scansion in TEI (metDecl, metSym, l, lg) — [CMO wiki](https://projects.academiccloud.de/projects/corpus-musicae-ottomanicae-cmo-public-wiki/wiki/a-prosodic-system-and-its-elements-aruz)

### Inferences
- For building ZenEng lookup tables, the quickest high-quality source is Hayes's `FolkLine.txt` and `DataForHayesTextsettingAsCC.txt`: stress plus grid position, with consultant consensus. They let you fit or verify the Hayes constraint ranking directly. For pop metre statistics across time signatures, DALI and the LMD-lyrics MIDI include non-4/4 songs and could be mined to fill the triple/compound gap (Q3). That would need a stress dictionary such as CMUdict, as Nichols et al. did.

### Gaps
- Whether the Rolling Stone corpus melody and lyric transcriptions with stress coding are publicly downloadable was not verified in this session.
- No public syllable-aligned corpora were confirmed for French (T&T coding), Finnish runosong (the SKVR/Finnish Literature Society archives were not checked), Greek or Bulgarian sung melodies.
- The DALI language distribution and licence terms were not confirmed.
